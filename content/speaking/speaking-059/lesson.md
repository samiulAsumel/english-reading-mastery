## Introduction

[Lesson 59](/lessons/59/) taught you that an example illustrates a claim
without automatically proving every stronger version of it. Giving an
example out loud, live, tests something writing doesn't: whether you can
resist the easy, sweeping version of the claim in the moment, and instead
say clearly what the one case shows — and what it still leaves open.

## Shadow the Model

::: shadow rate="0.9"
For example, one of our clients cut onboarding time in half after we
introduced a checklist tool. That's a strong result, but it was a small
team, so I can't say every client would see the same jump.
:::

Shadow this once. Notice how "but" carries a clear pause and stress before
it — that's the exact spot where the speaker pulls back from overclaiming.

## Your Turn

::: prompt time="45s"
Give an example that supports a broader claim you believe. Introduce it with
a signal phrase, state the specific claim it genuinely supports, and end by
naming one limitation — a small sample, an unusual starting condition, or a
confound — that stops it from proving the fuller claim on its own.
:::

::: timer seconds="45" label="Speak now — introduce the example, state what it shows, then name its limitation"
:::

## Record Yourself

::: record label="Record your example, then check whether you named a real limitation instead of just moving on."
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
There's no single correct example to pick — what matters is naming the
limitation honestly. A strong answer sounds like: "For example, one
warehouse we worked with cut picking errors by 40% after switching to
barcode scanning. That's a genuinely strong result. But it was one
warehouse, already running fairly modern equipment, so I wouldn't say every
warehouse would see the same 40% drop — older, more manual operations might
face a very different starting point." Signal phrase, specific claim,
honest limitation — all delivered live.
:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="anecdotal" pos="adjective" register="formal" meaning="based on one story or case, and not necessarily representative of the wider group (উপাখ্যানমূলক)" collocation="anecdotal evidence, merely anecdotal, anecdotal account" example="The story about one late driver is anecdotal, not proof of a trend."
:::

::: vocabulary word="hold up" pos="phrasal verb" register="neutral" meaning="to remain true or valid when tested more broadly (প্রযোজ্য থাকা)" collocation="hold up under scrutiny, hold up well, may not hold up" example="I doubt the finding will hold up on a larger sample."
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Did you introduce the example with a clear spoken signal phrase?
- Did you state the specific claim the example genuinely supports?
- Did you name a real limitation, live, instead of skipping past it?
- Did you speak immediately, without a long pause to plan first?
:::
