## Introduction

[Lesson 8](/lessons/8/) taught you that *but* and *although* both signal
a contrast, just in different sentence shapes. Saying a real contrast
out loud means holding two opposing facts in your head at once and
connecting them without a pause in the middle — exactly the pressure
this drill applies.

## Shadow the Model

::: shadow rate="0.9"
The task was hard, but we finished it. Although the task was hard, we
finished it.
:::

Both sentences say exactly the same thing. Shadow both shapes, and
notice *but* sits in the middle of one breath while *although* opens the
sentence and the contrast lands at the end.

## Your Turn

::: prompt time="7s"
Think of something true and slightly surprising about your day — a hard
task you still finished, a delay that didn't matter, bad weather that
didn't stop you. When the timer starts, say it as one *but* sentence.
:::

::: timer seconds="7" label="Say the contrast now — both halves, one breath"
:::

## Record Yourself

::: record label="Say three contrast sentences with but, then say the same three again starting with although."
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
There's no single correct sentence — "The delay was long, but we still
finished on time" or "Although the delay was long, we still finished on
time" both work. What matters is that both halves of the contrast came
out in one connected breath, not as two disconnected facts.
:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="opposing" pos="adjective" register="formal" meaning="pulling in a different or contrary direction (বিপরীতমুখী)" collocation="opposing view, opposing force, opposing argument" example="The two reports reach opposing conclusions about the new tariff."
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Did both halves of your contrast come out in one connected sentence, not two separate statements?
- Did you say the same contrast both ways — with but, and with although?
- Did the contrast you chose feel genuinely surprising, not forced?
:::
