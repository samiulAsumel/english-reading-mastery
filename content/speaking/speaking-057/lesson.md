## Introduction

[Lesson 57](/lessons/57/) taught you to find the single criterion behind
any classification and catch it when two rules get mixed together.
Classifying something out loud, live, tests whether you can actually hold
one criterion in your head for the whole answer — it's easy to drift onto a
second, unrelated rule halfway through when you're speaking without notes.

## Shadow the Model

::: shadow rate="0.9"
Delivery methods can be classified by speed. Standard delivery arrives
within five days. Express delivery arrives within two days. Same-day
delivery, as the name suggests, arrives the same day it's ordered.
:::

Shadow this once. Notice how "by speed" gets a clear stress right after
"classified" — naming the criterion early is what keeps the rest of the
sentence from wandering onto price or distance instead.

## Your Turn

::: prompt time="50s"
Pick a concept you know well and classify it out loud. State a quick
definition, name your one classification criterion clearly, then walk
through two or three categories that follow that same criterion, giving one
example for each.
:::

::: timer seconds="50" label="Speak now — definition, one criterion, categories with examples"
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
There's no single correct concept to pick — what matters is holding one
criterion steady. A strong answer sounds like: "Job interviews can be
classified by format. A phone interview happens entirely over a call,
usually as a first screening step. A panel interview puts several
interviewers in the room at once, asking questions together. A technical
interview focuses specifically on a live problem or task related to the
job." Definition, one clear criterion — format — and three categories that
never drift onto a different rule like interview length or seniority.
:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="broadly" pos="adverb" register="formal" meaning="signals that a classification is high-level, and finer subcategories likely exist underneath it (বিস্তৃতভাবে)" collocation="broadly speaking, broadly consistent, broadly similar" example="Broadly, incidents fall into two groups: equipment faults and human error."
:::

::: vocabulary word="consists of" pos="phrase" register="formal" meaning="presents a relatively complete, closed description of what something is made up of (গঠিত হওয়া)" collocation="consists of, the team consists of, consists of three parts" example="The terminal consists of four berths and two storage yards."
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Did you state a clear definition before starting to classify?
- Did you name your one classification criterion explicitly, out loud?
- Did every category you gave actually follow that same criterion?
- Did you give a concrete example for each category without a long pause?
:::
