## Introduction

[Lesson 85](/lessons/85/) warned about silent strengthening — quietly
restating a cautious "suggests" as a confident "proves." Speaking
spontaneously makes this worse, not better: under time pressure, people
often drop hedge words because a flat, confident sentence is easier to
produce quickly than a carefully qualified one. This drill trains you to
resist that shortcut.

## Shadow the Model

::: shadow rate="0.9"
The pilot suggests that scanners may reduce errors, though it's only
one site over six months, so we can't say this proves anything yet.
:::

Notice "suggests" and "may" are said with a slightly softer, lower
stress than the confident words around them — a fluent speaker's voice
actually signals the hedge, not just the wording.

## Your Turn

::: prompt time="25s"
A single six-month pilot at one warehouse found barcode scanners
reduced picking errors by 15%. No other site has tried it. When the
timer starts, say a correctly calibrated claim about what this evidence
actually supports — out loud, with an honest hedge word.
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
There's no single correct wording, but a strong answer sounds like:
"This pilot suggests barcode scanners may reduce picking errors, but
it's only one site over six months, so we shouldn't treat this as
proven yet." What matters is that a real hedge word (suggests, may)
survived under time pressure, instead of collapsing into a flat, unhedged
claim.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Did your spoken claim keep an honest hedge word rather than dropping it?
- Did the hedge sound natural, not evasive or vague?
- Did you speak immediately, without a long pause to plan the sentence?
:::
