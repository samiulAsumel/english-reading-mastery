## Introduction

Lesson 54 turned process descriptions into flow diagrams. Today's
lesson tackles something that looks simpler but is genuinely just as
precise: numbers, percentages, tables, and charts. A number is easy to
read wrong — not because the arithmetic is hard, but because the
English wrapped around it (*by* vs. *to*, *most* vs. *all*, *up to* vs.
*typically*) carries exact, non-interchangeable meaning.

## A Number Alone Has No Meaning

::: warning title="Always ask: X% of what, and compared with what?"
*The company reduced processing time by 30%.* The number alone tells
you nothing until you ask: 30% *of what* (processing time is the
measured variable), and what kind of change (*reduced by 30%* is the
change itself, not the final value).
:::

## "By" vs. "To" — One of the Most Consequential Distinctions Here

::: important title="A completely different final number, from one preposition"
**Reduced by 30%:** original 100 minutes → after 70 minutes (a 30%
decrease). **Reduced to 30%:** original 100 minutes → after 30 minutes
(a 70% decrease). *Error rates fell by 20%* means the rate dropped 20%
relative to its old value; *error rates fell to 20%* means the final
rate is 20%, full stop. The same logic runs in reverse for increases:
*revenue increased by 15%* (new = old × 1.15) is entirely different
from *revenue increased to 15%* (final value = 15, whatever the unit
is).
:::

## Percentage Point vs. Percent

::: warning title="60% to 70% is not a '10% increase'"
If a success rate rises from 60% to 70%, the **absolute** change is 10
**percentage points**. The **relative** change is 10/60 ≈ 16.7%.
Calling this "a 10% increase" is technically misleading — the
precise phrasing is *increased by 10 percentage points*, or
*increased by approximately 16.7% relative to the original level.*
The same logic applies to a drop from 10% to 8% error rate: absolute
change = −2 percentage points; relative change = −20% (2 ÷ 10).
Always read *percent* and *percentage points* as two distinct units,
never interchangeably.
:::

## "Twice as Much" and Multiplicative Comparisons

::: concept title="Precise multiplication language, worth reading exactly"
*X is twice as large as Y* means X = 2Y. *A produces three times as
much output as B* means A = 3B — if B = 100, A = 300, which is a 200%
**increase**, even though the final amount is 300% **of** B. "Two times
more" is genuinely ambiguous in casual English; professional writing
prefers *twice as large as* or *twice the amount of* precisely because
it avoids that ambiguity.
:::

## More Than, Less Than, At Least, At Most, Nearly, Almost

::: framework title="Six precision words for approximate or bounded quantities"
- More than 500: excludes 500 itself
- Less than 500: excludes 500 itself
- At least 500: 500 or more
- At most 500: 500 or fewer
- Approximately 500: near 500, not exact
- Nearly / almost 80%: a bit under 80%, not exactly 80%
:::

## Most vs. All, and Vague Majority Language

::: warning title="A majority could be 51% or 95% — don't assume a number that isn't given"
*Most employees supported the policy* means over 50%, not 100% — never
silently upgrade *most* to *all* in a summary. *A majority of
respondents supported the proposal* gives no exact figure — it could
be 51%, 70%, or 95%; without a number, don't invent one. *A small
minority opposed the change* is qualitative quantification — resist
assigning it a specific percentage like "5%" unless the text states
one.
:::

## The Quantifier Ladder, and Few vs. A Few

::: framework title="A rough conceptual scale, not exact mathematical categories"
- none
- few
- some
- many
- most
- almost all
- all
:::

::: warning title="Few carries a negative tone that a few does not"
*Few employees attended* implies disappointingly low turnout. *A few
employees attended* is comparatively neutral — some employees did show
up. The same split applies to uncountable nouns: *there is little
evidence that the policy worked* (evidence is scarce) versus *there is
a little evidence that the policy worked* (some evidence exists) — one
word article changes the claim's strength substantially.
:::

## "No Evidence" and Its Precise Meaning, Recapped

::: important title="Lesson 30's principle, worth repeating at data scale"
*There is no evidence that X caused Y* does **not** mean *X definitely
did not cause Y* — only that available evidence doesn't establish
causation. Separately, **"no evidence of an effect"** (no evidence was
found supporting an effect) is not the same as **"evidence of no
effect"** (evidence was found specifically showing there is no
effect) — a distinction that matters enormously in scientific and
policy reading.
:::

## Average, Mean, Median, and Mode

::: concept title="Three different 'centers,' each telling a different story"
*The average processing time was 12 minutes* is a combined central
measure — it does **not** describe every individual case (some took 5
minutes, some took 20). **Mean** = sum of values ÷ number of
observations. **Median** = the middle value once sorted. **Mode** = the
most frequently occurring value. For 2, 3, 3, 4, 20: mean = 6.4, median
= 3, mode = 3 — three quite different numbers from the same five-item
dataset.
:::

::: warning title="Median resists distortion by extreme values — that's often why it's chosen"
Income data like 20k, 22k, 25k, 30k, 500k produces a mean dragged far
upward by one outlier, while the median stays representative of the
typical case. Seeing *median income* in a report is a signal the
author wanted a figure less distorted by extremes.
:::

## Range, Between, From…To, and By

::: framework title="Four ways English states a quantitative span or change"
- Range: "processing times ranged from 5 to 40 minutes" states minimum and maximum only, not an average
- Between X and Y: "the rate remained between 5% and 8%" — check whether endpoints are included
- From X to Y: "revenue increased from $2 million to $3 million" gives both initial and final values, so absolute ($1m) and relative (50%) change can both be computed
- By X: "revenue increased by $1 million" gives only the change, not necessarily the starting or ending value
:::

## Trend Vocabulary

::: framework title="Four families of verbs describing data movement"
- Increase: increase, rise, grow, climb, expand, improve
- Decrease: decrease, fall, decline, drop, shrink, contract
- Stability: remain stable, remain unchanged, level off, stabilize
- Fluctuation: fluctuate, vary, oscillate
:::

## Degree of Change, and "Significant" — a Genuine Trap

::: warning title="Everyday 'significant' and statistical 'significant' are not the same claim"
*Slightly, moderately, significantly, substantially, dramatically*
increase are not exact mathematical categories, but they do signal
relative magnitude. *Sales increased significantly* uses *significant*
in its everyday sense — noticeably large. But *statistically
significant* is a technical term entirely: significant ≠ automatically
statistically significant, and the reverse fails too.
:::

## Trend vs. One-Time Change

::: important title="Tense carries real information here"
*Sales increased in 2025* describes one observed change. *Sales have
been increasing steadily since 2022* describes an ongoing trend — the
tense difference is not stylistic, it's substantive. **Steadily**
signals relatively consistent movement; **rapidly** signals speed or
magnitude — the two are not synonyms. **Fluctuated** ("prices
fluctuated throughout the year") means up and down movement, distinct
from a plain *increased.* **Remained stable** ("demand remained stable
despite seasonal changes") means no major change occurred — crucially,
*stable ≠ low.*
:::

## Reading a Table

::: example title="Numbers alone in a table don't establish causation"
| Year | Processing Time | Error Rate |
|---|---|---|
| 2023 | 20 min | 5% |
| 2024 | 16 min | 4% |
| 2025 | 12 min | 3% |

Both columns decreased together. That observation alone does not
license "decreasing processing time caused the decreasing error rate"
— correlation and causation remain separate questions, exactly as in
Lesson 32.
:::

## Reading a Chart

::: framework title="Six questions to ask before drawing any conclusion from a chart"
- What is the chart measuring?
- What is the X-axis?
- What is the Y-axis?
- What unit is used?
- What time period does it cover?
- What population or sample does it represent?
:::

::: warning title="Two ways a chart can mislead without a single false number"
A **dual-axis chart** overlaying, say, cargo volume and fuel cost can
make two unrelated-scale variables look like they move together
visually, even when their actual units and scales are entirely
different. A Y-axis truncated to 90-100 makes a small change look
huge; a Y-axis spanning 0-1000 makes a large change look tiny. Never
interpret visual magnitude on a chart without first checking the axis
range.
:::

## Correlation, Confounding, and Sample Size

::: warning title="Three checks before accepting a data-based causal claim"
If automation adoption rises and processing time falls together,
that's an **association** — a causal claim (*automation caused the
reduction*) needs more. If staff training *also* rose at the same time,
that's a **confounding variable**: is the improvement due to
automation, training, both, or something else entirely? And *8
managers reported improved efficiency* rests on a **sample size** of
n=8 — likely too small to support a broad claim like "managers
generally believe…"
:::

## Percentage Without a Denominator

::: important title="80% of what, exactly?"
*80% of users experienced improvement* means something very different
if it's 8 out of 10 versus 8,000 out of 10,000 — the percentage's
practical weight depends entirely on its denominator, which is part of
the claim's context, not an afterthought.
:::

## Rate, Per, Per Capita, Ratio, and Proportion

::: framework title="Five normalized-quantity words, each with a distinct structure"
- Rate: a standardized frequency or ratio of an event/quantity ("error rate," "adoption rate")
- Per: a quantity normalized to a fixed unit ("5 errors per 1,000 transactions")
- Per capita: normalized per person ("income per capita" is not total income)
- Ratio: a proportional relationship between two quantities ("a ratio of 9:1" — 9 successful for every 1 failed, so 90% vs. 10% if these are the only two categories)
- Proportion: a share of a total ("a higher proportion of users selected option A" — without an exact percentage, don't assume one)
:::

## Compared With, Relative To, and Baseline

::: important title="Every quantitative claim has a hidden reference point — find it"
*Error rates were 15% lower compared with the previous year* names its
baseline explicitly: *the previous year.* *Processing time fell by
20%* leaves the baseline implicit — compared with what: the previous
month, previous year, a control group, an initial measurement? A
quantitative claim without an identified baseline is incomplete, and
*relative to the baseline* phrasing should always trigger the
question: *relative to what, specifically?*
:::

## Statistical Language, and Two Kinds of "Significant"

::: concept title="Statistical significance is not the same as mattering in practice"
Terms like *statistically significant, confidence interval, standard
deviation, variance, correlation, regression, probability, p-value*
signal a technical statistical layer to a claim. Roughly: *statistically
significant* means an observed difference is unlikely to be pure
random variation, according to a formal test — but it does **not**
automatically mean *practically important.* *A new system reduced
processing time by 0.2 seconds, and the difference was statistically
significant* may be true and simultaneously operationally
insignificant — statistical significance and practical significance
are genuinely separate judgments.
:::

## Confidence Intervals and "Estimated" Values

::: important title="An estimate carries built-in uncertainty — don't treat it as exact"
*The estimated effect was 12%, with a 95% confidence interval of 8% to
16%* — for reading-level comprehension, the core takeaways are: the
estimate is 12%, and real uncertainty surrounds that number. Never
treat *12%* here as an unquestionable, exact truth. *Estimated cost,
estimated effect, estimated population* all signal the same thing: this
is not a directly observed exact value.
:::

## Approximately, Up To, As Much As, On Average, Typically

::: warning title="Five hedges that each bound a claim differently — none of them mean 'usually exactly'"
**Approximately 40%** means around 40%, not exactly 40.000…%. **Up to
40%** and **as much as 40%** both state a **maximum**, not a typical
or average result — *the system can reduce processing time by up to
40%* does not mean most users see a 40% reduction; it means 40% is the
ceiling. **On average** states a mean, not a universal outcome: *users
saved 15 minutes on average* does not mean every user saved exactly 15
minutes. **Typically** states the usual case, not every case: *users
typically complete the process within 10 minutes* leaves room for
exceptions.
:::

## A Quantitative Claim Strength Scale

::: framework title="Precision decreases as you move down this list"
- Exact
- Estimated
- Approximately
- Typically
- Generally
- Often
- Some
:::

## A Chart/Table Reading Algorithm

::: framework title="The full sequence for any chart or table you encounter"
- Title
- What is measured?
- Units
- X-axis / categories
- Y-axis / values
- Time period
- Baseline
- Main trend
- Largest change
- Smallest change
- Outliers
- Comparisons
- Qualifications
- Possible explanations
- What the data actually support
:::

## Data → Claim → Conclusion

::: warning title="An observed difference is a fact; a causal explanation is a further, separate claim"
System A: 90% success; System B: 80% success. You can accurately say
*System A had a higher observed success rate.* Saying *System A is
definitely better* requires knowing sample size, conditions,
measurement method, statistical uncertainty, comparable populations,
and testing environment — none of which the bare numbers alone
establish.
:::

::: framework title="The data-to-conclusion chain, one more time"
- Data
- Observed pattern
- Interpretation
- Claim
- Causal claim?
- Qualification
- Conclusion
:::

## A One-Sentence Data Summary, Done Well

::: example title="What's the biggest story — and what does it NOT establish?"
Given a table showing declining processing time and error rates from
2023-2025, a first-level summary: *processing time and error rates both
declined steadily between 2023 and 2025.* A second, equally necessary
step: *this does not by itself establish why they declined* — the
critical-reading half of quantitative reading, not an optional add-on.
:::

## A Fully Worked Example

::: example title="Every quantitative-reading skill from this lesson, in one passage"
**Following the implementation of the automated billing system,
average processing time fell from 18 minutes to 12 minutes,
representing a 33% reduction. However, because the analysis did not
include a comparable control group, the observed improvement cannot be
attributed solely to the new system.**

Data: 18 → 12 minutes. Absolute change: 6 minutes. Relative change:
≈33.3%. Interpretation: processing time decreased. Qualification: no
comparable control group. Causal limitation: improvement cannot be
attributed solely to the new system.
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read this passage once, then identify every quantitative claim's exact
scope before checking your reading against the notes that follow.

> A survey of 45 warehouses found that facilities using barcode
> scanning reported, on average, 18% fewer picking errors than those
> relying on manual entry. However, the improvement varied
> considerably: some facilities saw reductions of up to 35%, while a
> few reported no measurable change. The researchers noted that this
> variation was associated with differences in staff training levels,
> though the study did not include a controlled comparison sufficient
> to establish a causal relationship.

*On average, 18% fewer* is a mean, not a universal result. *Up to 35%*
states a maximum observed reduction, not a typical one. *A few
reported no measurable change* signals real variation exists — not
every facility benefited. *Associated with* signals correlation, and
the passage itself explicitly flags that no controlled comparison
supports a causal claim — the researchers are being appropriately
cautious about exactly the confounding-variable problem this lesson
covered.

## Golden Rule

::: golden
Don't just read the number — read what it measures, what it's compared with, how it changed, how certain it is, and what conclusion it actually supports.
:::

## Lesson Summary

This lesson built precise quantitative reading: the difference between
*by* and *to*, percentage points versus percent, relative versus
absolute change, the quantifier ladder from *none* to *all*, averages
versus medians, how to read a chart's axes before its shape, why
correlation in a table is not causation, sample size and confounding
variables, and the difference between statistical and practical
significance. Threaded through all of it was one recurring warning:
hedged language like *estimated, approximately, up to,* and *on
average* each bound a claim in a specific, non-interchangeable way.

::: important title="Ten distinctions worth keeping permanently separate"
By ≠ to. Percent ≠ percentage points. Most ≠ all. Few ≠ a few. No
evidence ≠ evidence of no effect. Average ≠ every case. Statistically
significant ≠ practically important. Correlation ≠ causation. Up to
40% ≠ typically 40%. Observed difference ≠ proven cause.
:::
