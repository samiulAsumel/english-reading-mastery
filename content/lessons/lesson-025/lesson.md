## Introduction

Every sentence you've studied so far in this course has a grammar (how
it's built) and a logic (how its ideas relate to the sentences around
it). Today adds a third, quieter layer: **information structure** — how
an author decides what the reader already knows, what's being
introduced for the first time, and which single piece of that
information deserves the reader's full attention. Native and highly
fluent readers track this layer almost unconsciously. Learning to
notice it on purpose is what makes a long, dense paragraph start to
feel natural instead of like a wall of equally-weighted facts.

## Given Information vs. New Information

::: concept title="What the reader already knows, and what's arriving for the first time"
**Given information** — content the reader already knows, or can
identify from context. **New information** — content the author is
introducing for the first time.

**A company introduced a new billing system. The system reduced
processing time.**

Sentence 1: *a new billing system* is new information. Sentence 2:
*the system* is now given information (you already met it); *reduced
processing time* is the new information this sentence adds.

Flow: **Given → New.**
:::

## Why This Matters

::: important title="Today's new information becomes tomorrow's given information"
English writers typically guide a reader's attention from *old/given*
information toward *new* information — and then that new information
becomes the *given* information the very next sentence builds on.

**The company introduced a new system. The system improved billing
accuracy. This improvement reduced customer complaints. These
reductions helped the company lower support costs.**

Chain: *new system → the system → billing accuracy → this improvement →
customer complaints → these reductions → support costs.* This handoff,
sentence after sentence, is what makes a paragraph feel like it's
moving somewhere rather than just listing facts.
:::

## Topic: What Is This Sentence About?

::: concept title="The entity a sentence is built around"
**The new billing system reduced processing time.**

Topic: *the new billing system* — the thing the sentence is about.
What's said about it: *reduced processing time.*
:::

## Focus: What's Specially Highlighted?

::: example title="The part carrying the sentence's real point"
**The new billing system reduced processing time by 30%.**

Topic: *the new billing system.* Focus: *reduced processing time by
30%* — the part carrying the sentence's new, specific, noteworthy
information.
:::

## Two Useful Models: Topic + Comment, Given → New

::: note title="Two ways of describing the same underlying pattern"
**Topic → Comment:** *The new system* (topic) *→ reduced processing
time* (comment).

**Given → New:** *The company introduced a new system. The system*
(given) *reduced processing time* (new).

Both models describe the same habit from slightly different angles —
use whichever one makes a given sentence's structure clearer to you.
:::

## Information Flow Across Several Sentences

::: example title="Watching new information become the next given"
**The company introduced a new billing system. The system automated
invoice generation. This automation reduced manual work.**

Sentence 1 — new: *new billing system.* Sentence 2 — given: *the
system*; new: *automated invoice generation.* Sentence 3 — given: *this
automation*; new: *reduced manual work.* This handoff is exactly what
lets a paragraph move forward naturally.
:::

## Why Authors Avoid Repeating Everything

::: warning title="Full repetition is grammatical, but it reads as clumsy"
**The company introduced a new billing system. The new billing system
automated invoices. The new billing system reduced manual work.**

Grammatically fine, but repetitive and flat. Compare: **The company
introduced a new billing system. The system automated invoices. This
change reduced manual work.** The second version has both cohesion
(Lesson 23) and a clear given→new information flow — which is exactly
why it reads as more natural, professional English.
:::

## Topic Continuity and Topic Shift

::: example title="A paragraph can hold one topic, or deliberately move to a new one"
**Continuity:** *Digital technology has changed workplace
communication. These technologies allow employees to exchange
information rapidly. Such systems can also support remote
collaboration. Their widespread use has changed how organizations
manage teams.* Topic chain: *digital technology → these technologies →
such systems → their widespread use* — the same topic, continuously
carried forward.

**Shift:** *Digital technology has changed workplace communication.
These systems allow employees to exchange information quickly.
However, employee training remains a major challenge.* Here the topic
shifts from *technology/systems* to *employee training* — and
*however* is exactly the signal warning the reader that a new
dimension is arriving.
:::

## Topic vs. Main Idea

::: important title="What it's about is not the same as what the author claims about it"
**Remote work has become increasingly common because organizations can
reduce office costs and access a wider talent pool.**

Topic: *remote work* — simply, *what is this about?* Main idea: *remote
work is becoming common partly because of organizational benefits* —
*what does the author actually claim about that topic?* Confusing the
two means you can name a paragraph's subject without ever grasping its
point.
:::

## Reordering for Emphasis: Fronting

::: example title="Moving a phrase to the front changes what gets emphasized first"
**Normal order:** *The company improved the system after several
months of testing.*

**Fronted:** *After several months of testing, the company improved the
system.*

Same information, but the fronted version puts the time/context
emphasis first — the reader processes *"after months of testing"*
before anything else.

**In particular, the new system improved billing accuracy.** Opening
with *in particular* signals immediately: *this specific point matters
more than a neutral statement would suggest.*
:::

## Emphasis Through Cleft Sentences

::: concept title="'It was X that...' isolates one piece of information for emphasis"
**Normal:** *The new billing system reduced processing time.*

**Cleft:** *It was the new billing system that reduced processing
time.* Focus: *the new billing system* — the cleft structure exists
specifically to put that phrase under a spotlight.

**It is employee training that determines whether the system is
adopted successfully.** Focus: *employee training* — the author is
claiming this factor matters more than any alternative a reader might
otherwise have assumed.
:::

::: example title="A second cleft pattern: What + clause + be + focus"
**Normal:** *The company needed a more reliable system.*

**What-cleft:** *What the company needed was a more reliable system.*
Focus: *a more reliable system.*

Both sentences carry the same basic meaning, but the *what*-cleft
version highlights *a more reliable system* far more strongly than the
plain version does.
:::

## "Not X But Y" and "Not Only X But Also Y"

::: example title="Rejecting one candidate, emphasizing the real one"
**The problem was not the software but the lack of training.**

Structure: *not X → but Y.* The author explicitly rejects *X* (the
software) and puts the emphasis on *Y* (lack of training).

**Successful implementation requires not only technology but also
organizational support.** Here both items matter, but the second item —
*organizational support* — typically carries the stronger rhetorical
weight, since English tends to save its most important item for last.
:::

## "Only" and Its Position

::: warning title="Where 'only' sits changes what it restricts"
**Only trained employees can access the system.** — meaning: *nobody
except trained employees can access it.*

**Trained employees can access only the new system.** — meaning: *they
can access the new system, and nothing else.*

Same words, rearranged — completely different restriction. Always check
exactly which phrase *only* is sitting next to before deciding what
it's limiting.
:::

## "Even": Signalling Surprise

::: example title="Even marks the least-expected case as still being true"
**Even experienced employees had difficulty using the new system.**

*Even* signals surprise: *experienced* employees — the group you'd
least expect to struggle — struggled too. The implication a careful
reader should draw: if even the most capable group had trouble, less
experienced employees almost certainly had more.

**The new system created difficulties for experienced employees. Even
senior managers required additional training.** Same pattern: naming
the most surprising case (*senior managers*) to imply that the problem
was genuinely widespread.
:::

## Especially, Particularly, and In Particular

::: example title="Narrowing a general claim down to the part that matters most"
**Especially:** *The system is useful especially for large
organizations.* Focus: *large organizations.*

**Particularly:** *The new system is particularly useful for complex
billing operations.* Highlights a specific condition or group.

**In particular:** *Several problems were identified. In particular,
users experienced difficulties during data entry.* Structure: *general
→ specific.* The general claim (*several problems*) gets narrowed to
the one the author wants you to focus on (*data-entry difficulties*).
:::

## Specifically, For Example, In Other Words, More Specifically

::: concept title="Four ways to move from general to specific, or to restate more clearly"
**Specifically:** *The system improved several aspects of billing.
Specifically, it reduced calculation errors.* General claim, then
specific evidence.

**For example:** *The system improved several processes. For example,
invoice generation became faster.* General claim, then one concrete
instance.

**In other words:** *The system is highly efficient. In other words, it
can process large amounts of data with relatively few resources.* Not
new information at all — a restatement, taking a general/abstract claim
and making it concrete.

**More specifically:** *The system improves performance. More
specifically, it reduces processing time during peak periods.* A broad
claim narrowed to a more precise one.
:::

## "At Least" and "Only Partly"

::: example title="Two ways an author deliberately limits their own claim"
**At least:** *The system improved at least some aspects of the
process.* — a deliberately minimal claim: *some* aspects, not
necessarily all of them.

**Only partly:** *The improvement was only partly due to automation.*
— automation contributed, but other factors mattered too. This
connects directly back to Lesson 24's causal qualifiers — *only partly*
is exactly the kind of hedge that keeps a causal claim honest.
:::

## "In Fact" and "Indeed"

::: example title="Correcting an expectation, or reinforcing a claim"
**In fact — correction:** *The system was expected to reduce costs. In
fact, costs increased during the first year.* *In fact* here highlights
a contrast between *expectation* and *actual result.*

**Indeed — reinforcement:** *The problem is significant. Indeed, it
affects several departments.* Here *indeed* strengthens the previous
claim rather than correcting it — context is what tells the two uses
apart.
:::

## Given/New Information and Pronouns, Together

::: note title="Lesson 23's reference chains carry given information forward"
**The company introduced a new platform. It was initially difficult to
use. This difficulty resulted from inadequate training.**

Given chain: *platform → it → this difficulty.* New information at each
step: *difficult to use → inadequate training.* Reference chains and
given/new structure are really two views of the same underlying habit —
a writer constantly recycling what's already known to introduce
something that isn't.
:::

## Information Packaging Inside a Noun Phrase

::: example title="New information can be packed into a single dense phrase"
**The company introduced a new billing system designed to automate
invoice processing.**

Core: *the company introduced a system.* Additional new information —
*designed to automate invoice processing* — is packed directly into
the noun phrase itself rather than given its own sentence. Lesson 22's
skill for unpacking dense noun phrases is exactly what lets you recover
this extra information cleanly.
:::

## Passive Voice as an Information-Structure Tool

::: important title="Passive voice moves what the author wants foregrounded into subject position"
**Active:** *Researchers identified several problems.* Focus:
*researchers' action.*

**Passive:** *Several problems were identified by researchers.* Focus:
*the problems themselves* — because *several problems* now occupies the
sentence's subject/topic position.

Revisit Lesson 13's passive voice with this in mind: the choice between
active and passive is very often not random — it's the author deciding
which piece of information belongs at the front of the sentence.
:::

::: example title="Why the agent so often disappears"
**Several problems were identified.** — the agent, *researchers*, has
been dropped entirely. The author judged the agent less important than
the problems themselves, so it's simply left out.
:::

::: example title="Passive voice sustaining a paragraph's information flow"
**Researchers conducted a large study. Several important patterns were
identified. These patterns were later examined in greater detail.**

Sentence 1 topic: *researchers.* Sentence 2 focus (via passive):
*important patterns.* Sentence 3 topic: *these patterns.* The paragraph
naturally moves *researchers → patterns → patterns* — passive voice is
what made that smooth handoff possible.
:::

## Existential "There"

::: concept title="Introducing something into the discussion for the first time"
**There are several factors that affect the outcome.**

*There* introduces the *existence* of something first. New information:
*several factors.* The relative clause that follows — *that affect the
outcome* — explains them further.

**There are several problems with the current system.** The reader
didn't know about these problems before this sentence — *there is/there
are* is one of English's most common ways to bring a brand-new entity
onto the page.
:::

::: example title="After the introduction, a pronoun takes over"
**There are several problems with the current system. These problems
affect both employees and customers.**

Sentence 1: *problems* = new. Sentence 2: *these problems* = given.
This two-step move — introduce with *there is/are*, then refer back
with a pronoun or *this/these* — is an extremely common and very
readable pattern.
:::

## Three Labels for Reading

::: concept title="G, N, and F — a fast way to mark up a sentence"
**G = Given** — already known. **N = New** — introduced for the first
time. **F = Focus** — specially emphasized.

**The company introduced a new system. The system reduced processing
time.**

Second sentence: *The system* = **G**. *Reduced processing time* =
**N/F**. Marking sentences this way, even mentally, makes a dense
paragraph's structure visible almost instantly.
:::

## Paragraph-Level Information Flow

::: example title="Following the handoff across a whole paragraph"
**The company introduced a new billing system. The system automated
invoice generation. This automation reduced manual work. The reduction
in manual work allowed employees to focus on customer service.**

Flow: *new billing system [N] → the system [G] → automated invoice
generation [N] → this automation [G] → reduced manual work [N] → the
reduction in manual work [G] → customer service [N].* Recognising this
handoff pattern is one of the most reliable ways to follow a long,
information-dense paragraph without losing the thread.
:::

## Thematic Progression

::: concept title="How a paragraph's topic develops, sentence by sentence"
Discourse analysis calls a paragraph's topic development **thematic
progression**. The basic pattern: *Theme 1 → Rheme 1*, then *Rheme 1 →
Theme 2*, then *Rheme 2 → Theme 3.* In plain terms: *new information
becomes the next sentence's topic* — exactly the handoff you've been
tracking throughout this lesson.
:::

::: example title="Three common progression patterns"
**Constant topic:** *The company introduced a new system. The company
trained its employees. The company monitored performance.* Theme stays
fixed: *company → company → company* — strong topic continuity.

**Linear progression:** *The company introduced a new system. The
system improved accuracy. Improved accuracy reduced customer
complaints.* Flow: *system → accuracy → complaints* — each sentence's
new information becomes the next sentence's topic. This is the most
common pattern in explanatory writing.

**Split progression:** *The new system improved accuracy and speed.*
followed by: *Improved accuracy reduced errors. Faster processing
reduced delays.* One sentence introduces two ideas, and the following
sentences each develop one of the two branches separately.
:::

::: important title="Recognising the pattern lets you summarize faster"
Tracking a paragraph's thematic progression tells you *where the author
started and where they ended up* — which is very often the fastest
route to summarizing a dense paragraph accurately.
:::

## Topic Sentences

::: example title="Usually first, but not always"
**Automation is changing logistics operations.** followed by *these
systems…, such technologies…, these changes…* — every subsequent
sentence connects back to the opening topic sentence.

But not every paragraph opens with its main point: **Logistics
operations have become increasingly complex as shipment volumes have
grown. Automation is therefore becoming an important part of modern
port management.** Here the main point arrives in the *second*
sentence, after a sentence of context — so *"the topic sentence is
always first"* is not a safe rule to rely on.
:::

::: note title="The last sentence often carries the real weight"
**Automation reduces processing time. It can also improve accuracy.
However, successful implementation requires employee training.
Therefore, technological investment alone is not sufficient.**

The final sentence frequently carries the paragraph's conclusion,
implication, or qualification — the single sentence worth reading most
carefully if you're skimming for the main takeaway.
:::

## Explicit Emphasis Markers

::: concept title="Phrases that announce their own importance directly"
**What matters is…** *What matters is how the system is implemented.*
Structure: *what matters* (frame) + *how the system is implemented*
(focus).

**The key point is…** *The key point is that technology alone cannot
solve the problem.* The author is telling the reader outright: *this is
the important part.*

**The main reason is…** *The company delayed the project. The main
reason was insufficient testing.* Topic: *delay.* New focus:
*insufficient testing.*

**One important difference is…** *One important difference is that the
new system requires less manual input.* — a comparison text explicitly
flagging its own focal point.
:::

## Contrast and Focus

::: example title="Whereas, unlike, compared with, rather than"
**Whereas:** *The old system was relatively simple, whereas the new
system is significantly more flexible.* Contrast: *old vs. new.*
Focus: *the new system's flexibility.*

**Unlike:** *Unlike the previous system, the new platform can process
data in real time.* The opening phrase immediately points the reader's
attention toward a comparison.

**Compared with:** *Compared with traditional methods, automated
systems can process information more rapidly.* Given: *traditional
methods.* New: *the automated systems' advantage.*

**Rather than:** *The company focused on improving the existing system
rather than replacing it.* Focus: *improving the existing system.*
Contrast: *improving vs. replacing.*
:::

## Negative Information Structure

::: important title="Telling the reader what not to focus on"
**The problem is not the technology itself but the way it is
implemented.**

Sometimes an author's clearest move is naming what the reader
*shouldn't* focus on before naming what they should. Focus:
*implementation.* This move is especially common in argumentative
writing, where correcting a likely misreading is often the whole point
of the sentence.
:::

## A Six-Step Reading Strategy for Information Structure

::: framework title="Working through any sentence's information structure"
- Who or what is this sentence about? → Topic
- What does the author say about it? → Comment
- What is already known? → Given
- What is new? → New
- What is emphasized? → Focus
- Does the new information become the next sentence's topic? → Flow
:::

## Full Worked Example: Port Operations

::: example title="Six sentences, tracked through every layer at once"
**Modern port operations increasingly depend on digital systems. These
systems collect large amounts of operational data. The data can then be
analyzed to identify delays and inefficiencies. Such analysis provides
managers with information that can support faster decisions. However,
the value of these systems depends partly on data quality. Poor-quality
data can produce misleading results, regardless of how advanced the
technology is.**

**Sentence 1** — topic: *modern port operations*; new: *depend
increasingly on digital systems.*

**Sentence 2** — topic: *these systems*; given: *digital systems*; new:
*collect operational data.*

**Sentence 3** — topic: *the data*; given: *operational data*; new:
*can be analyzed.*

**Sentence 4** — topic: *such analysis*; given: *analysis of data*;
new: *provides decision-support information.*

**Sentence 5** — topic: *the value of these systems*; new: *depends
partly on data quality.* *However* signals a topic shift/qualification.

**Sentence 6** — topic: *poor-quality data*; new: *can produce
misleading results.* The closing phrase — *regardless of how advanced
the technology is* — carries heavy emphasis: it insists that
technology quality alone cannot compensate for poor data.
:::

::: note title="The paragraph's backbone, as a flow diagram"
Digital systems → operational data → data analysis → decision support →
data quality → reliability of results.

Reading only the vocabulary — *digital, operational, data, analyzed,
inefficiency, managers, quality, misleading* — gets you the topic.
Reading the information flow gets you the actual argument: *digital
systems are useful, but their value ultimately depends on the quality
of the data they process.*
:::

## Seven Levels for Reading Any Difficult Paragraph

| Level | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Sentence | Subject → Verb → Object |
| Clause | Main clause → subordinate clauses |
| Logic | Cause / contrast / condition / result / evidence |
| Cohesion | Pronoun / repetition / synonym / lexical chain |
| Information | Given → New |
| Focus | What is emphasized? |
| Paragraph | Topic → development → evidence → qualification → conclusion |

From today, read every sentence not just as grammar, but as an
information system — a structure built specifically to manage what you
already know and what you're about to learn.

## Guided Reading Practice

Read this passage once for its overall meaning, then go back through it
sentence by sentence, labelling each one's topic, given information,
new information, and — where present — its point of special emphasis.

> Container terminals have traditionally relied on manual yard planning
> to decide where each container should be stored. This approach often
> led to inefficient use of yard space, particularly during periods of
> high volume. Automated yard-planning software can now calculate
> optimal container placement in real time. It is not the software
> itself that guarantees better yard efficiency, however; rather, the
> accuracy of the data it receives from gate and crane operations
> determines how well the system actually performs.

**Sentence 1** — topic: *container terminals*; new: *traditionally
relied on manual yard planning.*

**Sentence 2** — topic: *this approach*; given: *manual yard planning*;
new: *often led to inefficient use of yard space.* Focus, via
*particularly*: *periods of high volume.*

**Sentence 3** — topic: *automated yard-planning software*; new: *can
calculate optimal container placement in real time.*

**Sentence 4** — a cleft structure: *it is not the software itself that
guarantees better efficiency; rather, [data accuracy] determines
performance.* This sentence explicitly rejects one candidate focus (the
software) and redirects emphasis onto another (data accuracy) — exactly
the "negative information structure" pattern from earlier in this
lesson.

Work out for yourself what the paragraph's overall information flow is
— which noun phrase keeps returning, renamed, as each sentence's given
information — before moving on.

## Golden Rule

::: golden
Don't just ask what a sentence means — ask what's already known, what's new, and what the author wants you to notice.
:::

## Lesson Summary

Today added a layer of reading that sits underneath grammar and logic
alike: how an author manages what the reader already knows against what
they're about to learn, and which single piece of that information
deserves the spotlight. You practised sorting given information from
new information, identifying a sentence's topic and focus, reading
cleft structures and fronting as deliberate emphasis devices, and
tracing how passive voice and existential *there* reposition
information for exactly that purpose. Most importantly, you practised
following thematic progression — watching a paragraph's new information
become its next sentence's topic — which is very often the fastest
route to a dense paragraph's real backbone.

::: important title="The three questions to run automatically"
From now on, run every sentence through three quick questions: *what's
already known here? what's new? what does the author want me to
notice?* Those three questions are the whole of today's skill, compressed
into a habit you can use on anything you read.
:::

responsibilities for managers. In particular, managers must evaluate
the reliability of AI-generated recommendations. It is not the
technology itself that guarantees better decisions; rather, the quality
of the information used and the way the technology is applied are
critical.**
