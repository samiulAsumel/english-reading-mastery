## Introduction

Today's goal is not just another grammar rule. It is confidence: the
moment you see a long, complicated sentence in a real book and feel no
fear at all, because you already know exactly how to take it apart.

In Lesson 1 you learned the core pipeline every English sentence is built
from:

**Main Subject → Main Verb → Object/Complement → Modifiers**

Today we go one level deeper. By the end of this lesson you will be able
to look at a sentence three times longer than anything in Lesson 1 and
still find its meaning in seconds.

## Why a Book Sentence Feels So Hard

::: concept title="Skeleton first, decoration second"
A beginner usually reads left to right, translating each word as it
comes:

> Successful leaders carefully consider the opinions of their team
> members before making important decisions.

*Successful* = সফল, *leaders* = নেতারা, *carefully* = সতর্কভাবে,
*consider* = বিবেচনা করে... By the time this reaches the end of the
sentence, it feels enormous and exhausting.

A confident reader does something completely different: they find the
sentence's **skeleton** first.

**Skeleton:** Successful leaders consider the opinions.

Everything else is simply detail hung on that skeleton:

- *Successful* describes **leaders**
- *carefully* describes how they **consider**
- *of their team members* tells you whose **opinions**
- *before making important decisions* tells you **when** they consider

Find the skeleton, and the "big, scary sentence" turns out to be one
small, simple sentence wearing a lot of decoration. This is the single
most important skill in today's lesson.
:::

## Core vs. Extra Information

Nearly every long sentence has one small core sentence hiding inside it.

::: example title="Finding the core"
**The experienced manager carefully reviewed the financial report
yesterday.**

| Word/phrase | What it adds |
|---|---|
| experienced | describes *manager* |
| carefully | describes *how* the report was reviewed |
| financial | describes *report* |
| yesterday | tells you *when* |

**Core:** The manager reviewed the report.
:::

So every time you meet a long sentence, ask the same four questions in
order:

1. **Who?** — find the core subject
2. **Did what?** — find the main verb
3. **To what/whom?** — find the object or complement
4. Only then, add back the rest of the information

## What Is a Modifier?

A **modifier** is a word, or a group of words, that gives extra
information about another word — without changing what the core
sentence is about.

::: example title="A modifier decorates; it doesn't replace the core"
**Intelligent students learn quickly.**

*students* = the main noun · *intelligent* = information about
*students* · *learn* = the main verb · *quickly* = information about how
they *learn*.

**Core:** Students learn.

**The young researcher carefully examined the complex problem.**

**Core:** The researcher examined the problem.

Modifiers: *young* → researcher · *carefully* → examined · *complex* →
problem.
:::

## Adjectives: Extra Information About a Noun

An **adjective** almost always describes a noun.

::: example title="Adjective + noun"
**a smart student** — *smart* describes *student*

**a complex problem** — *complex* describes *problem*

**an important decision** — *important* describes *decision*

**successful leaders** — *successful* describes *leaders*
:::

This matters enormously once real books start stacking adjectives
together. When you meet a phrase like:

> a highly successful business leader

don't decode it word by word. Grab the whole phrase as one unit:

**Description:** highly successful business
**Core noun:** leader

Read the description as a block wrapped around the noun, not as four
separate translation problems.

## Adverbs: Extra Information About a Verb

An **adverb** tells you *how*, *when*, or *how often* something happens
— it modifies the verb, not a noun.

::: example title="Adverb + verb"
**She speaks clearly.**
Core: *She speaks.* — *clearly* answers **how** she speaks.

**He carefully analyzed the problem.**
Core: *He analyzed the problem.* — *carefully* answers **how**.

**They often discuss important issues.**
Core: *They discuss issues.* — *often* answers **how frequently**.
:::

A handful of adverbs you will meet constantly: *carefully, quickly,
slowly, clearly, usually, often, sometimes, rarely, completely,
strongly, effectively.* Once you recognise the shape "-ly word next to a
verb," you can skip straight to asking what question it answers, instead
of stopping to translate it.

## Prepositional Phrases

Small connector words — *of, in, on, for, with, about, from, to, by* —
build some of the longest phrases you will meet in real English writing.

::: example title="Prepositional phrase = connector + extra detail"
**the opinions of experienced managers**
Core noun: *opinions* — extra: *of experienced managers*

**people in modern society**
Core noun: *people* — extra: *in modern society*

**decisions about important issues**
Core noun: *decisions* — extra: *about important issues*
:::

::: note title="A trick for very long sentences: remove the prepositional phrases first"
**The behavior of people in different cultures can be difficult to
understand.**

At first glance this looks dense. Temporarily set the prepositional
phrases aside:

**Main structure:** The behavior can be difficult.

Now the rest simply answers follow-up questions:

- *of people* — whose behavior?
- *in different cultures* — in what context?
- *to understand* — difficult to do what?

This "temporarily remove the prepositional phrases" trick works on
almost any long sentence you'll meet.
:::

## Clauses: The Next Building Block

We now reach the most important building block for reading real books.

::: concept title="A clause has a Subject and a Verb"
**People learn.**
Subject: *People* · Verb: *learn* → this is a clause.

**because people practice regularly**
Subject: *people* · Verb: *practice* → this is also a clause.

But notice the difference: the second one doesn't feel finished. Hearing
*"because people practice regularly…"* makes you expect a *"...then
what?"* That incompleteness is the key to the next distinction.
:::

## Independent vs. Dependent Clauses

An **independent clause** can stand alone as a complete sentence.

> People practice regularly.

That's whole. Nothing is missing.

A **dependent clause** cannot stand alone, even though it has its own
Subject and Verb.

> because people practice regularly

The word *because* is exactly what makes it dependent — it promises a
result that hasn't arrived yet. Put the two together, and you get a
complete sentence with two parts:

> **People improve** *because they practice regularly.*

- Main (independent) clause: *People improve.*
- Dependent clause: *because they practice regularly.*

::: important title="Why clauses matter so much for reading books"
Authors rarely write only in short, simple sentences. A real book
sentence looks more like this:

> People often make poor decisions because they fail to recognize the
> factors that influence their judgment.

Read all at once, this is intimidating. Broken into its pieces, it is
not:

- **Main clause:** People often make poor decisions.
- **Reason (dependent clause):** because they fail to recognize the
  factors
- **Extra detail about "the factors":** that influence their judgment

Put back together as a structure, not a wall of words:

> People make poor decisions
> → *because* they fail to recognize the factors
> → *that* influence their judgment.

The sentence stops being frightening the moment you see its shape.
:::

## Relative Clauses

Today's most important advanced concept: the **relative clause**. A
relative clause gives extra information about a noun, and it usually
begins with *who, which, that, whose,* or *whom*.

::: concept title="A relative clause describes a noun"
**People who understand human behavior communicate better.**

Core sentence: *People communicate better.*
Extra information: *who understand human behavior* — this tells you
exactly **which** people.

**The book that I bought yesterday is useful.**

Core sentence: *The book is useful.*
Extra information: *that I bought yesterday* — this tells you exactly
**which** book.
:::

::: example title="Spotting a relative clause"
**The people who work here are experienced.**

Look closely at *who work here* — it behaves like a tiny sentence of its
own, with its own Subject and Verb:

Subject: *who* · Verb: *work*

That mini-clause is describing *people*. So *people* + *who work here*
combine into a single, larger noun phrase — one unit, not two separate
ideas.
:::

## The Six-Step Method for Decoding Any Sentence

::: framework title="Decode any long sentence in six steps"
- Find the main Subject
- Find the main Verb
- Find the Object or Complement
- Identify any relative clause (who / which / that / whose / whom)
- Identify any prepositional phrases (of / in / on / for / with / about / from)
- Reconstruct the full meaning
:::

Let's put this to work on two full sentences.

::: example title="Worked example 1 — 'Successful people develop skills that help them solve difficult problems.'"
**Step 1 — Subject:** Successful people
**Step 2 — Main verb:** develop
**Step 3 — Object:** skills

**Core:** Successful people develop skills.

**Step 4 — Relative clause:** *that help them solve difficult problems*
— this describes *skills*: what kind of skills, specifically.

**Reconstructed meaning:** Successful people build the kind of skills
that let them solve hard problems.
:::

::: example title="Worked example 2 — 'People who read regularly develop a deeper understanding of the subjects they study.'"
**Step 1 — Subject:** People
**Step 2 — Main verb:** develop
**Step 3 — Object:** a deeper understanding

**Core:** People develop a deeper understanding.

**Step 4 — Relative clause on the subject:** *who read regularly* —
which people, specifically.
**Step 5 — Prepositional phrase:** *of the subjects* — an understanding
of what.
**Step 5 (continued) — a second relative clause:** *they study* —
describing *the subjects*, telling you which ones.

Laid out as a structure:

> People
> → who read regularly
> → develop
> → a deeper understanding
> → of the subjects
> → they study.

This layered, step-by-step decoding is exactly how you should approach
every long sentence from now on.
:::

## From Translating to Understanding Directly

::: golden
Find the skeleton — Subject, Verb, Object or Complement, and any clause — before you look at a single modifier; everything else is detail hanging off that frame.
:::

Build this habit today, on every sentence you read:

::: framework title="Don't translate — decode, understand, reconstruct"
- English sentence
- Main Subject
- Main Verb
- Object or Complement
- Clauses (independent, dependent, relative)
- Modifiers (adjectives, adverbs, prepositional phrases)
- Overall meaning
:::

The old habit — English word → Bangla word → another Bangla word →
assemble a Bangla sentence — gets slower the longer the sentence gets.
This new habit gets *faster* the more you practise it, because you are
recognising shapes, not translating words.

## Vocabulary in Context

You already met **affect** in Lesson 1. Here it returns with a wider set
of natural partner words, because a verb like this is only half-learned
until you know what it usually attaches to.

::: vocabulary word="recognize" pos="verb" meaning="to become aware of or identify something (চিনতে/উপলব্ধি করতে)" example="Good readers recognize a difficult sentence before they finish it."
:::

::: vocabulary word="factor" pos="noun" meaning="one of several things that influences a result (প্রভাবক/কারণ)" example="Experience is an important factor in good judgment."
:::

::: vocabulary word="judgment" pos="noun" meaning="the ability to make sensible decisions (বিচার/মূল্যায়ন)" example="Stress can weaken a person's judgment."
:::

::: vocabulary word="behavior" pos="noun" meaning="the way a person acts (আচরণ)" example="The passage discusses human behavior in detail."
:::

::: vocabulary word="approach" pos="noun" meaning="a way of doing or thinking about something (পদ্ধতি/দৃষ্টিভঙ্গি)" example="A practical approach solves the problem without wasted effort."
:::

::: vocabulary word="require" pos="verb" meaning="to need something in order to work or succeed (প্রয়োজন হওয়া)" example="Clear writing requires careful planning."
:::

::: vocabulary word="involve" pos="verb" meaning="to include something as a necessary part (জড়িত থাকা)" example="Good decisions often involve several factors."
:::

::: vocabulary word="affect" pos="verb" meaning="to produce a change in something (প্রভাবিত করা)" example="Emotions can affect behavior, decisions, performance, and judgment."
:::

::: vocabulary word="depend" pos="verb" meaning="to rely on something in order to happen or be true (নির্ভর করা)" example="A good decision often depends on experience."
:::

::: vocabulary word="determine" pos="verb" meaning="to decide or control the outcome of something (নির্ধারণ করা)" example="Preparation can determine the outcome of a difficult situation."
:::

::: note title="Learn collocations, not isolated words"
Don't just memorise *affect = প্রভাবিত করা* as an isolated equation. Learn
which words it naturally attaches to:

*affect behavior · affect decisions · affect performance · affect
judgment*

A word you know only in isolation is a word you will hesitate over in
real text. A word you know through its natural partners is a word you
will recognise instantly.
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read the paragraph once, slowly, with no dictionary.

> People do not always make decisions based on facts alone. Their
> emotions, previous experiences, beliefs, and social environment can
> all affect the way they think. People who understand these influences
> may become more aware of their own thinking patterns. As a result,
> they may be able to make more careful and effective decisions.

Now decode it, sentence by sentence, using exactly the method from this
lesson.

**Sentence 1 — People do not always make decisions based on facts
alone.**
Core: *People do not always make decisions.* Extra: *based on facts
alone* tells you the basis for those decisions.

**Sentence 2 — Their emotions, previous experiences, beliefs, and social
environment can all affect the way they think.**
Core: *Their emotions, experiences, beliefs, and environment can affect
the way they think.*

**Sentence 3 — People who understand these influences may become more
aware of their own thinking patterns.**
Core: *People may become more aware of their thinking patterns.*
Relative clause: *who understand these influences* — which people,
specifically.

**Sentence 4 — As a result, they may be able to make more careful and
effective decisions.**
Core: *They may be able to make decisions.* Extra: *As a result* links
this sentence to the one before it; *more careful and effective*
describes the kind of decisions.

## Lesson Summary

Today you learned to see past a sentence's decoration to its skeleton:
Subject, Verb, Object or Complement. You learned that modifiers —
adjectives, adverbs, and prepositional phrases — add detail without
changing that skeleton, and that a clause is any Subject-Verb pair,
whether it can stand alone (independent) or not (dependent). You met the
relative clause, the tool authors use constantly to attach extra
information directly onto a noun. Most importantly, you now have a
six-step method for decoding *any* long sentence, however intimidating
it first looks.
