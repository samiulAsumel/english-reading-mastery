## Introduction

Lesson 34 asked why an author says something the way they do. Today's
lesson asks something closer to the heart: *how does the author feel
about what they're discussing?* This is **tone** — and closely related
to it, **bias**: a systematic tendency to present information in one
direction rather than a neutral or balanced one. Both matter enormously
for reading opinion pieces, editorials, business reports, and history
well, because an author's attitude shapes a text just as much as its
logic does, often without ever being stated outright.

## What Is Tone?

::: concept title="The attitude a writer expresses toward their subject"
**Tone** = the attitude or emotional/intellectual manner behind an
author's words. Tone can be positive, negative, neutral, cautious,
skeptical, critical, optimistic, pessimistic, concerned, enthusiastic,
dismissive, approving, doubtful, sarcastic, respectful, or analytical —
often several of these blended together in one passage.
:::

## Tone vs. Topic

::: important title="The same subject can carry very different attitudes"
**Topic:** artificial intelligence in logistics.

**Positive tone:** *AI offers promising opportunities to improve
logistics operations.* **Negative tone:** *AI introduces serious risks
that organizations have yet to address adequately.* **Neutral tone:**
*AI is increasingly being used in logistics operations.* Same topic,
three entirely different attitudes.
:::

## How to Detect Tone

::: framework title="Three sources of evidence for any tone judgment"
- Word choice: which adjectives, verbs, and nouns carry evaluation?
- Sentence structure: strong claim, cautious claim, rhetorical question, or concession?
- Evidence selection: which evidence does the author highlight, and which is left out?
:::

## Positive and Negative Evaluative Language

::: example title="Two word families, and what they signal"
**Positive:** effective, efficient, valuable, promising, beneficial,
innovative, successful, reliable, robust, compelling. **Negative:**
ineffective, inefficient, costly, problematic, flawed, questionable,
risky, inadequate, misleading, unreliable.

**The company adopted a promising technology** signals a positive
attitude; **the company adopted a questionable technology** signals a
negative or skeptical one.
:::

## Neutral and Analytical Tone

::: concept title="Descriptive writing, and evidence-focused academic writing"
**Neutral:** *The system was introduced in January and is currently
used in three distribution centers.* No *promising*, no
*problematic*, no *successful*, no *disastrous* — purely descriptive.

**Analytical:** *The results suggest that the intervention may have
contributed to the observed improvement, although alternative
explanations cannot be ruled out.* Very common in academic writing —
the author is engaging with evidence and limitation rather than
emotion.
:::

## Skeptical and Critical Tone

::: example title="Not accepting a claim easily, and actively challenging it"
**Skeptical signals:** questionable, uncertain, unclear, doubtful,
limited evidence, remains unclear, cannot be established, raises
concerns, may reflect, could be explained by. *Whether the observed
improvement was caused by the intervention remains unclear.*

**Critical (stronger) signals:** fails to, overlooks, ignores,
inadequately addresses, provides little support, is difficult to
justify. *The study fails to adequately address several important
methodological limitations.*
:::

## Cautious, Optimistic, and Pessimistic Tone

::: example title="Three more attitudes, in contrasting pairs"
**Cautious:** *The findings may indicate a relationship between
employee training and productivity* — *may indicate* signals genuine
uncertainty, not a firm causal claim.

**Optimistic:** *Advances in automation are likely to create
significant opportunities for logistics companies.* **Pessimistic:**
*Increasing dependence on automation could create new operational
vulnerabilities.* *Opportunities* vs. *vulnerabilities* — this is
lexical framing doing the work of tone.
:::

## Concerned, Enthusiastic, Dismissive, and Respectful Tone

::: concept title="Four more attitudes worth recognising by their signal words"
**Concerned:** *The rapid adoption of AI raises serious concerns about
data security and accountability.* Note: *"raises concerns"* signals
worry — it doesn't mean *"danger has been proven."*

**Enthusiastic:** *The new system represents a remarkable breakthrough
in logistics management* — *remarkable, breakthrough, transformative,
revolutionary, extraordinary* are strong positive rhetorical language;
ask whether the evidence is equally strong.

**Dismissive:** *Such objections are largely irrelevant to the central
issue*; *this argument overlooks the most basic facts* — treating an
opposing position as unworthy of serious engagement.

**Respectful/concessive:** *Critics raise a legitimate concern
regarding implementation costs. However, these costs may be reduced
over time.* Balanced, fair-minded, and analytical all at once.
:::

## Sarcasm

::: warning title="Rare in serious nonfiction, and risky to assume without context"
**Of course, manually entering thousands of records is the perfect way
to maximize efficiency.** Literal meaning: manual entry is perfect.
Intended meaning: manual entry is obviously inefficient. Sarcasm is
genuinely hard to detect reliably; in academic and nonfiction texts, it
appears far less often than in casual writing — don't assume it without
strong contextual signals.
:::

## Tone vs. Certainty

::: important title="Attitude and confidence are two separate dimensions"
**Tone** = attitude. **Certainty** = how strongly the author commits to
a proposition. These are genuinely different.

**The approach may be highly effective.** — tone: positive; certainty:
moderate/low. **The approach is clearly ineffective.** — tone:
negative; certainty: high. Attitude and certainty can combine in any
of four ways — don't assume one tells you the other.
:::

## Tone vs. Evidence

::: warning title="Positive-sounding rhetoric is not the same as positive-sounding proof"
**This remarkable innovation will transform the logistics industry.**
Strong positive rhetoric — but where is the evidence? **This deeply
flawed proposal…** — strong negative rhetoric — but is the proposal
actually shown to be flawed, with evidence? A confident tone, in either
direction, never substitutes for evidence.
:::

## What Is Bias?

::: concept title="A systematic tendency in one direction"
**Bias** = a consistent tendency to present or evaluate information in
a particular direction. Bias isn't always intentional — someone can be
sincerely, honestly biased without meaning to mislead anyone.
:::

## Bias vs. Opinion

::: important title="A stated preference, or a systematic imbalance"
**Opinion:** *I prefer automated systems* — a personal preference,
openly stated. **Bias:** *an author consistently highlights the
benefits of automation while repeatedly ignoring evidence of its
disadvantages* — a systematic imbalance running through the whole
text, not a single, honestly labelled preference.
:::

## Six Questions for Detecting Bias

::: framework title="Testing a text for systematic imbalance"
- What evidence is included?
- What evidence is omitted?
- How are opposing views described — respectfully, or dismissively?
- Which words are loaded?
- Is the evidence presented in a balanced way?
- Does the conclusion exceed what the evidence actually supports?
:::

## Selection Bias, Omission, and False Balance

::: example title="Three ways bias can operate without a single false sentence"
**Selection bias in writing:** *five companies successfully implemented
AI. Therefore, AI implementation is highly successful.* If unsuccessful
companies' data was simply left out, the presented evidence is already
skewed before any analysis begins.

**Omission:** a report highlights productivity gains from automation
but never mentions implementation cost, job displacement, error rates,
or maintenance cost. Bias doesn't require false information — leaving
out what matters is often enough.

**False balance:** if 95% of credible evidence supports position A and
5% of weak evidence supports B, writing *"both sides are equally
valid"* misrepresents the actual weight of evidence. True balance
means representing evidence *proportionally to its strength*, not
splitting the page in half.
:::

## Loaded Questions and Loaded Words

::: warning title="The wording of a question can smuggle in an assumption"
**Loaded question:** *Why has the company failed to address its
obvious safety problems?* This already assumes the company has obvious
safety problems. Neutral version: *What safety concerns, if any, has
the company faced?*

**Loaded words:** *government policy* vs. *government interference*;
*tax reform* vs. *tax burden*; *employee restructuring* vs. *mass
layoffs*; *security measure* vs. *surveillance mechanism.* Ask, term by
term: *is this word descriptive, or evaluative?*
:::

## Authorial Distance

::: important title="Reporting verbs can quietly signal how much the author personally believes"
Compare four versions: *the policy is ineffective* → *critics claim
that the policy is ineffective* → *critics have alleged that the policy
is ineffective* → *some commentators have suggested that the policy may
be ineffective.* Each version puts a little more distance between the
author and the claim's truth — the author's own commitment shrinks as
the sentence moves further from a direct statement.

*Claim* itself isn't always neutral: *researchers found…* (neutral) vs.
*researchers argued…* (a position) vs. *researchers claimed…* (some
distance) vs. *researchers alleged…* (often signals the claim is
disputed or unverified).
:::

## Admit, Acknowledge, and Refused to Admit

::: example title="Small verb choices, real attitude signals"
**Admitted** vs. **acknowledged:** *the company admitted that the
system had limitations* can carry a hint of fault or reluctance in some
contexts; *acknowledged* tends to read as more neutral.

**Refused to admit:** *the company refused to admit that the system had
failed* isn't just reporting a fact — *"refused to admit"* implies the
company *should* have accepted something, which is negative framing
built directly into the verb choice.
:::

## Hedge Stacking and Combined Signals

::: note title="Reading certainty and evaluation together"
**The findings may perhaps suggest that the intervention could
potentially improve efficiency.** Four hedges stacked in one sentence
(*may, perhaps, suggest, could potentially*) — read this as very low
author commitment, not moderate caution.

**Strong certainty + negative evaluation:** *the proposal is clearly
inadequate* (*clearly* = high certainty; *inadequate* = negative) → a
high-certainty negative stance. **Weak certainty + positive
evaluation:** *the proposal may be highly beneficial* (*may* = weak
commitment; *highly beneficial* = positive) → positive but cautious.
:::

## A Tone Map

| | Low certainty | High certainty |
|---|---|---|
| Positive | may be useful | is clearly useful |
| Neutral | may indicate | demonstrates |
| Negative | may be harmful | is clearly harmful |

Keeping tone and certainty on two separate axes, like this grid, makes
it much easier to describe a sentence's stance precisely instead of
reaching for a single vague label.

## A Deep Reading Passage

::: example title="Read once, slowly, before checking the tone analysis below"
**The rapid adoption of AI in logistics has generated considerable
enthusiasm among industry leaders. Supporters argue that automated
systems can substantially improve efficiency and reduce operational
costs. These claims are not without merit: several large-scale
implementations have reported measurable improvements in scheduling
and resource utilization. Nevertheless, the widespread enthusiasm may
be premature. Evidence regarding long-term reliability, workforce
adaptation, and system-wide costs remains comparatively limited.
Rather than treating AI as a guaranteed solution, organizations should
therefore approach adoption as a process requiring careful evaluation,
monitoring, and human oversight.**
:::

**Sentence 1** — descriptive, slightly positive context. **Sentence 2**
— attribution: *supporters argue* — not yet the author's own claim.
**Sentence 3** — *"these claims are not without merit"* concedes some
validity without full endorsement; tone: balanced/concessive.
**Sentence 4** — *nevertheless* signals a strong shift; *"enthusiasm
may be premature"* pairs a hedge (*may*) with a negative evaluation
(*premature*) — skeptical but cautious. **Sentence 5** — names the
evidence limitation directly. **Final sentence** — a recommendation, not
a rejection: *approach adoption as a process requiring careful
evaluation.*

::: important title="Reconstructing the author's real position"
The author does **not** reject AI, and does **not** claim it's
guaranteed to succeed. They acknowledge AI's benefits, highlight its
limitations, and recommend cautious adoption — best described as
**qualified support**, or a **cautiously favorable position.** Neither
extreme captures it accurately.
:::

## The Tone Spectrum

::: concept title="A useful mental scale for locating an author's position"
Strongly negative → critical → skeptical → cautious → neutral →
qualified support → positive → enthusiastic → strongly positive. Most
serious nonfiction authors land somewhere in the middle third of this
spectrum, not at either end.
:::

## Objectivity

::: important title="A direction to aim for, never a destination fully reached"
**Objectivity** = focusing on evidence and reasoning while minimizing
personal preference or loaded judgment. Objective writing typically:
identifies its sources, distinguishes evidence from interpretation,
acknowledges uncertainty, considers opposing evidence, uses precise
language, and maintains scope carefully.

But **no writing is perfectly objective** — topic selection, evidence
selection, framing, terminology, and emphasis all involve choices, and
every choice shapes the result at least a little.
:::

::: example title="More objective and less objective, compared"
**Less objective:** *This ridiculous policy will obviously destroy the
industry.* Problems: *ridiculous, obviously, destroy* — strong
emotional language throughout.

**More objective:** *Critics argue that the policy could impose
substantial costs on the industry, although the magnitude of these
effects remains uncertain.* Attribution, a hedge, and an explicit
acknowledgment of uncertainty — evidence-oriented framing throughout.
:::

## The Critical Reader's Golden Rule for Tone

::: warning title="Never infer attitude from a single word in isolation"
*Surprisingly* can carry either direction: *the results were
surprisingly strong* (positive) vs. *the results were surprisingly
weak* (negative). Always move outward: context → word choice → sentence
→ paragraph → overall position — never judge tone from one word alone.
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read this passage once for its overall claim, then map its tone
sentence by sentence before checking the analysis below.

> Port operators have shown growing interest in predictive-maintenance
> software, and several vendors describe their products as capable of
> eliminating unplanned equipment failures altogether. Early adopters
> report meaningful reductions in downtime, which lends some
> credibility to these claims. It would, however, be a mistake to treat
> vendor marketing as independent evidence. Most published results come
> from vendors themselves or from case studies they commissioned, and
> independent, long-term evaluations remain scarce. Ports considering
> this technology would be wise to seek evidence from sources with no
> financial stake in the outcome before making major investment
> decisions.

**Sentence 1** — neutral/descriptive, though *"eliminating…
altogether"* flags vendor rhetoric as unusually strong. **Sentence 2**
— *"lends some credibility"* is a cautious, qualified positive.
**Sentence 3** — *"it would… be a mistake"* signals a clear tone shift
toward skepticism. **Sentence 4** — the reason for that skepticism:
selection bias in the evidence base (vendor-commissioned studies).
**Sentence 5** — a recommendation, closing on a cautious, analytical
tone rather than outright rejection. Overall position: **qualified
skepticism** — not *"predictive maintenance doesn't work,"* but *"the
current evidence base doesn't yet support the strongest claims being
made about it."*

## Golden Rule

::: golden
Read the author's words, but also read the author's choices — tone, framing, and what's been left out reveal as much as any single sentence states directly.
:::

## Lesson Summary

Today's lesson trained you to detect an author's attitude from word
choice, sentence structure, and evidence selection — and, just as
importantly, to keep tone, certainty, and evidence as three separate
judgments rather than collapsing them into one impression. You
practised naming specific tones (skeptical, critical, cautious,
enthusiastic, dismissive) by their actual signal words, and detecting
bias through omission, selection, and framing rather than only through
outright false statements. You also learned to read authorial distance
in reporting verbs, and to recognise that objectivity is a direction a
text can move toward, never a status any text fully achieves.

::: important title="Ten questions worth running on any persuasive text"
What is the author's purpose? Position? Tone? Which words reveal that
tone? How is the issue framed? What evidence is emphasized? What
evidence or perspective might be missing? How certain is the author?
Is the presentation balanced? Does the rhetorical force match the
evidence? That sequence is this entire lesson, compressed into a habit.
:::
