## Introduction

Lesson 76 asked what a text shows and hides. Today's lesson — the
sixth part of this course's argument-analysis arc — asks a related but
distinct question: within what a text *does* say, which parts are
observation, which are interpretation, and which are pure judgment?
Without this distinction, a reader easily absorbs an author's
interpretation as if it were a plain fact — one of the most common,
and most consequential, misreadings in advanced English.

## Fact and Interpretation Are Not the Same

::: important title="Three sentences, three different certainty levels"
*The automated system reduced processing time by 28%* is a measurable
claim. *The automated system significantly improved operational
efficiency* leans toward interpretation and evaluation. *The company
made the right decision by adopting the system* is evaluation, close
to opinion. All three can appear in the same paragraph — but their
certainty, and what evidence would be needed to support each one, is
not remotely the same.
:::

## Observation

::: concept title="What was directly seen, recorded, or measured"
*System logs recorded a 28% reduction in average processing time.*
This is observation — raw data. What that data actually *means* is a
separate, later step.
:::

## Interpretation

::: concept title="Explaining what an observation means"
Observation: *processing time decreased by 28%.* Interpretation: *the
system appears to have improved processing efficiency.* The author is
now assigning meaning to the raw observation — a genuinely different
act from reporting the observation itself.
:::

## Inference

::: concept title="Connecting back to Lesson 72's inference bridge"
*Processing time decreased by 28%, while the workflow remained
otherwise unchanged. This suggests that automation contributed to the
improvement.* Evidence → reasoning → inference: a conclusion built by
reasoning from available evidence, not observed directly.
:::

## Evaluation

::: important title="Connecting back to Lesson 65 — a judgment that always needs a criterion"
*The system is efficient. The approach is practical. The solution is
preferable. The implementation was successful.* None of these should
be accepted as complete meanings on their own. Ask, every time:
efficient according to which criterion? Successful according to what
measure?
:::

## Opinion

::: concept title="Personal judgment or preference — not automatically unsupported"
*I believe the company should continue using the system.* A personal
stance, stated explicitly. Opinion is not automatically unsupported —
an expert's opinion can be genuinely evidence-informed — but **opinion
≠ automatically fact**, regardless of how confidently it's stated.
:::

## A Six-Level Reading Model

::: framework title="From raw observation to a fully interpretive recommendation"
- Observation
- Data / evidence
- Interpretation
- Inference
- Evaluation
- Recommendation / opinion
:::

::: example title="The same underlying event, moving through all six levels"
*Processing time fell by 28%.* → *Automation appears to have improved
workflow speed.* → *The system may improve operational efficiency.* →
*The system is a promising solution.* → *Management should expand its
use.* Each step becomes progressively more interpretive than the one
before it — and each step, in principle, needs its own supporting
justification, not a free ride from the step before it.
:::

## Why This Matters: The Interpretive Jump

::: important title="A short evidence claim can carry an enormous conclusion on its back"
*The pilot produced a 28% reduction in processing time* is
evidence-oriented. *Therefore, the project was an unequivocal success*
is a very large interpretive jump — success plausibly needs criteria
like cost, reliability, accuracy, maintenance, employee acceptance, and
scalability, none of which the processing-time figure alone
establishes.
:::

## "Shows" Versus "Suggests"

::: note title="Connecting back to Lessons 66, 69, and 71's evidentiary-verb work"
*The results show that automation improves efficiency* makes a strong
interpretive claim. If the data actually shows only that processing
time decreased, the more honest wording is *the results suggest that
automation may improve processing efficiency.* Ask, every time: does
the evidence really "show" this, or merely "suggest" it?
:::

## Fact-Like Language Can Hide Evaluation

::: important title="Connecting back to Lesson 76's loaded language — now inside single sentences"
*The company wisely invested in automation.* "Invested in automation"
is the event. "Wisely" is evaluation, smuggled into the same clause.
*The company irresponsibly ignored employee concerns* does the same
thing more strongly — "ignored," and especially "irresponsibly," carry
real evaluative weight dressed as plain description.
:::

## Separate the Event from the Judgment: A Technique

::: important title="A concrete way to isolate what's actually being claimed"
*Management recklessly replaced the manual system.* Break it apart.
Event: management replaced the manual system. Evaluation: recklessly.
Now ask: what evidence actually supports "recklessly"? This split — event
on one side, judgment on the other — is one of the most immediately
useful habits in this entire lesson.
:::

## Attribution Changes Meaning

::: note title="Connecting back to Lesson 75's attribution tracking"
*Researchers found that automation reduced errors* attributes the
claim to researchers. *The author concludes that automation reduced
errors* attributes it to the author's own reasoning. *Critics argue
that automation reduced errors only because workloads temporarily
declined* introduces a competing interpretation. Always track: who is
actually saying what?
:::

## "According to" Is a Signal

::: note title="Connecting back to Lesson 75"
*According to the company, the new platform improved efficiency.* The
author is relaying a claim, not necessarily endorsing it. "According to
X" means X's claim — not automatically the author's own established
fact.
:::

## "It Is Clear That…"

::: important title="A phrase that asserts certainty without supplying evidence"
*It is clear that automation is the future of logistics.* Ask: clear
based on what — adoption trends, investment, research, performance
data? "Clear" is not itself evidence; it's a claim about how confident
the author is.
:::

## "Obviously," "Clearly," "Undoubtedly"

::: important title="Certainty language is not evidence"
*Obviously, the system is better. Clearly, automation is necessary.
Undoubtedly, the policy will succeed.* These words are strong
certainty and stance signals — and **certainty language ≠ evidence**.
A confident-sounding sentence and a well-supported one are two entirely
separate things, exactly as Lesson 74's calibration work established.
:::

## Interpretation Can Be Reasonable Without Being Proven

::: important title="A genuinely important middle ground"
*Processing time decreased after automation was introduced.*
Interpretation: *automation probably contributed to the improvement* —
reasonable. *Automation definitely caused the improvement* — a
considerably stronger claim. **Plausible interpretation ≠ established
conclusion** — a reasonable reading is not automatically a proven one.
:::

## Competing Interpretations

::: important title="The same evidence can honestly support more than one reading"
Evidence: *productivity increased after automation.* Interpretation A:
automation improved productivity. Interpretation B: additional staffing
improved productivity. Interpretation C: workload changed.
Interpretation D: management introduced process improvements at the
same time. Critical reading means checking whether the author's
preferred interpretation is genuinely the only plausible one, or simply
the one they chose to present.
:::

## The Evidence-to-Interpretation Gap

::: note title="Connecting back to Lesson 73's measurement gap"
*Employees reported higher satisfaction after the new system was
introduced. Therefore, the system is objectively easier to use.* Gap:
a satisfaction report → an objective usability claim. Closer to the
evidence: *employee reports suggest that users perceived the new
system as easier to use* — the perception is real evidence for a claim
about perception, not automatically for a claim about objective
usability.
:::

## Evaluation Needs Criteria, Revisited

::: note title="Connecting back to Lessons 65 and 74"
*The new system is better.* Better in terms of speed, cost, accuracy,
security, usability, scalability? An evaluation without a stated
criterion remains incomplete, no matter how confidently it's phrased.
:::

## Recommendation Is Another Step

::: concept title="Each stage needs its own justification, not a free ride from the one before it"
*The system reduced processing time by 28%* — evidence. *The system
appears to improve operational efficiency* — interpretation. *The
system is a promising investment* — evaluation. *The company should
deploy it across all terminals* — recommendation. Evidence requirements
generally increase at each successive stage.
:::

## The "So What?" Test

::: framework title="Four questions that trace a paragraph from event to action"
- What happened?
- What does the author think it means?
- What conclusion does the author draw?
- What does the author want the reader to believe or do?
:::

::: note title="This traces event → meaning → judgment → action"
Running these four questions in order on any dense paragraph reliably
surfaces its full reasoning chain, layer by layer.
:::

## A Full Worked Example

::: example title="Observation, data, self-reported evidence, interpretation, evaluation, and recommendation, identified"
**A port authority introduced automated billing in three terminals.
During the first six months, average processing time fell by 28%,
while employee-reported billing difficulties declined. Management
interpreted these findings as evidence that the system had
substantially improved operational efficiency and recommended
extending the system to all terminals.**

Observation: automated billing was introduced. Data: processing time
fell by 28%. Self-reported evidence: employee-reported difficulties
declined. Interpretation: findings indicate improved operational
efficiency. Evaluation: "substantially improved." Recommendation:
extend to all terminals.
:::

## Where Is the Weakest Link?

::: important title="Connecting back to Lesson 73's generalization gap"
Which part of the example above needs the most additional support?
Very likely the extension to all terminals — the evidence came from
only three terminals over six months. **Evidence scope < recommendation
scope**: a generalization gap sitting right at the argument's final,
highest-stakes step.
:::

## Fact Versus Opinion Is Not Always Binary

::: concept title="Advanced texts move through several intermediate layers, not just two categories"
*The system reduced processing time* (relatively factual) → *this
suggests improved workflow efficiency* (interpretation) → *the system
is highly effective* (evaluation) → *it should be adopted across the
organization* (recommendation) → *this is clearly the right strategic
choice* (strong judgment). Sorting a text into only "fact" and
"opinion" misses everything happening in between.
:::

## A Tagging System for Reading

::: framework title="Ten short tags for marking a paragraph's reasoning layers directly"
- OBS — observation
- DATA — measured evidence
- CLAIM — claim
- INT — interpretation
- INF — inference
- EVAL — evaluation
- OP — opinion
- REC — recommendation
- ATTR — attributed claim
- QUAL — qualification
:::

::: example title="A tagged paragraph"
[DATA] Processing time decreased by 28%. [INT] This suggests improved
workflow efficiency. [EVAL] The system appears highly effective. [REC]
Management should expand its use. Tagging even a short passage this way
makes its logical layers visible almost instantly.
:::

## Why Authors Mix These Layers

::: concept title="Persuasive writing often blends interpretation into fact-shaped sentences"
Good writing generally moves evidence → interpretation → conclusion in
a traceable sequence. Persuasive writing can present interpretation as
though it were fact: *the company wisely adopted automation* packs
event and evaluation into one clause. A critical reader pulls them back
apart.
:::

## The "Remove the Adjective" Test

::: important title="A fast, reliable way to expose evaluative language"
*The company made a remarkably successful investment.* Remove the
adjective: *the company made an investment.* Now ask: what evidence
establishes "remarkably successful"? This test works on any evaluative
word — successful, effective, efficient, irresponsible, innovative,
outdated, excellent, superior.
:::

## The "Replace With Neutral Language" Test

::: important title="A second, complementary technique for the same job"
*The government imposed a harsh regulation.* Neutral version: *the
government introduced a regulation that restricted X.* The comparison
reveals "harsh" as evaluation, while "restricted X" is a more specific,
neutral description. Building a neutral paraphrase is a reliably
useful habit in critical reading.
:::

## Author Position Detection

::: framework title="What is the author actually doing in this passage?"
- Describing
- Explaining
- Interpreting
- Evaluating
- Criticising
- Defending
- Recommending
- Warning
- Predicting
:::

::: note title="The same topic, several possible purposes"
Two passages about the identical system can have entirely different
purposes — one purely descriptive, another building toward a warning or
a recommendation. Naming the purpose explicitly clarifies what kind of
claim the passage is actually making.
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read this passage once, straight through. Then tag each sentence using
this lesson's system (OBS, DATA, INT, EVAL, REC, and so on), apply the
"remove the adjective" test to any evaluative language, and identify
the weakest link, before checking your reading against the notes that
follow.

> A regional airline introduced a new baggage-tracking app across five
> hub airports. Over three months, lost-baggage reports fell by 22%,
> and the operations team wisely credited the app's real-time scanning
> feature. This clearly demonstrates that the app dramatically improved
> baggage handling, and the airline should roll it out network-wide
> immediately.

Tags: [DATA] "lost-baggage reports fell by 22%" — measured evidence.
[ATTR + EVAL] "the operations team wisely credited the app's real-time
scanning feature" — an attributed claim, with "wisely" as an embedded
evaluation of the team's own judgment, not evidence about the app
itself. [INT + EVAL] "this clearly demonstrates that the app
dramatically improved baggage handling" — "clearly demonstrates" and
"dramatically" are certainty and evaluative language layered onto a
single data point. [REC] "the airline should roll it out network-wide
immediately" — recommendation. Remove-the-adjective test: "dramatically
improved" → "improved" — what specifically established the size of the
improvement beyond the 22% figure already given? Weakest link: evidence
comes from five hub airports over three months; the recommendation
claims the entire network, immediately — a generalization and time gap
sitting directly underneath the passage's most confident language.

## Golden Rule

::: golden
Never treat an author's interpretation, evaluation, or recommendation as if it were raw evidence — the most important question for any sentence is: what is observed, what is inferred, and what is merely judged?
:::

## Lesson Summary

This lesson gave you a six-level model — observation, data,
interpretation, inference, evaluation, recommendation — for locating
exactly how interpretive any given sentence actually is. You practised
detecting evaluation hidden inside fact-shaped language using two
concrete techniques: removing the adjective, and building a neutral
paraphrase. You separated "shows" from "suggests," treated certainty
words like "obviously" and "clearly" as stance rather than evidence,
and held "plausible interpretation" and "established conclusion" apart
as two genuinely different claims. You identified competing
interpretations of the same evidence, tagged a full paragraph using a
ten-symbol system, and closed by naming an author's overall purpose —
describing, evaluating, recommending, warning, predicting — rather than
assuming every passage on a topic is doing the same job.

::: important title="Six lessons, one connected discipline"
Lessons 72 through 77 have built one continuous reading capability:
an argument's anatomy, its missing bridges, its evidence sufficiency,
its evidence's actual source and quality, what it shows and hides, and
now, exactly how interpretive each of its sentences actually is.
Together, they replace a single vague reaction — "this seems
convincing" or "this seems weak" — with a precise account of which
specific claim, at which specific layer, is or isn't earning the
confidence it's stated with.
:::

### Part A — The Six Levels

1. **'Average response time dropped from 12 minutes to 7 minutes.'** Tag this sentence's reasoning layer. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">DATA — measured evidence. It reports a directly recorded numeric change with no interpretation, evaluation, or recommendation attached yet.</div></details>
2. **'This drop suggests the new ticketing system helped agents resolve issues faster.'** Tag this sentence's reasoning layer, and explain what distinguishes it from question 1. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">INT — interpretation, carried by the phrase "this suggests." Unlike question 1, which simply reports the measured change, this sentence assigns a possible cause and meaning to that change, an interpretive step layered on top of the raw data.</div></details>
3. **'The company should adopt the ticketing system across every department.'** Tag this sentence's reasoning layer, and identify what additional evidence this layer typically requires. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">REC — recommendation, the most action-oriented and interpretive layer. It typically requires evidence beyond a single benefit, such as data confirming the improvement holds across departments with different needs, along with cost, reliability, and implementation considerations.</div></details>

### Part B — Detecting Hidden Evaluation

4. **'The manager boldly overhauled the entire approval process.'** Apply the "remove the adjective" test, and state what evidence would be needed to justify the removed word. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">Removing the adjective leaves: the manager overhauled the entire approval process, the plain event. Justifying "boldly" would require evidence about the risk involved, the opposition faced, or the courage required in making that decision, none of which the bare event alone establishes.</div></details>
5. **'The startup foolishly ignored early warning signs from customers.'** Separate the event from the judgment, using this lesson's technique. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">Event: the startup did not act on early warning signs from customers. Judgment: "foolishly," an evaluative label characterizing that inaction as unwise, a separate claim that would need its own justification, such as evidence that acting on the signs would clearly have prevented harm.</div></details>
6. **Rewrite 'the committee stubbornly refused to reconsider the policy' as a neutral paraphrase.** <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">Neutral paraphrase: the committee did not reconsider the policy after being asked to. This removes "stubbornly," which frames the decision as an unreasonable character trait rather than simply reporting what happened.</div></details>

### Part C — Shows vs. Suggests, and Certainty Language

7. **'The data show that remote work always increases productivity.'** Explain why "show" combined with "always" makes this a particularly strong claim, and what would justify toning it down. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">"Show" claims the data directly demonstrates the conclusion, and "always" claims a universal, exceptionless pattern, together making an extremely strong and easily falsifiable claim. Toning it down would be justified if the data only reflects some contexts or a general tendency, in which case wording such as "suggest that remote work often increases productivity" would more honestly match the evidence.</div></details>
8. **'Clearly, this is the best option available.'** Explain what is missing from this sentence despite its confident tone. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">Missing: any stated criterion for "best," such as cost, speed, or quality, and any actual evidence supporting the comparison to the other options. "Clearly" signals the author's certainty, not proof, so the sentence sounds confident while supplying no verifiable support for its claim.</div></details>
9. **Explain, in your own words, why 'plausible interpretation' and 'established conclusion' are not the same claim.** <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">A plausible interpretation is merely a reasonable reading consistent with the evidence, possibly one of several such readings. An established conclusion has been confirmed with enough evidence to rule out competing explanations, so treating a plausible interpretation as an established conclusion skips the additional verification that closing that gap actually requires.</div></details>

### Part D — Competing Interpretations and Weakest Links

10. **Sales increased the same month a new pricing model launched and a major competitor raised its prices. A report credits the pricing model alone.** Identify at least one competing interpretation the report ignores. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">A competing interpretation is that the competitor's price increase, not the new pricing model, drew customers toward this company and drove the sales increase, a plausible alternative explanation the report ignores entirely.</div></details>
11. **A three-week trial in one department found improved morale after a new benefits policy. The company announces the policy will be rolled out company-wide.** Identify the weakest link in this argument, using this lesson's terms. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">The weakest link is the recommendation stage: the evidence's scope, one department over three weeks, is far narrower than the recommendation's scope, the whole company, a clear case of evidence scope falling short of recommendation scope.</div></details>
12. **Explain why tagging a paragraph's sentences by reasoning layer is more useful than simply labeling the whole paragraph 'persuasive' or 'objective.'** <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">A single paragraph often blends several reasoning layers, observation, interpretation, evaluation, and recommendation, each with its own evidentiary requirement, so one blanket label hides exactly where the reasoning shifts from solid data to looser judgment. Tagging sentence by sentence lets a reader pinpoint precisely which specific claim is well-supported and which is not, rather than accepting or rejecting the whole paragraph at once.</div></details>
