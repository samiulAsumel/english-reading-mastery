## Introduction

Every argument you've studied so far has an evidence side and a
conclusion side. Today's lesson asks about the space between them: what
is the author quietly assuming has to be true for the evidence to
actually reach the conclusion? That invisible bridge is called an
**assumption**, or **hidden premise** — and learning to find it is what
separates a reader who accepts an argument because it sounds confident
from a reader who can tell exactly how far the evidence really carries
it.

## What Is an Assumption?

::: concept title="The unstated idea an argument depends on"
**Assumption** = something the author hasn't said directly, but has to
treat as true for the argument to work.

**Evidence:** *Automation reduced processing time.* **Conclusion:**
*Automation improved overall operational efficiency.*

Does faster processing necessarily mean greater *overall* efficiency?
Not necessarily — automation might have sped things up while also
increasing errors, maintenance costs, or training costs. The hidden
assumption here: *reduced processing time is a valid indicator of
improved overall operational efficiency, and no major offsetting costs
or problems occurred.* Spotting exactly that gap is what this whole
lesson trains you to do.
:::

## Premise vs. Conclusion

::: important title="What the argument stands on, and where it's trying to go"
**Premise** — the information the argument is built on. **Conclusion**
— the final point the author wants to establish.

**Employees using AI completed tasks 30% faster. Therefore, AI
increases employee productivity.**

Premise/evidence: *employees completed tasks 30% faster.* Conclusion:
*AI increases employee productivity.* Between these two sits a bridge
that hasn't been stated yet.
:::

## Hidden Premise

::: concept title="The invisible bridge, made visible"
Continuing the AI example, the hidden premise might be: *faster task
completion reflects higher productivity.* But further assumptions may
also be hiding underneath: *task quality did not decline*; *the AI
users and non-users performed comparable tasks*; *the difference was
caused by AI rather than by differences between the employees.*

**EVIDENCE → [hidden assumption] → intermediate conclusion → [hidden
assumption] → main conclusion.** A critical reader's job is finding
every hidden bridge in that chain, not just the first one.
:::

## Assumption vs. Inference

::: important title="What the reader derives, vs. what the argument depends on"
**The company introduced an automated billing system, and billing
errors decreased by 40%.**

A reader can reasonably *infer*: *the automated system may have
contributed to the reduction in errors* — that's an inference, built
cautiously from evidence.

But if the author writes **Therefore, the automated system improved
billing accuracy**, they're likely *assuming*: *the reduction in errors
was caused mainly by the automated system.* That's an assumption — an
unstated idea the argument is quietly resting on.

**Shortcut:** inference = what the reader derives; assumption = what
the argument depends on.
:::

## "What Must Be True?" — The Core Test

::: framework title="The single most powerful question for finding a hidden assumption"
- What is the evidence?
- What is the conclusion?
- What must be true for the evidence to actually support this conclusion?
:::

::: example title="Applying the test to a comparison"
**Port A processes more vessels per day than Port B. Therefore, Port A
is more efficient than Port B.**

Does *more vessels per day* necessarily mean *more efficient*? Not
necessarily — Port A might handle smaller vessels, use more manpower,
carry higher operating costs, or create longer waiting times. Hidden
assumption: *the two ports are being compared under sufficiently
similar conditions, and vessels processed per day is an appropriate
measure of operational efficiency.*
:::

## Necessary vs. Helpful Assumptions

::: important title="Not every assumption carries the same weight"
**Necessary assumption** — the argument cannot survive without it; if
it's false, the conclusion is seriously weakened. **The new software
reduced billing errors. Therefore, it improved billing accuracy.**
Necessary assumption: *the observed reduction in errors is relevant to
measuring billing accuracy.*

**Helpful assumption** — strengthens the argument, but the argument
doesn't collapse without it. *Employees found the new software easy to
use* is useful supporting context, but not strictly necessary for an
efficiency claim.

**Necessary = the argument needs it. Helpful = the argument benefits
from it.**
:::

## The Negation Test

::: framework title="Checking whether an assumption is genuinely necessary"
- State the possible assumption.
- Imagine its opposite is true instead.
- Ask: does the conclusion survive that negation?
- If the conclusion collapses, the assumption is materially important.
:::

::: example title="Applying the negation test"
Possible assumption: *faster completion reflects higher productivity.*
Negate it: *faster completion does NOT reflect higher productivity,
because quality has substantially declined.* If this negation is true,
the conclusion (*AI increases productivity*) falls apart — so the
original assumption was doing real, load-bearing work in the argument.
:::

## Causal Assumptions

::: warning title="The most common logical gap of all: assuming a cause"
**After the company introduced AI-assisted scheduling, delivery delays
decreased. Therefore, AI-assisted scheduling reduced delivery delays.**

*After* is not *because of* — new drivers might have been hired,
traffic conditions might have improved, delivery volume might have
decreased, or route planning might have changed at the same time.
Hidden causal assumption: *the reduction in delivery delays was caused
primarily by the AI-assisted scheduling system rather than by other
changes occurring at the same time.*
:::

## Correlation Sliding Into Causation

::: important title="X is associated with Y ≠ X causes Y"
**Employees who use AI tools tend to complete tasks faster. Therefore,
AI makes employees more productive.**

Alternative explanation: *more skilled employees may be more likely to
use AI in the first place* — so skill could be driving both AI usage
and faster performance, with AI itself not the sole cause. A critical
reader's honest summary: *the evidence shows an association, but it
does not by itself establish causation.*
:::

## Measurement, Scope, Time, and Comparison Assumptions

::: concept title="Four more places a hidden assumption commonly hides"
**Measurement assumption:** *The software reduced average processing
time by 25%. Therefore, employee productivity increased by 25%.*
Processing time isn't necessarily productivity — output quality, error
rate, complexity, cost, and rework all factor into productivity too.
Hidden assumption: *processing time is an adequate measure of
productivity in this context.*

**Scope/generalization assumption:** *A study examined 100 logistics
employees in one company. Therefore, AI improves productivity among
logistics workers worldwide.* Evidence covers 100 employees in one
company; the conclusion reaches for logistics workers worldwide. Hidden
assumption: *the findings from this particular sample and organization
can be generalized to workers in other organizations and contexts.*

**Time assumption:** *The company was profitable last year. Therefore,
its current strategy is successful.* Market conditions may have
changed. Hidden assumption: *the factors responsible for last year's
profitability remain relevant to current performance.*

**Comparison assumption:** *Port A handles twice as many containers as
Port B. Therefore, Port A performs better.* Port A might simply be
larger, with more cranes and a different cargo mix. Hidden assumption:
*the relevant conditions and performance measures are sufficiently
comparable for the comparison to be meaningful.*
:::

## Value-Judgment Assumptions

::: important title="Some hidden premises aren't factual — they're judgments about what's good"
**The company should automate more operations because automation
reduces labor requirements.**

The author is assuming: *reducing labor requirements is desirable* —
which isn't automatically true. An organization might have other goals:
employment, service quality, resilience, employee development, social
responsibility. A critical reader watches for **value assumptions**
just as carefully as factual ones.
:::

## Ambiguous Words as a Source of Logical Gaps

::: warning title="A vague key term can hide the whole gap"
Words like *effective, efficient, successful, better, improved,
significant, useful, optimal, reliable, high-quality* deserve extra
scrutiny. **The new system is more effective.** — effective *in what
sense*? Cost? Speed? Accuracy? User satisfaction? Reliability?
Security? An argument's conclusion can look solid while actually
resting on an undefined key term.
:::

## Ten Common Logical Gaps

| Pattern | Shape of the gap |
|---|---|
| Missing causal link | X happened → therefore X caused Y |
| Overgeneralization | A few cases → conclusion about everyone |
| False equivalence | Two things share one feature → treated as equivalent |
| Unsupported comparison | A is better than B, but the comparison criterion is unclear |
| Correlation → causation | X associated with Y → X causes Y |
| Anecdote → generalization | One person's experience → treated as generally true |
| Scope shift | One company's evidence → conclusion about an entire industry |
| Time shift | Past evidence → conclusion about the current situation |
| Measurement mismatch | Metric A improved → therefore broader concept B improved |
| False dilemma | Presented as "either A or B" when C, D, and more are also possible |

## "Therefore" Doesn't Guarantee Valid Logic

::: warning title="Therefore reports the author's claimed connection — not a verified one"
**Processing time decreased significantly. Therefore, overall
operational performance improved.**

Seeing *therefore* doesn't mean the logic is automatically correct — it
only shows you what connection the author is *claiming*. Your job is
always: *is this connection actually justified?*
:::

## True Evidence ≠ Supported Conclusion

::: important title="A fact can be real while the conclusion built on it isn't"
**Fact:** *The new system reduced processing time by 20%.* This can be
completely true. **Conclusion:** *The new system improved the entire
organization.* This does not necessarily follow. Evidence can be true
while the conclusion resting on it is insufficiently supported — always
keep these two judgments separate.
:::

## The Principle of Charity

::: concept title="Reconstruct the strongest version before you test it"
Critical reading doesn't mean hunting for the worst possible reading of
an argument. First: **reconstruct the strongest reasonable version of
the argument.** Then: test it.

**Bad critical reading:** *"This argument is wrong because the author
left out a small detail."* **Good critical reading:** *"The argument is
plausible, but its conclusion depends on an unstated assumption about
measurement and alternative explanations."* The second version is what
mature, fair-minded critical reading actually looks like.
:::

## A Full Argument Map

::: example title="Applying every tool from this lesson at once"
**The port introduced an AI-based vehicle-tracking system. After
implementation, average truck waiting time fell by 30%. Management
therefore concluded that the system significantly improved overall
port efficiency.**

**Evidence:** average truck waiting time fell by 30%. **Main claim:**
the system significantly improved overall port efficiency. **Hidden
assumption 1:** reduced truck waiting time is a meaningful indicator of
overall port efficiency. **Hidden assumption 2:** no major negative
effects offset the improvement. **Hidden assumption 3:** the reduction
was caused primarily by the new system. **Alternative explanations:**
traffic volume may have decreased; additional gates may have opened;
staffing may have increased. **Logical gap:** evidence measures one
operational metric, but the conclusion claims improvement in *overall*
efficiency — the conclusion may be too strong for the evidence.

**Better conclusion:** *the system appears to have improved one
important aspect of port operations, although its effect on overall
efficiency requires additional evidence.*
:::

## A Ten-Step Critical Reading Workflow

::: framework title="Working through any difficult argumentative paragraph"
- Find the claim: what is the author trying to establish?
- Find the evidence: what support is actually offered?
- Find the explicit premises: what has the author stated directly?
- Find the bridge: what link is needed to get from evidence to conclusion?
- Write the hidden assumption: "for this conclusion to follow, we must assume that…"
- Test it: does the conclusion weaken if the assumption is false?
- Check for alternative explanations
- Check the scope: has the conclusion outrun the evidence?
- Check the measurement: does the metric actually measure the claimed concept?
- Reach a final judgment: strongly supported, reasonably supported but qualified, or insufficiently supported
:::

## Deep Reading Example

::: example title="A logistics passage, worked through the full workflow"
**A logistics company introduced an AI-based scheduling system across
three distribution centers. Within six months, average delivery delays
fell by 18%. Managers therefore concluded that the system had
substantially improved operational efficiency. Although employee
training was conducted during the same period, management attributed
most of the improvement to the AI system because the largest reduction
occurred at the center that used the system most extensively.**
:::

**Sentence 1** — background/setup. **Sentence 2** — evidence/
observation: an 18% reduction. **Sentence 3** — conclusion: *"18%
reduction → substantially improved operational efficiency"* — where's
the bridge? **Sentence 4** — the author names its own alternative
explanation (training) and offers a dose-response pattern (*more AI
usage → larger improvement*) as supporting reasoning — useful, though
still not conclusive.

**Hidden assumptions here:** delivery delays are an adequate indicator
of operational efficiency; the reduction was not primarily caused by
employee training; other operational changes didn't explain it; the
centers were sufficiently comparable; greater AI usage is meaningfully
associated with the larger improvement.

::: important title="What a strong critical reader actually says"
Not *"the managers are wrong."* Instead: *"The evidence is consistent
with the claim that the AI system improved operational performance, but
it does not by itself establish that the system was the primary cause
of the improvement. The concurrent employee training and other possible
operational changes remain alternative explanations. In addition, the
reduction in delivery delays may not fully capture overall operational
efficiency."* This is what advanced academic reading sounds like — not
a verdict, but a calibrated judgment.
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read this passage once for its overall claim, then find the hidden
assumption bridging its evidence to its conclusion before checking the
notes underneath it.

> A shipping line introduced a new digital documentation platform
> across its regional offices. Within the first quarter, the average
> time to process a bill of lading fell from three days to one day.
> Regional management concluded that the platform had transformed the
> company's competitiveness. However, the same quarter also saw the
> company simplify several customs-clearance procedures and hire
> additional documentation staff at its busiest offices.

**Evidence:** processing time fell from three days to one day.
**Conclusion:** the platform *transformed the company's
competitiveness* — a much broader claim than the evidence directly
supports. **Hidden assumptions:** faster documentation processing is a
meaningful driver of overall competitiveness; the reduction was caused
primarily by the platform rather than by the simplified procedures or
additional staff; a faster process necessarily translates into a
competitive advantage in the market. **Logical gap:** scope shift — one
process metric (documentation time) is being used to support a claim
about the company's entire competitive position.

## Golden Rule

::: golden
Before accepting a conclusion, ask what must be true for the evidence to actually support it — and check whether that unstated assumption can survive being questioned.
:::

## Lesson Summary

Today's lesson trained you to see the part of an argument that never
gets written down: the assumption bridging evidence to conclusion. You
practised the core test — *what must be true for this conclusion to
follow?* — alongside the negation test for checking whether an
assumption is genuinely necessary. You learned to recognise causal,
measurement, scope, time, comparison, and value-judgment assumptions as
distinct categories, and to name ten recurring logical gaps by pattern
rather than by vague unease. Throughout, the principle of charity kept
you honest: reconstruct the argument's strongest form first, then test
it — never attack a weaker version than the one the author actually
made.

::: important title="The habit to carry forward"
Every time a conclusion follows a *"therefore,"* pause and ask: *what
unstated idea does this argument need me to accept for that "therefore"
to actually work?* That question is the whole of today's lesson,
compressed into a habit.
:::
