## Introduction

Lesson 62 trained you to read a passage moving through time — building
a timeline and a separate causal map, and refusing to let mere sequence
pass for causation. Today's lesson applies that same discipline to one
of the most common structures in serious nonfiction: the
problem-solution passage.

This shape appears constantly — technology books, business books,
management writing, policy analysis, history, engineering, software
documentation, port and logistics systems, AI and automation writing,
science, and social-problem writing all return to it again and again.
And exactly as with causation in the last lesson, the trap here is
premature closure: a reader who stops at "the author proposed a
solution" has stopped reading one step too early. The real questions —
did it work, what did it cost, what remained unsolved — usually arrive
later in the paragraph, sometimes hedged so quietly that a fast reader
misses them entirely.

## What Is a Problem-Solution Text?

::: framework title="The full architecture, when a passage runs it completely"
- Problem
- Cause
- Consequence
- Solution
- Implementation
- Result
- Limitation
- Next step
:::

::: note title="Real texts rarely run the full chain in one clean pass"
Advanced writing often loops instead of marching straight through:
problem → partial solution → new problem → revised solution. Treat the
framework above as a checklist of moves an author *might* make, not a
guarantee of the order they'll appear in.
:::

## A Problem Is Not the Same as Its Symptom

::: important title="The visible difficulty is rarely the deepest layer"
*Trucks are waiting for long periods at the port gate.* This is a
symptom — the visible, immediate problem. Dig one layer down and the
cause might be *manual document verification*. Dig further, and the
root cause might be *information is not available before trucks
arrive*.

```
Symptom
Long waiting time
      ↓
Immediate cause
Manual verification
      ↓
Root cause
Poor pre-arrival information
```

A confident reader never stops at the first layer a passage names —
they check whether the author is describing a symptom, an immediate
cause, or a genuine root cause.
:::

## Problem-Signalling Vocabulary

::: framework title="Words that flag an author is about to name a problem"
- problem, challenge, difficulty, issue, obstacle
- limitation, weakness, concern, barrier
- bottleneck, constraint, shortcoming
- failure, gap, inefficiency, risk, threat
:::

## These Words Are Not Synonyms

::: concept title="Seven problem-family words, each with a distinct meaning"
**Problem** — an undesirable situation. **Challenge** — a difficult
task or situation, without necessarily implying failure. **Limitation**
— a restriction or weakness built into a method or system.
**Constraint** — a factor that limits the options actually available.
**Bottleneck** — a point that restricts the flow or capacity of an
entire process. **Risk** — the possibility of future harm, not a
current fact. **Gap** — the difference between a current state and a
desired one. Treating all seven as loose synonyms for "bad thing"
erases distinctions the author almost certainly intended.
:::

## Problem Versus Risk

::: warning title="One names something happening now; the other names something that might happen"
*The system has a reliability problem* names an actual, current issue.
*The system poses a reliability risk* names a potential, future
possibility. Problem → exists now. Risk → may occur. Confusing the two
turns a hedge about the future into a claim about the present, or the
reverse.
:::

## "Challenge" Is Not "Failure"

::: note title="A challenge names difficulty, not defeat"
*Integrating legacy systems with modern platforms remains a major
challenge.* This says integration is difficult — it never says
integration has failed. Reading "challenge" as a quiet admission of
failure overstates what the sentence actually commits to.
:::

## "Constraint" and "Bottleneck"

::: concept title="Two words for how a limiting factor shapes an outcome"
*Limited network capacity constrained system performance* names a
limiting condition acting on an outcome:

```
Network capacity
      ↓
   Constraint
      ↓
System performance
```

*Manual document verification became a bottleneck in the billing
process* names a specific point that slows an entire process's flow:

```
Data entry → Verification → Billing → Invoice
                  ↑
             BOTTLENECK
```

A constraint limits a single factor; a bottleneck slows an entire
downstream flow through one narrow point.
:::

## Root Cause Versus Immediate Cause

::: important title="Keep digging past the first cause a sentence names"
*Invoice generation was delayed because tariff information was
outdated.* The immediate cause is outdated tariff information. But the
root cause may be that the tariff database was never regularly
maintained — and deeper still, that no one held clear responsibility
for maintaining it.

```
Organizational responsibility
          ↓
Poor database maintenance
          ↓
     Outdated tariff
          ↓
Incorrect / slow billing
          ↓
      Invoice delay
```

A passage that names only the immediate cause has not yet told you
*why* that immediate cause exists.
:::

## Signals of Root-Cause Language

::: framework title="Phrases that point past the surface toward a deeper source"
- stems from, arises from, is rooted in
- is driven by, is caused by, originates from
- results from, can be traced to
- underlying cause, root cause, fundamental problem
:::

::: note title="'Can be traced to' names a specific deeper source"
*The delays can be traced to outdated infrastructure.* This phrase
signals that the author is identifying a causal source that sits
deeper than the immediately visible symptom.
:::

## Solution-Signalling Vocabulary

::: framework title="Words that flag an author is about to propose or describe a response"
- solution, approach, strategy, intervention, measure
- response, remedy, reform, initiative
- action, proposal, recommendation, alternative, framework
:::

## Solution Is Not the Same as Recommendation

::: warning title="One has happened; the other is only being urged"
*The system introduced automated validation* names an implemented
**solution**. *The organization should introduce automated validation*
names a **recommendation** — what the author thinks ought to happen,
not something that has happened. Reading a recommendation as though it
described an implemented solution imports an event into the text that
was never actually reported.
:::

## "Address" and "Tackle"

::: note title="Both describe engaging with a problem — neither promises it's gone"
*The new system addresses the problem of duplicate records* means the
system directly works against the problem — but **addresses ≠
completely solves**. *The policy aims to tackle congestion* means
actively dealing with congestion — again, **tackle ≠ guaranteed
elimination**. Both verbs describe engagement with a problem, not proof
that the problem is over.
:::

## A Strength Ladder: Mitigate, Minimize, Prevent, Reduce, Eliminate

::: important title="Five solution-strength verbs — rank them before trusting any of them"
*The new procedure helps mitigate operational risk* — **mitigate**
means reduce severity or impact, not necessarily eliminate:

```
ELIMINATE          MITIGATE
100% → 0%          100% → lower level
```

*The system is designed to minimize errors* — **minimize** means made
as low as practical, not necessarily zero. **Prevent** means stop from
occurring; **reduce** means decrease frequency, size, or severity —
*the system reduces billing errors* does not mean billing errors never
happen. **Eliminate** is the strongest claim of the five — *automation
eliminated repetitive data entry* potentially means the task was
completely removed — but always check the **scope** of "eliminated":
here, only repetitive data entry, not all manual work whatsoever.
:::

## "Improve" Needs a Named Dimension

::: note title="Connecting back to Lesson 61's 'in terms of' — evaluation always needs a dimension"
*The system improved processing efficiency.* "Improve" stays vague
until you ask: improve *what*? Speed? Cost? Quality? Reliability?
Accuracy? An unqualified "improved" is an incomplete claim in exactly
the way an unqualified "better" was in Lesson 60's comparison reading.
:::

## Solution Dimension and Trade-offs

::: concept title="A solution can be good along one dimension and bad along another, in the same sentence"
*Automation increased processing speed but increased implementation
costs.*

```
Speed ↑
 BUT
Cost ↑
```

This is a **trade-off**: one benefit is gained at the expense of
sacrificing something else. Common trade-off pairs worth recognising on
sight: speed vs. accuracy, cost vs. quality, flexibility vs.
standardization, security vs. convenience, automation vs. human
control, and short-term cost vs. long-term benefit.
:::

## Naming a Trade-off: At the Expense Of, At the Cost Of, Comes at a Cost, Trade-off Between

::: important title="Four ways authors flag that a benefit was not free"
*The company increased processing speed at the expense of flexibility*
— speed improved, flexibility suffered. *The system improved accuracy
at the cost of higher processing time* — benefit plus sacrifice, named
in one clause. *Greater automation comes at a cost* signals a sacrifice
is coming without naming it yet — treat this phrase as a cue to predict
forward and expect the next sentence to specify what the cost actually
is. *There is a trade-off between speed and reliability* states the
relationship directly — but remember a trade-off is not always a
perfect inverse relationship; its exact degree still depends on
context.
:::

## Three Separate Questions About Solution Effectiveness

::: framework title="Never collapse these three questions into one"
- Was it implemented?
- Did it work?
- Did it solve the original problem completely?
:::

## "Designed To" and "Intended To" Are Not "Achieved"

::: warning title="Purpose language describes intention, not outcome"
*The system was designed to reduce errors* states intention and
purpose — it does **not** say errors actually decreased. *The policy
was intended to improve access* names the policy's goal, with the
outcome left unknown. *The intervention aimed to reduce congestion*
again names an objective, not a confirmed result. All three phrases —
"designed to," "intended to," "aimed to" — describe purpose. None of
them, by themselves, report what actually happened.
:::

## From Purpose to Evidence: Helped To, Resulted In, Was Effective In

::: note title="A rising ladder from partial contribution to confirmed outcome"
*The new system helped reduce processing time* claims a contribution —
not necessarily the sole cause. *The intervention resulted in shorter
waiting times* is a stronger outcome claim — though it still deserves a
check for the causal evidence actually backing it, exactly as Lesson
62's causal-strength ladder taught. *The intervention was effective in
reducing errors* gives an outcome-based evaluation directly, closer to
a confirmed result than either of the previous two phrases.
:::

## "Successful" Demands a Criterion

::: important title="Never accept 'successful' without asking: by what standard?"
*The project was successful.* An advanced reader immediately asks:
successful according to *what* criterion? Completed on time? Within
budget? Improved performance? Met user requirements? Reduced errors?
"Successful" is, by itself, an incomplete claim — it needs an
operational definition exactly like the abstract concepts from Lesson
61.
:::

::: framework title="A success-evaluation chain to apply to any claimed solution"
- Problem
- Target
- Measure
- Result
- Success?
:::

::: example title="Turning the chain into real evidence"
Goal: reduce waiting time. Measure: average waiting time. Baseline: 45
minutes. Result: 28 minutes. With a named target, measure, baseline,
and result all present, the evidence for "success" here is genuinely
stronger than a bare claim of "the project was successful" would ever
be on its own.
:::

## Before-After Evaluation Still Needs Scrutiny

::: warning title="A before/after number is a start, not a finished causal case — connecting back to Lesson 56 and Lesson 62"
*Waiting time fell from 45 to 28 minutes after automation.* The change
is 17 minutes — but before accepting a causal story built on that
number, check: was there a control or comparison group? What else
changed at the same time? Could seasonality explain part of the drop?
Did staffing or demand shift too? A before-after number, exactly like
the "before-and-after structure" from Lesson 62, is not causal proof by
itself.
:::

## Partial Solutions

::: concept title="A solution can address one part of a problem while leaving another part untouched"
*Digital tracking improved visibility but did not solve transportation
capacity constraints.*

```
Problem
├── Information visibility
└── Transport capacity

Solution
├── Visibility  → improved
└── Capacity    → unchanged
```

Recognising that a "problem" in the passage is actually made of several
distinct parts — and that a solution may only touch some of them — is
one of the most valuable reading patterns in this entire lesson.
:::

## Degree Words for Partial Success: Partly, To Some Extent, Largely, Not Fully

::: note title="Four hedges that cap how complete a claimed improvement really is"
*The intervention was only partly successful* signals a mixed outcome.
*The reform improved efficiency to some extent* signals real but
unspecified, likely modest, improvement — not a complete one. *The
problem was largely resolved* means mostly solved, while leaving room
for some residual problem to remain. *The system automated most
routine tasks but did not fully eliminate manual intervention* draws a
direct line between "mostly automated" and "fully automated" — the two
are not the same claim.
:::

## Unintended Consequences

::: concept title="A solution's side effect can be a genuinely new problem"
*Automation reduced manual data entry but increased dependence on data
quality.*

```
SOLUTION
Automation
 ↓
INTENDED BENEFIT
Less manual work
 ↓
UNINTENDED CONSEQUENCE
Greater dependence on data quality
```

Watch for this pattern specifically after a passage reports a benefit —
an unintended consequence often arrives in the very next sentence,
introduced quietly rather than flagged loudly.
:::

## "However," "Despite," "Although," and "Nevertheless" After a Solution

::: important title="Four connectors that commonly introduce a solution's limitation or cost"
*The new system improved processing speed. However, it also increased
maintenance requirements* — benefit, then limitation or trade-off, and
often the paragraph's real turning point. *Despite improving processing
speed, the system remained difficult to maintain* states the benefit
and the limitation together, with "despite" conceding the benefit
first. *Although the system reduced errors, it required extensive
staff training* follows the same benefit-then-cost structure directly:

```
BENEFIT
   ↓
ALTHOUGH
   ↓
COST / LIMITATION
```

*The system was expensive to implement. Nevertheless, the organization
adopted it* shows a decision made *despite* an obstacle — the obstacle
didn't disappear, it was simply outweighed.
:::

## A Response Is Not Always a Solution

::: concept title="Organizations can respond to a problem without actually solving it"
A response to a problem might be to ignore it, adapt to it, delay
acting, investigate it, regulate around it, negotiate, compensate for
it, redesign something, replace something, or escalate it upward —
solving it is only one option among many. *In response to the delays,
the organization increased staffing* names a response or intervention,
without yet claiming the delays were resolved.
:::

::: important title="'In response to' names the trigger, not necessarily a successful fix"
*In response to rising demand, the company expanded capacity.*

```
Rising demand
      ↓
    RESPONSE
      ↓
Capacity expansion
```

The relationship here is explicit — but it is a trigger-to-response
relationship, not automatically a problem-to-solved relationship.
:::

## Purpose Phrases: In an Effort To, To Address, In Order To

::: note title="Connecting back to Lesson 62's purpose clauses — 'once' and 'in order to' both mark intention, not result"
*The organization introduced automation in an effort to reduce
processing delays* names intention, with the outcome still uncertain.
*The company redesigned the workflow to address the bottleneck* names a
purpose — addressing the bottleneck — without confirming that purpose
was achieved. *The organization upgraded its servers in order to
improve system reliability* again states purpose; as with "designed to"
and "intended to" earlier in this lesson, purpose is never the same
claim as a guaranteed result.
:::

## Alternative Solutions and Their Evaluation

::: concept title="Authors often weigh one option against another before (or instead of) settling on one"
*The organization could either increase staffing or automate routine
verification.*

```
PROBLEM
   ↓
OPTION A                OPTION B
Increase staffing   OR   Automation
```

*Increasing staffing is easier in the short term, whereas automation
may provide greater long-term scalability* compares the two options
along two explicit dimensions — short-term ease and long-term
scalability — another trade-off, this time between entire strategies
rather than between two properties of a single solution.
:::

## "Rather Than," "Instead," and "Instead Of"

::: important title="Naming the chosen path never proves the rejected one would have failed"
*Rather than expanding staff, the company automated routine checks*
names the chosen alternative — but this does **not** automatically mean
staffing would never have worked; it only means the approaches differ,
and one was selected. *The organization did not replace the system.
Instead, it redesigned the workflow around it* rejects one option
before naming the alternative actually adopted. *Instead of replacing
the software, the company integrated it with the existing platform*
makes the same move within a single sentence:

```
REJECTED / NOT CHOSEN
Replace software
       ↓
     CHOSEN
Integrate software
```
:::

## Constraint-Driven Solutions

::: concept title="Sometimes the solution is explained by what wasn't possible, not by what was best"
*Because the organization could not afford a complete replacement, it
chose to upgrade the existing system.*

```
Financial constraint
       ↓
Replacement impossible / unattractive
       ↓
      Upgrade chosen
```

Understanding *why* a particular solution was chosen sometimes requires
identifying the constraint that ruled other options out, not just
evaluating the chosen solution on its own merits.
:::

## Solution Lifecycle

::: framework title="Advanced technical and business texts rarely treat a solution as a one-time event"
- Problem
- Diagnosis
- Options
- Selection
- Implementation
- Monitoring
- Evaluation
- Adjustment
:::

## Iterative Problem Solving and Feedback Loops

::: concept title="Real-world solutions are rarely final on the first attempt"
Real systems commonly cycle through implement → measure → find
problems → adjust → implement again — an **iterative** improvement
process, not a single fixed fix.

```
Implement
   ↓
 Measure
   ↓
Find problems
   ↓
 Adjust
   ↓
Implement again
```

*After implementation, performance data were reviewed and the workflow
was modified accordingly* names this exact feedback loop directly:

```
Implementation
      ↓
Performance data
      ↓
   Evaluation
      ↓
  Modification
      ↓
New implementation
```
:::

## A Full Problem-Solution Paragraph, Traced

::: example title="Problem, cause, constraint, solution, result, limitation, and conclusion, in one paragraph"
**Manual verification created delays during peak periods. Because
operators had to check each document individually, processing capacity
was limited. The organization therefore introduced automated validation
for standard records. This reduced routine verification time, although
unusual or incomplete records still required manual review. As a
result, the new system addressed the main bottleneck without completely
eliminating human involvement.**

```
PROBLEM
Manual verification → delays
        ↓
CAUSE
Individual checking
        ↓
CONSTRAINT
Limited capacity
        ↓
SOLUTION
Automated validation
        ↓
RESULT
Routine verification faster
        ↓
LIMITATION
Exceptions still manual
        ↓
CONCLUSION
Bottleneck addressed, not human work eliminated
```
:::

## "Addressed" Versus "Solved"

::: warning title="A quiet distinction that changes how strong a claim actually is"
**Addressed the problem** means meaningful action was taken against the
problem. **Solved the problem** means the problem was substantially or
completely resolved. If an author writes "addressed," do not
automatically upgrade that, in your own head, to "solved" — the two
words are not interchangeable.
:::

## A Second Strength Ladder: Overcome, Resolve, Alleviate, Mitigate

::: concept title="Four more solution-outcome verbs, roughly ranked by strength"
*The organization overcame the technical limitations* — stronger than
"addressed"; it means the limitation was successfully dealt with,
though its exact strength still depends on context. *The update
resolved the compatibility problem* often carries the stronger
implication that the problem no longer exists at all. *The reform
alleviated congestion* means congestion's severity was reduced — not
necessarily that congestion disappeared. *The new controls mitigated
security risks* — as earlier in this lesson, risk reduced, not risk
eliminated.
:::

## Compensate For, Adapt To, Accommodate

::: note title="Three verbs describing how a system or organisation responds to a limitation, rather than removing it"
*Additional staff were hired to compensate for limited automation* —
one limitation's effect is offset by a separate action, not removed at
its source. *The organization adapted its workflow to the new system*
— the organisation itself changed in response to the system or context,
which can itself be part of implementing a solution. *The system was
redesigned to accommodate unusual cargo categories* — the system
changed specifically so special cases could be handled, rather than
being excluded.
:::

## Resilience, Robustness, and Reliability

::: concept title="Three technical-sounding words that are frequently, and wrongly, treated as synonyms"
**Reliability** — works consistently. **Robustness** — works despite
disturbance or variation. **Resilience** — recovers or adapts after a
disruption. *The redesigned workflow improved operational resilience*
immediately raises a follow-up question: resilience measured how —
recovery time, downtime, continuity, or failure tolerance? As with
"successful" earlier in this lesson, none of these three words means
much without an operational definition attached.
:::

:::

## Critical Reading Questions for Problem-Solution Text

::: framework title="Ten questions to hold in mind while reading any problem-solution passage"
- Problem — what exactly is wrong?
- Cause — why does it happen?
- Root cause — what lies deeper?
- Solution — what is being proposed or done?
- Mechanism — why should this solution work?
- Evidence — did it actually work?
- Trade-off — what was sacrificed?
- Limitation — what remains unsolved?
- Scope — where does the solution apply?
- Alternative — could another solution work?
:::

## A Full Advanced Example

::: example title="Bottleneck, layered cause, mechanism, result, limitation, and a final qualified judgment"
**During peak periods, manual verification became a major bottleneck in
the port's cargo-processing workflow. The problem was not simply the
number of documents involved; rather, operators had to repeatedly
cross-check information across several systems that were not fully
integrated. To address this problem, the port introduced an automated
validation layer that compared incoming records against predefined
rules before they reached the billing stage. The change substantially
reduced routine verification time. However, the system could not
reliably process incomplete or unusual records, which continued to
require human review. The intervention therefore mitigated the main
bottleneck without eliminating the underlying need for human
judgment.**

Problem: manual verification bottleneck. Deeper cause: disconnected
systems plus repeated cross-checking — not simply document volume, as
the passage explicitly rules out. Solution: automated validation layer.
Mechanism: incoming records → predefined rules → validation → billing.
Result: routine verification time down. Limitation: incomplete or
unusual records. Human role: exception handling. Final judgment:
bottleneck mitigated — the passage's own verb — not eliminated; human
judgment still needed.
:::

## One-Line Compression

::: note title="Connecting back to Lesson 47's paragraph-compression skill"
The entire paragraph above compresses into one sentence without losing
its real claim: *Automation reduced routine verification delays by
integrating rule-based validation, but exceptional cases still required
human judgment.* Notice what survives the compression: the mechanism
(rule-based validation), the result (reduced delays), and the
limitation (exceptional cases, human judgment) — exactly the parts a
careless summary drops first.
:::

**Cause vocabulary:** stem from, arise from, be rooted in, be traced

**Purpose vocabulary:** intended to, designed to, aimed at, in an

**Solution-strength verbs:** address the problem, mitigate the risk,
reduce the impact, minimize errors, prevent failures, eliminate

**Outcome vocabulary:** result in, lead to, contribute to, be effective

**Trade-off and limitation vocabulary:** at the expense of, at the cost

## A Master Problem-Solution Algorithm

::: framework title="The full sequence for reading any problem-solution passage"
- Identify the problem
- Distinguish symptom from problem
- Find the immediate cause
- Find the root cause
- Identify the consequences
- Identify the target
- Identify the proposed or implemented solution
- Identify the mechanism
- Identify the implementation
- Identify the evidence of effect
- Check the baseline and comparison
- Identify the benefits
- Identify the trade-offs
- Identify unintended consequences
- Identify the limitations
- Identify remaining problems
- Identify alternatives
- Identify the next response
- Evaluate overall success
- Compress the argument
:::

::: vocabulary word="robustness" pos="noun" meaning="the ability of a system to keep performing steadily despite variation, errors, or unexpected conditions (দৃঢ়তা)" example="The system's robustness meant it kept working correctly even when input data was inconsistent."
:::

::: vocabulary word="baseline" pos="noun" meaning="the starting measurement against which later results are compared to judge whether a change actually happened (ভিত্তিরেখা)" example="Average waiting time fell from a baseline of 45 minutes to 28 minutes after automation."

## Guided Reading Practice

Read this passage once, straight through. Then identify its symptom,
immediate cause, root cause, solution, trade-off, and remaining
limitation, before checking your reading against the notes that follow.

> Berth allocation at the terminal was frequently delayed, which
> operators initially attributed to a shortage of tugboats. Closer
> review, however, showed that tugboat availability was rarely the
> actual constraint; the deeper issue was that vessel arrival times
> were not shared with the scheduling team until vessels were already
> approaching the port. To address this, the terminal introduced a
> mandatory pre-arrival notification system requiring shipping agents to
> submit estimated arrival times 24 hours in advance. This substantially
> improved scheduling accuracy and reduced idle berth time. However, the
> new requirement was not always followed by smaller operators, and
> berths reserved for non-compliant vessels sometimes sat empty when
> those vessels arrived later than expected. The terminal has since
> begun exploring penalties for late or missing notifications, though
> this measure has not yet been implemented.

Symptom: delayed berth allocation. First (wrong) suspected cause:
tugboat shortage — the passage explicitly rules this out with "rarely
the actual constraint." Root cause: vessel arrival times not shared
early enough. Solution: mandatory pre-arrival notification system.
Mechanism: 24-hour advance notice → improved scheduling accuracy.
Result: idle berth time reduced. Trade-off / limitation: smaller
operators' non-compliance created a new, related problem — empty
reserved berths. Next response: penalties under consideration, but
explicitly "not yet implemented" — an intention, not yet a solution.

## Golden Rule

::: golden
When reading a problem-solution text, never stop at "what is the solution?" — reconstruct the entire chain: what the actual problem is, what causes it, why the proposed solution should work, what actually happened, what trade-offs appeared, what remains unresolved, and why the author considers the solution successful, or not.
:::

## Lesson Summary

This lesson gave you a full architecture for reading problem-solution
writing, the structure underneath technology, business, policy, and
engineering texts alike. You learned to separate a symptom from its
immediate cause and its root cause, and to rank solution-strength verbs
— eliminate, prevent, reduce, minimize, mitigate, alleviate — instead of
treating them as interchangeable. You practised keeping a solution's
stated purpose (designed to, intended to, aimed to) separate from its
actual, evidenced outcome (resulted in, was effective in), and learned
to demand a criterion the moment an author calls something
"successful." You traced trade-offs, unintended consequences, and
partial solutions, and worked through the full evaluation chain — root
cause, mechanism, result, trade-off, limitation, remaining problem —
that turns a bare "a solution was proposed" into a genuinely complete
reading.

::: important title="The habit this lesson is really building"
A problem-solution passage almost never ends where a fast reader stops
reading. The proposed solution is rarely the last word — a trade-off,
an unintended consequence, or a quietly hedged "partly" or "largely" is
often still to come. Reading past the solution, all the way to the
passage's actual, qualified conclusion, is the skill this lesson has
been building throughout.
:::
