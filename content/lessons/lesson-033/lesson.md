## Introduction

Every tool this course has built — evidence, causation, counterfactual
reasoning, hidden assumptions — now converges on one question: *how
good is this argument, exactly?* Today's lesson gives you both the
evaluation framework and the vocabulary of *fallacies* — recurring,
nameable patterns of weak reasoning. But the goal isn't to become a
fallacy-hunter who rejects every argument on sight. It's to evaluate
precisely: to say exactly how far the evidence actually allows a
conclusion to travel.

## Argument Evaluation: Three Questions

::: framework title="Every argument deserves these three separate checks"
- Is the evidence true?
- Is the evidence relevant to the claim?
- Does the evidence support the conclusion strongly enough?
:::

::: important title="True evidence doesn't guarantee a strong conclusion"
An argument's evidence can be entirely factual while its conclusion is
still too broad or too certain for what that evidence actually shows —
the third question is usually where an argument's real weakness hides.
:::

## Argument Strength: Three Tiers

::: concept title="Rate the whole argument, not just its individual sentences"
**Strongly supported** — evidence is substantial, and major
alternatives are reasonably addressed. **Reasonably supported** —
evidence exists, but real uncertainty or limitation remains.
**Insufficiently supported** — the evidence doesn't establish the
conclusion. Reaching one of these three verdicts, explicitly, is the
end goal of every argument evaluation in this lesson.
:::

## Validity vs. Soundness

::: note title="Two related, but different, standards"
**Valid reasoning** — if the premises are true, the conclusion
logically follows. **Sound argument** — valid, *and* its premises are
actually true or well-supported.

**All ports in Country X use System A. Port B is in Country X.
Therefore, Port B uses System A.** If the first premise is true, the
conclusion follows validly. But if that first premise is false, the
argument is valid yet **not sound**.
:::

## What Is a Logical Fallacy?

::: important title="Naming a fallacy is the start of the analysis, not the end"
A **logical fallacy** is a recurring, nameable error or weak pattern in
reasoning. Recognising the name of a fallacy doesn't automatically
prove an argument false — your real job is to **identify the reasoning
problem and explain why it weakens the argument**, not just to shout
*"fallacy!"* and move on.
:::

## Fallacy 1 — Hasty Generalization

::: example title="Too little evidence, too big a conclusion"
**Two employees made mistakes while using the new software. Therefore,
the software is unsuitable for all employees.**

Two cases can't support a universal conclusion. Better: *two employees
experienced difficulties with the software* — a claim that stays
inside the evidence's actual scope.
:::

## Fallacy 2 — Anecdotal Generalization

::: example title="One story, treated as the whole population"
**One truck driver said the automated system made his work harder.
Therefore, automation makes logistics work harder.**

One person's experience — a useful anecdote — cannot by itself establish
a general, population-level claim.
:::

## Fallacy 3 — Correlation → Causation

::: example title="A familiar mistake, now seen as a named fallacy"
**Employees who use AI tend to perform better. Therefore, AI causes
better performance.**

Alternative: more skilled employees may simply be more likely to use
AI, or managers may assign AI to already high-performing teams.
Association alone does not establish causation.
:::

## Fallacy 4 — Post Hoc Reasoning

::: important title="Post hoc ergo propter hoc — 'after this, therefore because of this'"
**The company introduced a new dashboard in March. Profits increased in
April. Therefore, the dashboard increased profits.**

Sales, prices, costs, and demand could each explain the same increase.
This is Lesson 32's *after vs. because* trap, given its formal name.
:::

## Fallacy 5 — False Dilemma

::: example title="Presenting only two options when more exist"
**Either the company adopts AI or it will become uncompetitive.**

Real alternatives might include partial automation, process redesign,
employee training, outsourcing, or better non-AI software. *"Either A
or B"* is rarely a complete map of the real options.
:::

## Fallacy 6 — False Equivalence

::: example title="One shared feature does not make two things the same"
**Both systems use computers, so they provide the same level of
security.**

Relevant dimensions like architecture, access control, encryption, and
monitoring can differ enormously even when a surface feature is shared.
:::

## Fallacy 7 — Straw Man

::: warning title="Attacking a weaker, distorted version of the actual argument"
**Original:** *"AI should be introduced gradually with appropriate
oversight."* **Response:** *"So you want companies to replace every
human employee with AI."*

The response exaggerates the original position into something easier
to attack. Always ask: *did this response actually address the
argument that was made?*
:::

## Fallacy 8 — Appeal to Authority

::: example title="An expert's opinion is not automatic proof"
**A famous business leader says AI always improves productivity.
Therefore, AI always improves productivity.**

Expertise can be relevant supporting evidence, but it isn't automatic
proof. Check: is the expertise relevant? Is there independent evidence?
What does the wider research say?
:::

## Fallacy 9 — Appeal to Popularity

::: example title="What many people believe is not automatically true"
**Most managers believe automation improves efficiency. Therefore,
automation improves efficiency.**

Popular belief isn't evidence on its own — though if public opinion
*is* the actual topic under discussion, it can become genuinely
relevant data. Context matters.
:::

## Fallacy 10 — Ad Hominem

::: example title="Attacking the person instead of the argument"
**The analyst's proposal should be rejected because he has little
management experience.**

The proposal's own evidence is the real question. A person's background
might be relevant context, but it doesn't automatically make their
proposal false.
:::

## Fallacy 11 — Circular Reasoning

::: warning title="Using the conclusion as its own premise"
**The policy is effective because it works well.** Why does it work
well? *Because it is effective.* Structure: *A is true because A is
true* — with no independent evidence anywhere in the loop.
:::

## Fallacy 12 — Slippery Slope

::: example title="A small step, assumed to cascade into an extreme outcome"
**If the company allows employees to use AI for simple tasks,
eventually employees will stop thinking independently, and the
organization will lose all expertise.**

Each link in this chain needs its own evidence. The real question:
*what evidence supports each individual causal step, not just the
scary ending?*
:::

## Fallacy 13 — Appeal to Ignorance

::: important title="Absence of disproof is not proof, and absence of proof is not disproof"
**X hasn't been disproved. Therefore, X is true.** Or: **we don't have
evidence that X is true. Therefore, X is false.** Both moves are
problematic. Remember Lesson 30's rule: *absence of evidence ≠ evidence
of absence* — though how much evidence a claim needs can genuinely vary
by the claim's nature.
:::

## Fallacy 14 — Cherry Picking

::: warning title="Choosing only the evidence that fits, and ignoring the rest"
An author cites one positive study while ignoring five other studies
with mixed results on the same question. The critical reader's
question: *is the cited evidence representative of the full body of
evidence, or has it been hand-picked?*
:::

## Fallacy 15 — Moving the Goalposts

::: example title="Shifting the standard of proof after it's been met"
*"Show me one study."* A study is given. *"One study isn't enough."*
More studies are given. *"They must all be from a particular
country."* The bar keeps moving. This is different from a genuine,
evolving methodological concern — the difference is whether the new
standard is actually reasonable, or just a way to never be satisfied.
:::

## Fallacy 16 — Appeal to Emotion

::: example title="Feeling replaces evidence"
**We must adopt this system because our employees deserve the best
technology.**

*Employees deserve good technology* may be a legitimate value claim,
but whether *this specific system* is actually effective is a separate
factual question with its own evidence requirement.
:::

## Fallacy 17 — Red Herring

::: example title="Changing the subject instead of answering it"
**Question:** did the software improve billing accuracy? **Response:**
the company has always been innovative. This doesn't answer the actual
question. Always check: *is this response relevant to the claim that
was actually made?*
:::

## Fallacy 18 — Overgeneralization From Scope

::: important title="This one deserves special attention"
**Evidence:** 100 workers in one port. **Conclusion:** AI improves
productivity in all ports worldwide. Sample scope and conclusion scope
don't match — this is precisely Lesson 30's scope-matching skill,
looked at again as a formal reasoning error.
:::

## Not Every Weak Argument Is a Named Fallacy

::: important title="Sometimes the honest answer is simply 'insufficient,' with no label needed"
**The company introduced a new dashboard, and employee satisfaction
increased. Therefore, the dashboard caused the increase.**

Rather than forcing a fallacy name onto this, the more precise
statement is: *"The argument makes a causal inference from a
before-and-after association without adequately ruling out alternative
explanations."* This is more accurate, and more useful, than reaching
for a fallacy label that doesn't quite fit.
:::

## The Problem With Fallacy-Hunting

::: warning title="Chasing fallacies everywhere is its own reading mistake"
Treating every argument as a hunt for faults can turn a minor issue
into a major one, cause you to miss the author's strongest
interpretation, and make you unnecessarily skeptical of reasonable
claims. **Better approach: first understand → then reconstruct → then
evaluate.**
:::

## Principle of Charity + Fallacy Detection

::: concept title="Two questions, always in this order"
First: *"What is the strongest reasonable version of this argument?"*
Then: *"Does that strongest version survive scrutiny?"* This protects
you from unfair criticism and keeps your evaluation genuinely honest,
carrying Lesson 31's principle of charity directly into fallacy
detection.
:::

## A Twelve-Point Argument Evaluation Framework

::: framework title="Working through any argumentative paragraph, end to end"
- What is the claim?
- What evidence is offered?
- Is the evidence relevant to the claim?
- Is the evidence sufficient?
- How reliable is the evidence?
- What unstated assumptions does the argument need?
- If it's a causal claim, what alternative explanations exist?
- Does the conclusion stay inside the evidence's scope?
- Is there a counterargument or opposing evidence?
- Does the author acknowledge any uncertainty?
- Does the conclusion reasonably follow from the premises?
- Overall judgment: strong, moderate, weak, or insufficiently supported
:::

## Deep Example: AI Forecasting Tool

::: example title="A full evaluation, worked from claim to final judgment"
**A logistics company introduced an AI-based forecasting tool and
subsequently reduced inventory costs by 15%. Management concluded that
the tool had substantially improved supply-chain efficiency. The
conclusion is supported by the fact that the greatest cost reduction
occurred in warehouses that used the tool most extensively. Critics,
however, note that the company also reduced overall inventory levels
and renegotiated several supplier contracts during the same period.
Management responds that these changes cannot fully explain the
improvement because the warehouses with the highest AI adoption
experienced the largest reductions.**
:::

**Claim:** the AI forecasting tool substantially improved supply-chain
efficiency. **Evidence:** costs decreased by 15%; warehouses with
higher AI adoption saw larger reductions (useful dose-response
evidence). **Alternative explanations:** reduced inventory levels;
renegotiated supplier contracts. **Potential logical problem:**
management still assumes AI adoption is the primary explanation for the
warehouse-to-warehouse *difference* — but were the inventory reductions
and contract renegotiations themselves warehouse-specific? That
information is missing. **Most reasonable conclusion:** *"The evidence
is suggestive of a beneficial effect of the AI tool, but the causal
contribution of the tool is not fully isolated from concurrent
operational changes"* — considerably more precise than *"AI definitely
caused the improvement."*

## Ready-Made Evaluation Language

::: note title="Phrases that let you evaluate precisely instead of vaguely"
**Strong criticism:** *the evidence does not support the conclusion.*
**Moderate criticism:** *the evidence provides limited support for the
conclusion.* **Qualification:** *the evidence is consistent with the
claim, but does not establish it conclusively.* **Alternative
explanation:** *the observed effect may be attributable to other
factors.* **Scope problem:** *the conclusion extends beyond the scope
of the evidence.* **Causal problem:** *the analysis does not establish
a causal relationship.* **Measurement problem:** *the measure used may
not adequately capture the underlying concept.* **Evidence quality:**
*the evidence is suggestive but not definitive.*
:::

## "Wrong" vs. "Not Established"

::: important title="One of the most important distinctions in academic English"
If evidence is insufficient for a claim like *"the evidence proves that
AI caused the improvement,"* you should **not** automatically conclude
*"AI did not cause the improvement."* The precise statement is: *"the
evidence does not establish that AI caused the improvement."* *Not
established* ≠ *disproved* — exactly the same distinction from Lessons
21, 30, and 32, now applied directly to evaluating an argument's final
verdict.
:::

## The Master Logical Reading Map

::: concept title="Lessons 30 through 33, assembled into one framework"
DATA → PATTERN → INTERPRETATION → CLAIM → EVIDENCE → ASSUMPTION →
CAUSAL MECHANISM → COUNTERFACTUAL → ALTERNATIVE EXPLANATIONS → SCOPE →
LOGICAL VALIDITY → COUNTERARGUMENT → QUALIFICATION → FINAL EVALUATION.
This is now your working framework for any serious nonfiction
paragraph.
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read this passage once for its overall claim, then identify any
fallacy pattern at work before checking the analysis underneath it.

> A regional port authority recently claimed that its new automated
> crane system had eliminated the need for further safety investment,
> stating: "Since the system was introduced, we haven't had a single
> reported incident, and no one has proven that it isn't completely
> safe." Critics who raised concerns about the system's long-term
> reliability were dismissed by port management as "people who simply
> don't understand modern technology."

**First claim's fallacy:** *"no one has proven that it isn't completely
safe"* is an appeal to ignorance — absence of disproof is being treated
as proof of safety, when in fact a short incident-free record could
simply mean too little time has passed to observe a rare failure.
**Second move's fallacy:** dismissing critics as *"people who simply
don't understand modern technology"* is an ad hominem — it attacks the
critics rather than engaging with their actual concern about long-term
reliability. A precise evaluation: *the passage does not establish that
the system is safe in the long term; it only reports an absence of
incidents so far, and the dismissal of critics does not address their
underlying concern.*

## Golden Rule

::: golden
Good critical reading is not about finding reasons to reject an argument — it is about determining exactly how far the evidence allows you to go.
:::

## Lesson Summary

Today's lesson gave you both a framework for evaluating any argument's
overall strength and a vocabulary of eighteen named reasoning errors —
from hasty generalization and post hoc reasoning to straw man, cherry
picking, and moving the goalposts. You practised the discipline of
naming a fallacy only when it genuinely fits, and of saying simply
*"insufficiently supported"* when no specific label applies cleanly.
Throughout, the principle of charity kept your evaluation fair:
reconstruct the argument's strongest form first, then test it — and
close with a precisely calibrated verdict, distinguishing *"not
established"* from *"disproved"* every time.

::: important title="The sequence worth automating"
What is the claim? What is the evidence? Is it relevant? Is it
sufficient? What assumptions does it need? Could there be another
explanation? Does the conclusion exceed the evidence? Is there a named
fallacy, or just insufficient support? How strong is the argument
overall? Running any argument through this sequence is today's entire
lesson, compressed into a habit.
:::
