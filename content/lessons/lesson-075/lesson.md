## Introduction

Lesson 74 asked whether an argument's evidence was sufficient for its
conclusion. Today's lesson — the fourth part of this course's
argument-analysis arc — goes one layer deeper still: when evidence for
the same claim comes from several different places, how much should
each piece actually be trusted, and how much does combining them
actually help?

## Why Evidence Quality Matters

::: concept title="Two sentences, both 'evidence,' with very different natures"
**A.** *Three employees said the new system is faster.* **B.** *System
logs show that median processing time decreased by 28% over six
months.* A is self-reported evidence; B is system-generated
quantitative evidence. Simply noting "there is evidence" isn't enough —
the next question always has to be: *what kind* of evidence is it?
:::

## Types of Evidence, by Source and Method

::: framework title="Eight recurring categories, each with its own strengths and limits"
- Anecdote — a single person's story: *one manager reported the system saved several hours a week* — useful, but weak for generalisation
- Personal experience — *employees said they found the interface easier to use*: evidence about experience, not necessarily objective performance
- Case study — a detailed look at one organization or project: useful for mechanism, context, and implementation, but one case ≠ universal evidence
- Observational data — recorded without deliberate intervention: *ports using automation had lower average processing times* — can show association, not easily causation
- Experimental evidence — a deliberate, controlled test: *teams were randomly assigned to automated and manual billing workflows* — supports causal inference more strongly
- Administrative or system data — logs, transaction records, billing records, audit records: often valuable and objective, though data quality and measurement definitions still need checking
- Expert judgment — *industry specialists argue that…*: potentially useful, but expert opinion ≠ empirical proof
- Multiple independent sources — several separate sources converging on the same finding, this lesson's central concept
:::

## Triangulation, Revisited

::: note title="Connecting back to Lesson 74"
Examining the same claim through several independent kinds of evidence
— system logs, audit records, employee reports, cost records — and
finding them point the same direction strengthens a claim
considerably. This lesson takes triangulation further by asking a
sharper question: how do you tell genuinely independent evidence from
evidence that only *looks* independent?
:::

## Independent Evidence: Counting Sources Versus Counting Origins

::: important title="The single most valuable new idea in this lesson"
If four sources all trace back to the same original study, they are
not four independent pieces of evidence — they're one piece of evidence
repeated four times. *Newspaper A reports a study. Newspaper B reports
the same study. Website C repeats Newspaper A.* Three articles, one
underlying finding. **Number of sources ≠ number of independent
evidence sources.** Before counting how many places support a claim,
trace each one back to its actual origin.
:::

## Corroboration, Revisited

::: note title="Connecting back to Lesson 74"
*Employee reports suggest fewer billing errors. Audit records
independently show fewer billing corrections.* The second, genuinely
independent source reinforces the first — corroboration that
meaningfully raises confidence, precisely because it comes from a
different origin, not a repeated one.
:::

## Contradictory Evidence, Revisited

::: note title="Connecting back to Lesson 74's conflicting evidence"
*Automation reduced processing time by 28%. But maintenance costs
increased by 40%.* One metric improved; another worsened — overall
evaluation now needs more criteria than either fact alone supplies.
:::

## Source Quality Is Not Evidence Quality

::: important title="Two separate questions, easy to collapse into one"
A respected newspaper can report a weak study. An obscure technical
document can contain extremely strong primary data. **Source quality**
asks: who produced this information? **Evidence quality** asks: how
strong is the underlying evidence itself? Keep both questions active —
reputation alone answers neither.
:::

## Primary Versus Secondary Sources

::: important title="Getting as close as possible to where the evidence actually originates"
A **primary source** is where original information or data can be
found: an original research paper, official statistics, system
records, an audit report, an original interview, experiment results. A
**secondary source** summarises or analyses someone else's evidence: a
news article, a review article, a textbook, commentary, analysis.
Secondary sources are useful for orientation — but whenever the exact
evidence needs verifying, the reading move is always the same: go
closer to the primary source.
:::

## "According to…" and Whose Claim Is This?

::: important title="A phrase that flags where a claim actually originates"
*According to a recent industry report, automation can reduce
processing costs.* The author is naming their source explicitly — the
industry report, not the author's own independent conclusion. Ask
directly: whose claim is this — the author's, or someone else's being
relayed?
:::

## Attribution Tracking

::: concept title="Connecting back to this course's earlier work on attribution and reported claims"
*Researchers argue that automation improves efficiency, although other
studies have reported mixed results.* "Researchers argue" — a reported
claim. "Other studies" — counterevidence, also reported. The author is
likely taking a balanced position, visible only if you track each
claim back to whoever is actually making it. A reported claim is not
automatically the author's own final belief.
:::

## Reporting Verb Strength

::: important title="How a source's claim gets relayed changes how strong it sounds — track the verb"
**Stronger**: *researchers demonstrated that…* **Moderate**:
*researchers found that…* **Cautious**: *researchers suggested that…*
**Very cautious**: *researchers speculated that…* **Neutral**:
*researchers reported that…* The reporting verb an author chooses
often reveals their own stance toward a cited claim — even when the
underlying source material is identical.
:::

## Evidence Type Must Match Claim Type

::: important title="A common, easy-to-miss mismatch"
Claim: *employees enjoy using the system* — an employee survey is
directly relevant evidence. Claim: *the system reduces processing time*
— employee opinion alone is not enough; processing-time measurements
are needed instead. **Evidence must match the claim being evaluated** —
strong evidence for one kind of claim can be weak or irrelevant
evidence for a different one, even about the same system.
:::

## Measurement-Claim Match

::: note title="Connecting back to Lesson 74's reliability dimension"
*Users reported that the software was reliable* is a subjective
perception. *The system experienced 99.95% uptime* is a quantitative
measure of operational reliability. Both can be useful, but they are
not the same construct — always ask exactly what was measured before
treating two "reliability" claims as interchangeable.
:::

## Proxy Evidence

::: important title="When a concept can't be measured directly, something else stands in for it"
"Operational efficiency" is hard to measure directly, so researchers
often reach for a **proxy**: processing time, cost per transaction,
error rate, throughput. But **proxy ≠ the entire concept** — processing
time falling does not automatically mean overall efficiency has risen;
it means the specific proxy moved, which is a narrower claim.
:::

## The Construct-Validity Intuition

::: important title="One question that captures the core of formal construct validity, without the formal apparatus"
Ask: does this measurement actually represent the concept being
claimed? *Employee happiness* measured through *number of hours
worked* is a questionable proxy — hours worked ≠ happiness. You don't
need full research-methodology training to catch this; you just need
to hold the question in mind every time a passage measures one thing
and claims something broader.
:::

## Data Quality Checklist

::: framework title="Questions worth asking of any data, even system-generated data"
- Is the data complete?
- Are there missing values?
- Are there measurement errors?
- Are there duplicate records?
- Is the definition used consistent throughout?
- Was the same measurement method used throughout?
- Could the data have been manipulated?
- Is there selection bias in how the data was collected?
:::

::: example title="A number that looks better than it is"
*Error rate fell from 8% to 4%.* A strong-looking improvement — unless
reporting rules changed during the same period, in which case the
apparent improvement's interpretation shifts considerably. Even clean,
system-generated numbers deserve this kind of scrutiny.
:::

## Selection Bias

::: important title="Who ended up in the sample can quietly distort the whole picture"
*The company surveyed only employees who volunteered to use the new
system.* Then: *90% of participants liked the system.* Ask: did
employees who disliked the system simply avoid volunteering in the
first place? If so, the sample is biased before a single response was
even collected.
:::

## Survivorship Bias

::: important title="A classic reasoning trap — only counting the survivors"
*The most successful companies all use technology X. Therefore,
technology X causes business success.* This counts only companies that
survived and succeeded. Companies that used technology X and failed
have been silently excluded from the count — precisely the mechanism
behind survivorship bias, and a strong candidate explanation any time a
"look at all these successes" argument appears without mentioning any
failures.
:::

## Publication Bias

::: important title="What gets published is not automatically what actually happened, in full"
A study with an interesting, positive result is more likely to be
published than a study finding *"no meaningful effect."* **Published
evidence ≠ all evidence that exists.** This matters enormously for
advanced research reading — a literature that looks uniformly
supportive of a claim may simply reflect which findings were
interesting enough to publish, not which findings actually occurred.
:::

## Replication

::: note title="Connecting back to Lesson 74's evidence-weight hierarchy"
One positive result doesn't settle a question on its own. When
independent researchers study the same thing again and find a similar
result, confidence rises — this is **replication**, and repeated
findings are generally more persuasive than any single isolated one.
:::

## Conflicting Studies: Why Might an Author Omit Some?

::: important title="Selective citation is worth noticing, not assuming malice about"
Study A: automation improved productivity. Study B: automation had no
significant effect. Study C: automation improved productivity only in
highly standardized environments. If an author cites only Study A, ask:
why were B and C left out? Possible reasons include selective evidence,
genuine context differences, methodological differences, population
differences, or measurement differences — not necessarily deception,
but always worth naming explicitly.
:::

## Evidence Synthesis: Turning Conflict Into a Refined Claim

::: important title="Conflicting evidence doesn't mean 'we know nothing'"
Given Studies A, B, and C above, the productive question isn't "which
study is right?" — it's *why do the findings differ?* Possible
explanation: automation may work under certain conditions but not
others. Refined claim: *automation is beneficial under sufficiently
standardized operating conditions* — considerably more precise, and
more defensible, than either "automation works" or "automation
doesn't work" taken alone.
:::

## An Evidence Ladder, Revisited

::: note title="Connecting back to Lesson 74's evidence-weight hierarchy — the same idea, restated as six levels"
Level 1 — anecdotal ("I experienced this"). Level 2 — single case (one
organization or project). Level 3 — small observational evidence.
Level 4 — larger, more systematic observational evidence. Level 5 —
comparative or controlled evidence. Level 6 — replicated, converging
evidence. Not an absolute ranking, but a reliable default for judging
weight while reading.
:::

## A Complete Worked Example

::: example title="Primary and subjective evidence, a limitation, corroboration, and contradictory evidence, synthesised"
**A port authority introduced an automated billing system in four
terminals. Six months later, system records showed a 28% reduction in
average invoice-processing time. Employee surveys also indicated higher
satisfaction with the billing workflow. However, the participating
terminals had relatively standardized operations, and the study did
not assess long-term maintenance costs. Similar improvements have been
reported in two independent studies, although results from smaller
ports have been mixed.**

Primary evidence: system records, 28% reduction. Subjective evidence:
employee surveys, satisfaction increased. Limitation: standardized
terminals. Missing dimension: long-term maintenance costs.
Corroboration: two independent studies found similar improvements.
Contradictory evidence: smaller ports had mixed results. Refined
interpretation: automation appears promising, especially in
standardized environments, but its broader effectiveness and
long-term cost-effectiveness remain uncertain — synthesis, not
contradiction paralysis.
:::

:::

## A Source-Reading Algorithm

::: framework title="Twelve steps for any research-based passage"
- Who is making the claim?
- What is the source?
- Is it primary or secondary?
- What type of evidence is used?
- What exactly was measured?
- Does the measurement match the claim?
- How large or representative is the sample?
- Are there alternative explanations?
- Is there contradictory evidence?
- Has the finding been independently replicated?
- What are the limitations?
- What conclusion is actually justified?
:::

## Vocabulary in Context

:::

::: vocabulary word="secondary source" pos="noun phrase" meaning="a source that summarises, analyses, or reports on someone else's original evidence, rather than presenting it firsthand (গৌণ উৎস)" example="A textbook chapter on the study is a secondary source; the original published study is the primary one."
:::

::: vocabulary word="selection bias" pos="noun phrase" meaning="a distortion that arises when the group being studied or surveyed is not representative, often because of how participants came to be included (নির্বাচন পক্ষপাত)" example="Surveying only volunteers who chose to use the new system introduced selection bias into the satisfaction results."
:::

::: vocabulary word="survivorship bias" pos="noun phrase" meaning="a reasoning error that draws conclusions only from cases that 'survived' or succeeded, while silently excluding the failures (টিকে থাকার পক্ষপাত)" example="Praising a strategy because all the surviving successful companies used it ignores the failed companies that used it too."
:::

::: vocabulary word="publication bias" pos="noun phrase" meaning="the tendency for studies with interesting or positive results to be published more often than studies finding no meaningful effect (প্রকাশনা পক্ষপাত)" example="Publication bias means a body of published research can look more supportive of a claim than the full body of research actually is."
:::

::: vocabulary word="proxy evidence" pos="noun phrase" meaning="a measurable stand-in used for a concept that is difficult to measure directly, which may not fully capture that concept (প্রক্সি প্রমাণ)" example="Processing time is proxy evidence for 'efficiency' — a narrower measure standing in for a broader concept."
:::

::: vocabulary word="construct validity" pos="noun phrase" meaning="the degree to which a measurement actually represents the concept it claims to measure (গঠন বৈধতা)" example="Measuring 'employee happiness' as hours worked raises a serious construct validity concern."
:::

::: vocabulary word="reporting verb" pos="noun phrase" meaning="the verb an author uses to relay a cited source's claim — such as demonstrated, found, suggested, or speculated — which signals how strongly that claim was made (প্রতিবেদনকারী ক্রিয়া)" example="Switching the reporting verb from 'suggested' to 'demonstrated' would overstate how strongly the original researchers made their claim."
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read this passage once, straight through. Then identify its evidence
types, check whether its cited sources are genuinely independent, and
name any bias risk it raises, before checking your reading against the
notes that follow.

> According to three separate news outlets, a recent industry report
> found that companies adopting predictive maintenance saw a 20% drop
> in equipment downtime. All three articles cited the same report,
> published by a vendor that sells predictive-maintenance software. The
> report's data came from a self-selected group of the vendor's own
> customers who volunteered to share their results. A separate,
> independently funded academic study of a broader set of
> manufacturers found more modest and inconsistent downtime
> improvements, with the largest gains concentrated among companies
> that already had strong existing maintenance data systems.

Evidence type: the "three separate" citations are not three
independent pieces of evidence — all trace back to one vendor report, a
case of source-counting, not origin-counting. Source quality concern:
the report comes from a vendor with a direct commercial interest in a
positive result. Selection bias: the data came from self-selected
volunteer customers, likely already satisfied ones, not a representative
sample of all adopters. Corroboration and conflict together: the
independently funded academic study partly corroborates (some
improvement, gains among certain adopters) and partly conflicts (more
modest, inconsistent overall) with the vendor report — pointing toward
a refined claim: predictive maintenance may help most where strong
existing data systems already exist, not universally.

## Golden Rule

::: golden
Do not ask only "is this source credible?" — ask what exactly this evidence establishes, how reliably it establishes it, and how far the conclusion can legitimately extend, since one piece of evidence can support a claim, but only genuinely independent, converging evidence can substantially strengthen it.
:::

## Lesson Summary

This lesson took the argument-analysis arc from Lessons 72–74 and
extended it to the evidence itself: how to classify it, trace it to its
origin, and weigh it honestly. You worked through evidence types by
source and method — anecdote, case study, observational, experimental,
administrative data, expert judgment — and learned to separate primary
sources from secondary ones. You practised tracing cited sources back
to their actual origin before counting them as independent, and
separated source quality from evidence quality. You tracked reporting
verbs (demonstrated, found, suggested, speculated, reported) as signals
of a cited claim's own strength, checked whether a proxy measurement
actually represents the concept a claim is about, and learned to watch
for selection bias, survivorship bias, and publication bias. You closed
by synthesising conflicting studies into a more precise, conditional
claim, rather than treating disagreement between sources as a dead end.

::: important title="What this four-lesson arc has built, start to finish"
Lesson 72 gave you an argument's anatomy. Lesson 73 gave you a
taxonomy for naming its missing bridges. Lesson 74 gave you a way to
judge whether its evidence was sufficient. This lesson gave you the
tools to evaluate that evidence's actual origin and quality before
trusting it at all. Together, these four lessons replace "the author
cited some evidence" with a precise, defensible account of what that
evidence is, where it came from, how independent it really is, and
exactly how far it can honestly carry a conclusion.
:::

## Practice: Test What You've Learned

Work through every question yourself before checking anything.

::: important title="Before you start"
For every scenario below, name the evidence type, the bias risk (if
any), or the source-independence issue explicitly — "the evidence seems
weak" is not yet a complete answer.
:::

### Part A — Evidence Types and Source Independence

1. **A blog post, a podcast, and a social media thread all cite the same unpublished internal company memo as their source.** Explain why these three citations do not count as three independent pieces of evidence.
2. **A company's own system logs show a drop in support tickets after a product update.** Classify this evidence type, and explain one limitation worth checking even though it's system-generated.
3. **An industry analyst predicts a technology will "revolutionize" a sector, without citing data.** Classify this evidence type, and explain why it should not be treated as empirical proof.

### Part B — Primary/Secondary Sources and Reporting Verbs

4. **A magazine article summarizes a university's clinical trial results.** Identify which is the primary source and which is the secondary source.
5. **Compare: 'Researchers demonstrated that the drug reduces symptoms' versus 'Researchers speculated that the drug may reduce symptoms.'** Explain how the reporting verb changes the claim's apparent strength.
6. **A report says, 'According to the manufacturer, the product is 99% effective.'** Explain why this attribution matters for how the reader should weigh the claim.

### Part C — Proxy Evidence and Bias

7. **A school measures 'teaching quality' using only end-of-year test scores.** Explain why test scores might be a questionable proxy for the broader concept of teaching quality.
8. **A fitness app claims success because its most active, engaged users report high satisfaction, based on an in-app survey.** Identify the bias risk in this evidence.
9. **A business book highlights ten highly successful entrepreneurs who all dropped out of college, concluding that dropping out helps success.** Name the bias this argument commits, and explain what evidence is missing.

### Part D — Conflicting Evidence and Synthesis

10. **Study A finds a training program boosts performance; Study B finds no effect; Study C finds an effect only for new employees.** Propose a refined claim that could account for all three findings.
11. **A widely cited statistic turns out to originate from a single small survey repeated across dozens of articles.** Explain why this weakens, rather than strengthens, the claim's evidential support.
12. **Explain why publication bias means a body of published studies can look more one-sided than the full picture actually is.**

### Part E — Full Passage Analysis

Return to this lesson's guided reading passage about predictive
maintenance.

13. Explain why the "three separate news outlets" do not constitute independent evidence.
14. Identify the selection bias in the vendor report's data collection.
15. Identify how the academic study both corroborates and conflicts with the vendor report.
16. State the refined, conditional claim this lesson's notes propose, and explain why it is more defensible than either "predictive maintenance works" or "predictive maintenance doesn't work."
