## Introduction

Today's goal: given a complex sentence from a real book, quickly tell
which part carries the main idea, which part gives a reason, which part
gives a time, and which part is simply describing a noun. Master this,
and long sentences stop being a wall of words — they become a small set
of clearly labelled relationships.

## What Is a Clause, Again?

::: concept title="A clause is a Subject-Verb relationship"
**People learn.**
Subject: *People* · Verb: *learn* → a clause.

**because people practice**
Subject: *people* · Verb: *practice* → also a clause.

But there is an important difference between these two.
:::

## Independent Clause

A clause that can stand on its own as a complete sentence is an
**independent clause**.

> People learn. · People make mistakes. · Experience matters.

Each of these carries a complete idea on its own.

## Dependent Clause

A clause that has its own subject and verb, but does not give a complete
idea by itself, is a **dependent clause**.

> because people make mistakes

Subject: *people* · Verb: *make* — the grammar is complete, but hearing
*"Because people make mistakes…"* leaves you expecting *"...then
what?"* That's exactly what makes it dependent.

## The Simplest Test You Have

::: important title="Ask: can this stand alone as a complete sentence?"
**People learn.** → Yes. **Independent.**

**because people learn** → No. **Dependent.**

**when people practice** → No. **Dependent.**

**People improve** → Yes. **Independent.**

This one question resolves almost every case you'll meet.
:::

## Subordinating Conjunctions

Many dependent clauses begin with a specific small word. Recognise these
on sight, and you'll spot a dependent clause the instant it starts.

**Reason:** because · since · as
**Time:** when · while · before · after · until
**Condition:** if · unless
**Contrast:** although · though · even though

Whenever you see one of these words, expect a dependent clause to
follow.

## Reason Clauses

::: example title="A dependent clause that answers 'why?'"
**People improve because they practice regularly.**

Main clause: *People improve.*
Dependent (reason) clause: *because they practice regularly.*

This answers the question **Why?** — Because they practice regularly.

**People make mistakes because they lack experience.**

Core: *People make mistakes.* Reason: *because they lack experience.*
:::

## Time Clauses

::: example title="A dependent clause that answers 'when?'"
**People improve when they practice regularly.**

Main clause: *People improve.* Time clause: *when they practice
regularly.* This answers **When?**

**Before people make an important decision, they should consider the
consequences.**

Here the dependent clause comes first: *Before people make an important
decision.* Main clause: *they should consider the consequences.*
:::

## Condition Clauses

A **condition** clause describes what must be true for something else to
happen.

::: example title="A dependent clause that states a condition"
**If you practice regularly, you will improve.**

Condition: *If you practice regularly.* Main: *you will improve.*
Meaning: practising regularly leads to improvement.

**People can improve if they practice consistently.**

Main: *People can improve.* Condition: *if they practice consistently.*
:::

## Contrast Clauses

A **contrast** clause sets up a surprising or opposite relationship with
the main clause.

::: example title="A dependent clause that signals contrast"
**Although the problem is difficult, we can solve it.**

Dependent: *Although the problem is difficult.* Main: *we can solve it.*
Relationship: the problem is difficult — *and yet* — it can be solved.

**Although people have different opinions, they can still communicate
effectively.**

Main: *they can still communicate effectively.* Contrast: *Although
people have different opinions.*
:::

## Relative Clauses, Revisited

You met this briefly in Lesson 2. A **relative clause** gives additional
information about a noun, and usually begins with *who, which, that,* or
*whose.*

::: example title="A relative clause describes a noun"
**People who understand psychology can better understand behavior.**

Main clause: *People can better understand behavior.* Relative clause:
*who understand psychology* — this describes *people*.
:::

::: concept title="The question a relative clause answers"
When you meet a relative clause, ask: **which person?** or **which
thing?**

**The book that I bought yesterday is useful.**

Which book? → *that I bought yesterday.* The clause is describing
*book*.
:::

## Relative Clause vs. Reason Clause

This is where learners commonly get confused, because the two can sound
similar in a quick read.

::: important title="Same-sounding, different jobs"
**Relative:** People who practice regularly improve.
*who practice regularly* → **which** people? It describes the noun
*people*.

**Reason:** People improve because they practice regularly.
*because they practice regularly* → **why** do they improve? It gives a
reason.

The rule: **who** describes a noun. **because** gives a reason. Don't let
the similar vocabulary fool you — check what question the clause is
actually answering.
:::

## Relative Clause vs. Time Clause

::: important title="Another pair worth telling apart"
**People improve when they practice regularly.**
*when they practice regularly* → **when**? This is time information.

**People who practice regularly improve.**
*who practice regularly* → **which** people? This is a relative clause.

The words involved can feel close in meaning, but the sentence's
*function* is completely different — always check what job the clause is
doing, not just which words it contains.
:::

## Noun Clauses — A New Concept

A **noun clause** is a clause that works like a noun inside the larger
sentence — as a subject or an object.

::: concept title="A whole clause, acting like a single noun"
**I know that people can change.**

*I know…* what? → **that people can change.** This entire clause is
acting as the object of *know*.

**What people believe can affect their decisions.**

Here, *What people believe* is the whole subject of the sentence. Main
verb: *can affect.*
:::

::: example title="Testing for a noun clause"
Ask yourself: **what?**

**I believe that practice is important.**

I believe *what*? → *that practice is important.* → a noun clause.
:::

## Clause Types at a Glance

| Clause type | Job | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Independent | Complete idea | People learn. |
| Reason (adverb) | Gives a reason | because people practice |
| Time (adverb) | Gives a time | when people practice |
| Condition (adverb) | Gives a condition | if people practice |
| Contrast (adverb) | Signals contrast | although it is difficult |
| Relative | Describes a noun | who practice regularly |
| Noun | Acts like a noun | that people can change |

::: note title="The name matters less than the job"
Your real goal is never to memorise these seven labels. It's to be able
to ask, of any clause you meet: **what job is this clause doing in the
sentence?** The label follows naturally once you can answer that.
:::

## Decoding a Real, Complex Sentence

::: example title="Worked example — three clauses in one sentence"
**People who understand their own biases can make better decisions
because they are more aware of the factors that influence their
judgment.**

This looks dense at first. Broken apart:

**Main clause:** People can make better decisions.
**Relative clause 1:** *who understand their own biases* — which people?
**Reason clause:** *because they are more aware of the factors* — why
better decisions?
**Relative clause 2:** *that influence their judgment* — which factors?

As a structure:

```
People
├── who understand their own biases
├── can make better decisions
└── because
    └── they are more aware of the factors
        └── that influence their judgment
```
:::

::: example title="A second complex example"
**Although learning a new language can be difficult, people who practice
consistently can develop strong communication skills.**

**Main idea:** People can develop strong communication skills.
**Relative clause:** *who practice consistently* — which people?
**Contrast/background:** *Although learning a new language can be
difficult* — sets up context before the main idea arrives.
:::

## Let Punctuation Help You

A comma often signals where one clause ends and another begins.

::: example title="A comma marking the boundary"
**Although the problem is difficult, we can solve it.**

Before the comma: *Although the problem is difficult* (dependent). After
the comma: *we can solve it* (main).

Compare: **People who practice regularly improve faster.**

No comma here, because *who practice regularly* is tightly attached to
*people* — it's identifying exactly which people, not adding a side
comment.
:::

::: warning title="Don't decide 'is this a clause?' by comma alone"
**People, who are social creatures, often communicate with others.**

Here, *who are social creatures* sits between commas — an additional
relative clause, adding a side comment rather than narrowing down which
people. But plenty of relative clauses (like the example just above)
carry no comma at all.

Never use *"there's a comma, so this must be a separate clause"* as your
rule. Instead, always check directly: is there a Subject + Verb here, and
what job is that Subject-Verb pair doing?
:::

## An Upgraded, Seven-Step Reading Method

::: framework title="Decode any long sentence in seven steps"
- Find the main Subject
- Find the main Verb or verb phrase
- Find the Object or Complement
- Find any relative clause
- Find any reason, time, condition, or contrast clause
- Temporarily set aside prepositional phrases and modifiers
- Find the core meaning, then add the details back
:::

::: example title="Practising the seven steps together"
**People who regularly read difficult books often develop the ability to
understand complex ideas more easily.**

**Main subject:** People
**Relative clause:** who regularly read difficult books
**Main verb:** develop
**Object:** the ability
**Extra:** to understand complex ideas more easily

**Core:** People develop the ability.

Add the pieces back in order:

*People who regularly read difficult books develop the ability… to
understand complex ideas more easily.*
:::

## The Reading Golden Rule

::: golden
Never try to understand a long sentence all at once. Break it, find the core, understand each clause's relationship to that core, then rebuild the full meaning.
:::

This is the central reading skill of the entire Foundation phase — every
lesson so far has been building toward exactly this habit.

:::

::: note title="Watch for these exact combinations in non-fiction"
These words appear constantly in non-fiction writing, almost always
attached to *that*:

*assume that… · suggest that… · indicate that… · evidence suggests
that…*

Once you recognise this shape, you'll immediately know a noun clause is
coming next — the writer is about to tell you exactly what is assumed,
suggested, or indicated.
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read the paragraph once, slowly, without a dictionary.

> Although people often believe that they make decisions rationally,
> research suggests that many factors can influence their judgment.
> People who are aware of these factors may be better able to recognize
> their own biases. When people understand how their emotions and
> experiences affect their decisions, they can make more deliberate
> choices.

**Sentence 1 — Although people often believe that they make decisions
rationally, research suggests that many factors can influence their
judgment.**
Main idea: *Research suggests that many factors can influence
judgment.* Background/contrast: *Although people often believe…* Noun
clause: *that they make decisions rationally.*

**Sentence 2 — People who are aware of these factors may be better able
to recognize their own biases.**
Main: *People may be better able to recognize their own biases.*
Relative clause: *who are aware of these factors.*

**Sentence 3 — When people understand how their emotions and experiences
affect their decisions, they can make more deliberate choices.**
Main: *they can make more deliberate choices.* Time clause: *When people
understand…* Object (noun) clause inside it: *how their emotions and
experiences affect their decisions.*

## Today's Mental Model

So far, the course has built up one continuous chain:

**WORD → PHRASE → CLAUSE → SENTENCE**

And inside a sentence, the pieces sort into two groups:

```
SENTENCE
├── MAIN CLAUSE
│     ├── Subject / Noun Phrase
│     ├── Verb Phrase
│     └── Object / Complement
└── DEPENDENT INFORMATION
      ├── Relative Clause
      ├── Reason Clause
      ├── Time Clause
      ├── Condition Clause
      ├── Contrast Clause
      └── Noun Clause
```

Your reading of real English books should now be noticeably more
structural than it was four lessons ago.

## Lesson Summary

Today you learned to tell an independent clause from a dependent one
using a single test — can it stand alone? — and to recognise reason,
time, condition, and contrast clauses by the subordinating conjunction
that introduces them. You practised telling a relative clause (which
describes a noun) apart from a reason or time clause that can sound
deceptively similar, and met the noun clause, a clause that functions as
a subject or object. You also learned not to trust commas alone as a
signal, and gained a seven-step method for decoding any complex sentence
by combining everything you now know about phrases and clauses.
