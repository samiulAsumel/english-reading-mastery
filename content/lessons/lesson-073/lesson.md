## Introduction

Lesson 72 gave you the core move: evidence → what does it actually
show? → what is inferred? → what assumption connects it to the
conclusion? Today's lesson is a deliberate second pass on that same
territory — going deeper into the hidden bridge between an argument's
evidence and its conclusion, naming the specific *types* of gap that
bridge can be missing, and giving you a concrete test for telling a
genuinely load-bearing assumption from a merely decorative one.

## The Argument Gap, Revisited

::: important title="Connecting back to Lesson 72 — the same gap, a bigger example"
*A six-month pilot reduced billing processing time by 28%. Therefore,
the organization should replace its entire billing system with
automation.* The evidence is real; the conclusion is far larger than
the evidence. Ask: how does a 28% processing-time reduction justify a
full system replacement? Several assumptions could be filling that
gap: processing time is an important decision criterion; the pilot
result will generalize to the entire organization; other costs won't
outweigh the time savings; accuracy won't decline; maintenance costs
stay manageable. The pattern: **evidence → interpretation → hidden
assumption → conclusion.**
:::

## Hidden Assumption, Revisited

::: note title="Connecting back to Lesson 72"
*Employees who receive additional training make fewer operational
errors. Therefore, the company should increase its training budget.*
Hidden assumption: reducing operational errors is important enough to
justify the additional training cost. The author never states this —
but the argument's logic depends on it being true.
:::

## Evidence, Assumption, and Conclusion Are Three Different Things

::: concept title="A quick, precise recap worth keeping sharp"
**Evidence**: what was observed, measured, or documented — *errors
decreased by 20% after training.* **Assumption**: the bridge used to
get from evidence to conclusion — *reducing errors justifies the cost
of additional training.* **Conclusion**: *therefore, the company should
increase its training budget.* Evidence ≠ assumption ≠ conclusion —
three distinct roles, easy to blur together when reading quickly.
:::

## The Missing Bridge

::: important title="Connecting back to Lesson 72's argument gap — restated as a bridge you can name explicitly"
*The new system processes invoices faster. Therefore, it is the better
system.* Evidence: faster processing. Conclusion: the better system.
But better in what respect? Maybe it isn't cheaper, more accurate, more
secure, easier to maintain, or more reliable. The missing bridge:
*processing speed is the most important criterion for evaluating the
systems.* Naming that missing bridge explicitly is the entire skill —
vague discomfort with an argument becomes a precise, statable gap.
:::

## Necessary Versus Helpful Assumption, Revisited

::: note title="Connecting back to Lesson 72"
A **helpful assumption** strengthens an argument without which it
survives; a **necessary assumption** is one the conclusion collapses
without. *Automation reduced processing time by 30%. Therefore,
automation should be adopted.* Of the possible assumptions — processing
time matters; automation is technically interesting; the company wants
faster processing; faster processing outweighs implementation costs —
the first and the last are doing the real load-bearing work; the
others are closer to decoration.
:::

## The "If This Were False" Test

::: important title="A concrete, repeatable technique for finding which assumption actually matters"
Ask directly: **if this assumption were false, would the conclusion
still make sense?** *A software system reduced errors by 25%.
Therefore, the company should adopt it.* Candidate assumption: the cost
of adopting the system is justified by the reduction in errors. Now
test it: suppose the system costs $10 million, while the error
reduction saves only $20,000 a year. The conclusion collapses
immediately. That confirms the assumption is **material** — genuinely
necessary, not merely supportive. Run this test on every suspected
assumption before deciding how much weight it's actually carrying.
:::

## Causal Arguments' Hidden Assumptions, Revisited

::: note title="Connecting back to Lessons 62 and 72"
*After the company introduced automation, productivity increased by
15%. Therefore, automation caused the increase.* Hidden assumptions:
no other major change occurred; workload didn't independently shift;
staffing stayed comparable; the measurement method stayed consistent;
no other factor caused the increase. After X ≠ because of X — the same
sequence-versus-causation gap this course has returned to repeatedly,
here treated as a specific, nameable category of hidden assumption.
:::

## The Generalization Gap

::: important title="Connecting back to Lesson 72's case-study warning — now named as its own gap type"
*Four teams used the new billing system and processing time improved.
Therefore, the system will improve performance across the entire
organization.* Question: are those four teams representative of the
whole organization? Hidden assumption: the four teams are sufficiently
representative of the wider organization. If that assumption is false,
the conclusion's scope has quietly outgrown what the evidence actually
covers.
:::

## The Comparison Gap

::: concept title="Cheaper is not automatically better"
*System A is cheaper than System B. Therefore, System A is better.*
Cheaper according to what overall evaluation? Hidden assumption: cost
matters more than the systems' other differences. But System B might
be more secure, more reliable, more scalable, or easier to maintain —
in which case cheaper ≠ better at all.
:::

## The Evaluation Gap

::: concept title="Connecting back to Lesson 65 — an evaluation word with no stated criterion"
*This solution is highly effective.* Effective for what — speed,
accuracy, cost, reliability, user satisfaction, security? "Effective"
is incomplete without a named criterion. Whenever an evaluation word
like this appears, the reading move is the same one Lesson 65 built:
find the criterion before accepting the claim.
:::

## The Definition Gap

::: important title="Sometimes the problem isn't the evidence — it's an unstated, narrow definition"
*The system is efficient because it processes more transactions per
minute.* The author is implicitly defining efficiency as processing
speed. But efficiency more broadly means output relative to resources
used — if the system is fast but consumes enormous computing resources
to get there, it may not be efficient in that broader sense at all.
Whenever a key term does real work in an argument, ask: what exactly
does the author mean by this term, and is that definition doing more
work than it should?
:::

## The Scope Gap, Revisited

::: note title="Connecting back to Lessons 65, 66, 71, and 72"
*Most employees preferred the new system. Therefore, all employees
support the change.* Most ≠ all. *The system works well under
controlled conditions. Therefore, it will work well in production.*
Controlled conditions ≠ production conditions — the same scope shift
Lesson 72 introduced, here filed under its own named category.
:::

## The Time Gap, Revisited

::: note title="Connecting back to Lessons 62 and 72"
*The system performed well during the first six months. Therefore, it
will remain reliable for many years.* Hidden assumption: short-term
performance reliably predicts long-term reliability — even though
long-term maintenance, software updates, and hardware degradation can
all change that picture substantially.
:::

## The Measurement Gap

::: important title="What was actually measured, and by whom, matters enormously"
*Users reported that the system was faster. Therefore, the system's
actual processing time improved.* Self-reported perception ≠ objective
measurement. The users' impression may well be genuine — but a
perception is not the same evidence as a direct measurement of
processing time. Whenever a claim rests on what someone *reported*,
*felt*, or *said*, ask explicitly: what exactly was measured, and how?
:::

## A Taxonomy of Argument Gaps

::: framework title="Twelve named gap types, each with its own core question"
- Causal gap — did X really cause Y?
- Scope gap — does the evidence cover the conclusion's full scope?
- Comparison gap — better according to which criterion?
- Generalization gap — is the sample representative of the population?
- Measurement gap — does what was measured match the claimed concept?
- Time gap — does short-term evidence support a long-term conclusion?
- Definition gap — is the key term's meaning actually clear?
- Evaluation gap — "better/effective" according to what criterion?
- Alternative-explanation gap — is there another explanation?
- Cost-benefit gap — does the benefit actually outweigh the cost?
- Implementation gap — will the proposed solution actually work in practice?
- Reliability gap — how dependable is the evidence itself?
:::

## A Complete Worked Example

::: example title="A full argument, opened up into its evidence, interpretation, conclusion, and hidden assumptions"
**A port introduced an automated billing system in three terminals.
During the first six months, average invoice-processing time decreased
by 28%, while reported billing errors also declined. Based on these
results, management concluded that the system should be deployed across
all terminals because it would improve overall operational
efficiency.**

Evidence: processing time decreased by 28%; reported billing errors
declined. Interpretation: the automated system appears to improve
billing performance. Conclusion: deploy it across all terminals.
Hidden assumptions: the three terminals are representative of all
terminals; the results will generalize to other terminals; processing
time and billing errors are major components of overall efficiency;
implementation costs are acceptable; other terminals have sufficiently
similar workflows; the improvement will continue after wider
deployment; no major confounding factor caused the improvement.
:::

## Why "Reported Errors" Is Not "Actual Errors"

::: important title="One word changes the entire nature of the evidence"
The passage says **reported** billing errors, not **actual** billing
errors — precisely the measurement gap this lesson opened with.
"Reported errors declined" might simply mean employees reported fewer
errors, which is a different fact from the system's actual error rate
declining. Reported errors ↓ does not automatically mean actual errors
↓.
:::

## Why "Overall Operational Efficiency" Demands Caution

::: important title="Two narrow metrics quietly standing in for one much larger concept"
Processing time and billing errors are two specific metrics. "Overall
operational efficiency" is a considerably larger concept, potentially
including labor cost, infrastructure cost, maintenance, downtime,
training, customer impact, and scalability. "Two metrics improved,
therefore overall efficiency improved" needs a bridge — the inference
quietly expands from two measured dimensions to a much broader, mostly
unmeasured claim.
:::

## An Argument Gap Is Not the Same as a False Argument

::: important title="A distinction worth holding onto permanently"
Finding a gap in an argument does not mean its conclusion is
necessarily **false**. *The evidence is incomplete* means "we cannot
confidently establish the conclusion" — not "the conclusion is
definitely false." The precise, defensible statement in critical
reading is: **the evidence is insufficient to establish the
conclusion** — a genuinely different, more careful claim than declaring
the conclusion wrong.
:::

## Seven Questions for Any Argument

::: framework title="Run these on any serious argument you read"
- What is the claim?
- What supports it?
- What does the evidence actually show?
- What is being inferred?
- What assumption connects them?
- Could something else explain the evidence?
- Is the conclusion stronger than the evidence?
:::

## A Reading Habit to Build

::: important title="Five automatic reactions worth training into instinct"
See "therefore…" → ask *why therefore?* See "because…" → ask *is this
causal explanation actually established?* See "this shows that…" → ask
*does it really show that, or merely suggest it?* See "this is
better…" → ask *better according to which criterion?* See "this
will…" → ask *why is the author so certain about the future?* Building
this habit is what moves reading comprehension from a language skill
into a critical-reading skill.
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read this passage once, straight through. Then name at least three
specific gap types from this lesson's taxonomy, and state the missing
bridge for each, before checking your reading against the notes that
follow.

> A retail chain piloted a new inventory-forecasting algorithm in five
> stores located in the same metropolitan area. Over three months,
> stockouts fell by 22% and employees reported that restocking felt
> less rushed. Encouraged by these results, headquarters recommended
> rolling the algorithm out to all 200 stores nationwide, arguing that
> it would meaningfully improve overall supply chain performance.

Generalization gap: five stores in one metropolitan area may not
represent 200 stores nationwide, with different regional demand
patterns, supplier networks, and store sizes. Measurement gap:
"employees reported that restocking felt less rushed" is a
self-reported impression, not an objective measurement of restocking
efficiency or cost. Definition/scope gap: "overall supply chain
performance" is a far broader concept than "stockouts," potentially
including supplier lead times, transportation cost, warehousing, and
labor — two improved metrics (stockouts, perceived rush) don't
automatically establish the larger claim. Time gap: three months is a
short window to infer sustained, long-term nationwide performance. None
of this proves the algorithm won't work broadly — it means the
recommendation currently claims more certainty than the pilot's
evidence actually earns.

## Golden Rule

::: golden
Never evaluate an argument only by asking whether the evidence is true — ask whether the evidence is sufficient to justify the conclusion, because true evidence does not automatically produce a true conclusion; an inference, an assumption, and a logical bridge can still be missing in between.
:::

## Lesson Summary

This lesson took Lesson 72's argument gap and hidden assumption and
built them into a working system. You practised the "if this were
false" test as a concrete way to separate a necessary assumption from
a merely helpful one, and worked through a named taxonomy of gap
types — causal, scope, comparison, generalization, measurement, time,
definition, evaluation, alternative-explanation, cost-benefit,
implementation, and reliability. You learned to catch the difference
between a reported outcome and an objectively measured one, and to
question when two narrow, measured metrics get silently folded into a
much broader claim like "overall efficiency." You closed on one of
the most important distinctions in all of critical reading: a gap in
an argument means the evidence is insufficient to establish the
conclusion — not that the conclusion is false.

::: important title="What this second pass adds to Lesson 72"
Lesson 72 taught you to find that an argument has a gap. This lesson
taught you to name exactly what *kind* of gap it is, test which
assumptions inside it actually matter, and state the precise,
defensible verdict a real gap deserves — insufficient evidence, not a
false conclusion. That precision is what separates a reader who senses
something is off from one who can say exactly what and why.
:::

### Part A — Naming the Gap

1. **A survey of 50 customers in one city found strong support for a new product feature. The company concluded the feature would succeed nationwide.** Name the gap, and state the missing bridge. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">This is a generalization gap. The missing bridge is the assumption that 50 customers in one city are representative of customers nationwide, even though regional taste, demographics, and competitive conditions could differ substantially elsewhere.</div></details>
2. **Employees said the new dashboard "feels much clearer." The team concluded that decision-making speed had measurably improved.** Name the gap, and explain what kind of evidence would close it. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">This is a measurement gap: a self-reported feeling of clarity is not the same evidence as an objective measurement of decision speed. Closing it would require direct data, such as timestamps showing how long decisions actually took before and after the dashboard was introduced.</div></details>
3. **A tool performed reliably in three weeks of testing. The team concluded it would remain reliable for years.** Name the gap. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">This is a time gap: short-term reliability during a brief testing window does not establish that the tool will remain reliable over years of real use, once wear, updates, and changing conditions come into play.</div></details>

### Part B — The "If This Were False" Test

4. **A company adopts new software because it "looks more modern." Apply the "if this were false" test to the assumption that visual modernity matters for this decision, and explain what happens to the conclusion.** <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">Suppose visual modernity turned out not to matter at all to users, workflow, or business results. Then the conclusion "adopt this software" loses its entire justification, since modern appearance was the only stated reason for adopting it. Because the conclusion collapses once the assumption is false, the assumption is material, not merely decorative.</div></details>
5. **A hospital adopts a new scheduling system because it reduced average wait times in a pilot. Identify one assumption behind this decision, and test whether it is necessary or merely helpful.** <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">The assumption: the wait-time reduction seen in the pilot will generalize to full-scale hospital operations. Testing it as false — imagine the pilot unit's conditions were unusually favorable and full deployment shows no improvement — the conclusion to adopt the system for that reason collapses immediately. That confirms it is a necessary, material assumption, not merely a helpful one.</div></details>
6. **Explain, in your own words, why the "if this were false" test is more useful than simply listing every assumption an argument might depend on.** <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">Simply listing assumptions treats them all as equally important and buries the one or two that actually carry the conclusion's weight among many that are merely supportive. The "if this were false" test filters directly for materiality, letting a reader discard assumptions the conclusion would survive without and focus attention only on the ones whose failure would genuinely destroy it.</div></details>

### Part C — Definition and Measurement Gaps

7. **A company claims its product is "sustainable" because it uses recycled packaging.** Explain what definition gap this claim might involve. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">The company is implicitly defining "sustainable" as narrowly as "uses recycled packaging," while the broader concept of sustainability also covers manufacturing emissions, resource extraction, energy use, and supply-chain practices. Recycled packaging alone doesn't establish sustainability in that fuller sense.</div></details>
8. **A report states that a new tool is "efficient" because it completes tasks in less time, without mentioning resource or cost usage.** Identify the narrow definition being used, and the broader definition it leaves out. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">The narrow definition being used is efficiency as processing speed alone. The broader definition it leaves out is efficiency as output relative to resources consumed — the tool could be fast while using far more computing power, energy, or cost to achieve that speed, which would not be efficient in the fuller sense.</div></details>
9. **A team claims productivity "improved" because employees said they felt more productive.** Identify the measurement gap in this claim. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">The measurement gap is that "employees felt more productive" is a self-reported perception, not an objective measurement of output. Feeling more productive does not necessarily correspond to any actual increase in completed work, so the claim of measured productivity improvement isn't yet supported.</div></details>

### Part D — Comparison, Evaluation, and Insufficient Evidence

10. **Product A costs less than Product B. The buyer concludes Product A is the better choice.** Name the gap, and list two criteria besides cost that might change the conclusion. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">This is a comparison gap: cheaper does not automatically mean better, since the comparison lacks a stated overall criterion. Two other criteria that might change the conclusion are reliability or durability, and after-sales support — Product B could win decisively on either even while costing more.</div></details>
11. **A report concludes "the new process is clearly more effective" without stating what it is more effective at.** Explain what is missing, using this lesson's terms. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">This is an evaluation gap — "more effective" has no stated criterion, so the reader cannot tell whether the claim concerns speed, cost, quality, or accuracy. Until the author names what "effective" is being measured against, the claim cannot actually be verified.</div></details>
12. **Explain the difference between saying 'this argument's conclusion is false' and 'this argument's evidence is insufficient to establish its conclusion,' and why the second is usually the more defensible verdict.** <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">Calling the conclusion false is a positive claim that the opposite is true, and that claim needs its own supporting evidence. Saying the evidence is insufficient makes no claim either way — it only says the current evidence doesn't yet earn the confidence the conclusion asserts. The second is more defensible because it doesn't overreach beyond what a careful reader can actually establish from what's in front of them.</div></details>
