## Introduction

Today's goal: look at *of, in, on, at, for, with, by, from, to,* and
their relatives, and immediately see what relationship each one is
building between the parts of a sentence. Prepositions appear constantly
in real English writing, and translating them word by word causes more
confusion than almost any other part of speech. So today we learn them
as **relationships**, not as isolated words.

## What Is a Preposition?

::: concept title="A preposition connects two things and names their relationship"
**the book on the table**

Connection: *book* ↔ *table*. Relationship: *on*.

**people in society**

Connection: *people* ↔ *society*. Relationship: *in*.

**decisions about money**

Connection: *decisions* ↔ *money*. Relationship: *about*.
:::

## The Most Important Prepositions

Today's lesson covers: *of, in, on, at, for, with, by, from, to, about,
between, among, through.* Every one of these can carry more than one
meaning — so rather than memorising a single Bangla translation for each,
we'll learn the relationship each one typically signals.

## OF — Relationship, Part, or Belonging

::: example title="of connects a noun to another noun it belongs to"
**the opinions of people** — opinions ↔ people
**the behavior of humans**
**the history of science**
**the development of technology**

*of* almost always builds a relationship between one noun and another.
:::

## OF — Part of Something

> a piece of information · a group of people · a number of factors · one
> of the reasons

Here *of* signals a part-of, or quantity, relationship.

## IN — Inside, Context, Area, or State

::: concept title="in reaches well beyond 'physically inside'"
**Basic:** The book is in the room. — physically inside.

**Context:** people in society · behavior in children · changes in
technology — here *in* means *within a context or area*, not literally
inside a container.
:::

## IN — Time Period

> in 2026 · in the past · in the future · in recent years

## ON — Surface, Topic, or Basis

::: example title="on can mean 'about', not just 'on top of'"
**Basic:** The book is on the table. — a surface relationship.

**Topic:** a book on psychology — meaning *a book about psychology*.

**research on human behavior** — research *about* human behavior.
:::

## AT — A Specific Point

> at the door · at the station · at 5 o'clock

A specific point in space or time. But *at* also shows up in a set of
fixed abstract phrases you should simply learn as units: *at risk · at
work · at first · at least.*

## FOR — Purpose, Benefit, or Duration

::: example title="Three different jobs for the same word"
**Purpose:** a tool for learning — what is the tool's purpose? *learning.*

**Benefit:** exercise is good for health.

**Duration:** for three years · for many years —

**Researchers have studied the problem for many years.**
:::

## WITH — Together, Having, or Using

::: example title="with covers three related ideas"
**Together:** People work with others.

**Having:** a person with experience — a person *who has* experience.

**Using/means:** write with a pen.
:::

## BY — Agent, Method, or Deadline

::: example title="by is essential for reading the passive voice"
**Agent** (very common in passive-voice sentences, from Lesson 3): *The
book was written by a psychologist.* — Who wrote it? *a psychologist.*

**Method:** learn by practicing — How? *by practicing.*

**Deadline:** by tomorrow · by 5 p.m.
:::

## FROM — Source or Origin

> information from a reliable source · a letter from a friend · people
> from different cultures

## TO — Direction, Target, or Relationship

::: example title="Beyond simple direction"
**Basic:** go to school — direction.

**Target/connection:** communicate to people · explain something to
someone · a relationship to society.
:::

## ABOUT — Topic

> talk about psychology · think about a problem · a book about
> leadership · research about human behavior

::: note title="about and on can both mark a topic"
In some contexts, *on* sounds more formal or natural than *about*:
*research on human behavior*, *a book on psychology* — both are common
in academic writing.
:::

## BETWEEN — Two or a Small Number of Distinct Entities

> between two people · between theory and practice · the relationship
> between language and thought

Here there are clearly identified, separate entities.

## AMONG — Within a Group

> among people · among students · among different cultures

::: example title="between vs. among"
**between two people** — two clearly separate individuals.

**among many people** — distributed across a larger, less individually
distinguished group.
:::

## THROUGH — Process, Means, or Across

::: example title="through can mean 'by means of'"
**Process:** learn through practice · develop skills through experience
— *experience* is the means by which skill develops.

**Across/inside:** walk through the room — physical movement.
:::

## The Prepositional Phrase

**Preposition + noun phrase = prepositional phrase.**

> **in modern society** — preposition *in* + noun phrase *modern
> society*
> **of experienced managers** · **with several participants** · **through
> regular practice** — all prepositional phrases.

## Reading Prepositional Phrases as Chunks

::: example title="Set the chunk aside, find the core, then reattach it"
**People in modern society face many challenges.**

Core: *People face many challenges.* Extra: *in modern society*
(context).

**Researchers from different countries studied the problem.**

Core: *Researchers studied the problem.* Extra: *from different
countries.*
:::

## A Very Useful Reading Trick

::: important title="Temporarily remove prepositional phrases to find the core"
**The opinions of experienced managers in large organizations can
influence important decisions.**

Remove the prepositional phrases first:

**The opinions can influence important decisions.** — that's the core.

Then reattach them: *of experienced managers* — whose opinions? *in
large organizations* — in what context?
:::

## Multiple Prepositions in One Sentence

::: example title="Several chunks, each doing its own job"
**People in modern society learn from information through many
different sources.**

Core: *People learn.*

Extra: *in modern society* (context) · *from information* (source) ·
*through many different sources* (means).
:::

## Preposition + Verb-*ing*

::: important title="A verb right after a preposition usually takes -ing"
> before making a decision · after reading the book · by practicing
> regularly · without thinking carefully

**People improve by practicing regularly.**

*by + practicing* → the whole phrase *by practicing regularly* answers:
how do they improve?
:::

## Watch Out for "To"

::: warning title="'to' is not always a preposition"
**Infinitive:** want to learn — here *to learn* is an infinitive, part of
the verb form, not a prepositional phrase.

**Preposition:** talk to people — here *to people* is a genuine
prepositional phrase.

Telling these apart takes practice; we'll return to this distinction in
more depth later in the course.
:::

## Common Verb + Preposition Patterns

> depend on · focus on · deal with · learn from · communicate with ·
> contribute to · result in · result from

::: example title="Verb + preposition in real sentences"
Success **depends on** practice.
Researchers **focus on** behavior.
People must **deal with** problems.
People **learn from** experience.
People **communicate with** others.
Experience **contributes to** learning.
Poor decisions can **result in** problems.
Stress can **result from** pressure.
:::

## The Same Verb, a Different Preposition, a Different Relationship

::: important title="Swapping the preposition can reverse the direction of causation"
**result in:** Stress can result in poor decisions.
*Stress → causes → poor decisions.*

**result from:** Poor decisions can result from stress.
*Poor decisions ← caused by ← stress.*

The direction of the relationship has flipped completely — this is
exactly why verb + preposition combinations deserve to be learned as
whole units, not word by word.
:::

## Adjective + Preposition

> interested in · responsible for · aware of · familiar with · similar
> to · different from

::: example title="Fixed adjective + preposition pairs"
People are **interested in** psychology.
Leaders are **responsible for** their decisions.
People should be **aware of** their biases.
The two theories are **similar to** each other.
:::

## Noun + Preposition

> reason for · effect on · relationship between · difference between ·
> influence on · approach to

::: example title="One noun, two relationships at once"
**the effect of stress on behavior**

Two separate relationships packed into one phrase: *effect of stress*
and *effect on behavior*.
:::

## Worked Example 1

::: example title="Two prepositional phrases attached to one head noun"
**The effects of social pressure on human behavior can vary between
different cultures.**

Head noun: *effects*
*of* phrase: *of social pressure* — effects of what?
*on* phrase: *on human behavior* — effects on what?
Main verb: *can vary*
*between* phrase: *between different cultures*

**Core:** The effects can vary. Then the relationships are added back
one at a time.
:::

## Worked Example 2

::: example title="A prepositional phrase inside the subject itself"
**People with strong communication skills can often deal with difficult
situations more effectively.**

Noun phrase: *People with strong communication skills* — head noun:
*People* · prepositional phrase: *with strong communication skills*.

Verb phrase: *can often deal*
Prepositional phrase: *with difficult situations*
Adverb: *more effectively*

**Core:** People can deal with situations. Then the details are added
back.
:::

## Today's Mental Model

| Preposition | Core relationship |
|---|---|
| of | relationship / belonging / part |
| in | inside / context / period |
| on | surface / topic |
| at | a specific point |
| for | purpose / benefit / duration |
| with | together / having / using |
| by | agent / method / deadline |
| from | source / origin |
| to | direction / target |
| about | topic |
| between | relationship between distinct entities |
| among | within a group |
| through | process / means |

These are core meanings — real context can still shift them, so always
check what relationship the phrase is actually building in the sentence
in front of you.

## Vocabulary in Context

Several patterns from today are worth learning as whole collocations,
not single words:

::: note title="Today's collocations, worth memorising as whole units"
*depend on · focus on · deal with · learn from · communicate with ·
contribute to · result in · result from · be aware of · be responsible
for · be interested in · be familiar with · relationship between ·
effect on · reason for*
:::

::: vocabulary word="focus" pos="verb" meaning="to give most of your attention to one particular thing (মনোনিবেশ করা)" example="Researchers focus on behavior rather than on isolated events."
:::

::: vocabulary word="aware" pos="adjective" meaning="having knowledge of a fact or situation (সচেতন)" example="People who are aware of their own biases can make better decisions."
:::

::: vocabulary word="familiar" pos="adjective" meaning="well known to you, or having good knowledge of something (পরিচিত)" example="The two theories are familiar to most researchers in this field."
:::

::: vocabulary word="responsible" pos="adjective" meaning="having a duty to deal with or take care of something (দায়িত্বশীল)" example="Leaders are responsible for the decisions their teams make."
:::

::: vocabulary word="similar" pos="adjective" meaning="resembling something else without being identical (সদৃশ)" example="The two studies produced results that were similar to each other."
:::

::: vocabulary word="reason" pos="noun" meaning="a cause or explanation for something (কারণ)" example="One reason for the disagreement was a lack of clear communication."
:::

::: vocabulary word="effect" pos="noun" meaning="a change that results from something else (প্রভাব/ফলাফল)" example="The effect of stress on decision-making is well documented."
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read the paragraph once, slowly, without a dictionary.

> People learn from different sources of information, including books,
> teachers, personal experience, and other people. The quality of the
> information they receive can have a significant effect on the
> decisions they make. People who are aware of the limitations of their
> information are often better able to deal with uncertainty. Through
> careful analysis and discussion with others, they can develop a more
> complete understanding of complex problems.

**Sentence 1** — *learn from different sources* (source) · *sources of
information* (belonging/relationship).

**Sentence 2** — *effect on decisions* (an effect relationship).

**Sentence 3** — *aware of limitations* (an awareness relationship) ·
*deal with uncertainty* (a verb + preposition pattern).

**Sentence 4** — *Through careful analysis* (method/process) ·
*discussion with others* (together/interaction).

## Today's Reading Principle

::: golden
Don't translate a preposition the moment you see it — ask what relationship it's building between the word before it and the word after it.
:::

**the effect of stress on behavior**

Read it mentally as: *effect ← stress* and *effect → behavior*. Once you
see prepositions as arrows connecting ideas rather than as words with a
fixed Bangla meaning, long academic sentences stop being intimidating.

## Lesson Summary

Today you learned to treat a preposition as a small arrow connecting two
ideas, rather than a single word to translate. You covered the core
relationship behind thirteen common prepositions, practised removing
prepositional phrases to find a sentence's core and reattaching them
afterward, and met fixed verb+preposition, adjective+preposition, and
noun+preposition combinations — including the important warning that
swapping a preposition (*result in* vs. *result from*) can completely
reverse the direction of a relationship.
