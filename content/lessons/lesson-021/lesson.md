## Introduction

Every text so far in this course has rewarded you for reading closely:
finding the subject, tracking a pronoun, spotting a discourse marker.
Today's skill asks for something different. English writing — especially
news, reports, and academic prose — rarely states its full meaning
outright. An author gives you a fact and expects you to work out its
consequence. Your job is to follow that consequence exactly as far as
the evidence allows, and not one step further.

This is **inference**: building a reasonable conclusion from what a text
actually says. Get it right, and you understand not just the words on
the page but the author's reasoning. Get it wrong — by inventing a
conclusion the text never earned — and you've stopped reading and
started guessing.

## Three Levels of Reading a Text

::: concept title="Explicit, implication, and speculation are not the same thing"
Every sentence you read can be sorted into one of three levels.

**Level 1 — Explicit:** what the author states directly.
**Level 2 — Implication:** what reasonably follows from that statement.
**Level 3 — Speculation:** what you assume without enough evidence in
the text to support it.

Your goal as a reader is to travel from Level 1 to Level 2 — and to stop
before you slide into Level 3.
:::

::: example title="Watching the boundary between implication and speculation"
**The company reduced its operating hours because customer demand had
declined significantly.**

Explicit: hours were reduced; demand had declined; the second caused the
first.

Reasonable inference: the company adjusted its operations to match
lower demand.

Unsupported speculation: *the company was about to go bankrupt.* Nothing
in the sentence mentions the company's finances, its debts, or its
survival — bankruptcy is a story you'd be adding, not one the sentence
told you.
:::

## What Makes an Inference "Good"?

::: concept title="Evidence + reasoning = inference"
An inference is not a guess pulled out of thin air. It's evidence,
combined with ordinary reasoning, pointing toward a conclusion the text
didn't spell out.

**The road was wet, and people were carrying umbrellas.** →
It probably rained recently. That's a reasonable inference: two pieces
of evidence, one ordinary explanation that fits both.

**A storm destroyed the town.** That is not an inference from this
sentence — the evidence (wet road, umbrellas) doesn't come close to
supporting a claim that large.
:::

Notice the word *probably* in that inference. It isn't decoration — it's
doing real work.

::: important title="An inference is usually a probability, not a certainty"
**Sarah arrived at work late. Her clothes were completely wet.**

Reasonable inference: *she may have been caught in the rain.* Notice
*may have been* — not *she was definitely caught in the rain.* An
inference built from indirect evidence almost always deserves a hedge,
because the same evidence could, in principle, have another explanation
(a broken sprinkler, a spilled drink, a sudden dip in a pool on the way
to work). "Probably" and "may have" keep your conclusion honest about
how sure the evidence actually makes it.
:::

## Explicit vs. Implicit, and What the Author Chose Not to Say

::: example title="A direct statement and its reasonable extension"
**Explicit:** *The company hired 50 new employees.*

**Implicit:** *The company is expanding, or at least increasing its
workforce.* This is a reasonable extension of the explicit fact.

**Too far:** *The company will become the market leader.* Nothing about
hiring 50 people necessarily leads there — that's a much larger claim
than the evidence supports.
:::

What an author *doesn't* say is just as important as what they do.

::: warning title="Don't let one improvement stand in for a different one"
**The new software reduced processing time by 30%.**

We know: processing time decreased. We can reasonably infer: the
software may have improved efficiency in that specific respect. We
**cannot** say: *the software reduced costs by 30%.* Time reduction is
not the same claim as cost reduction — the sentence never mentioned
money at all. Silently swapping one measurable outcome for another,
similar-sounding one is one of the most common reading mistakes at this
level.
:::

## Respecting the Evidence Boundary

::: important title="An inference should travel no further than its evidence"
**Employees reported greater satisfaction after the new policy was
introduced.**

Reasonable: *the policy may have improved employee satisfaction.*

Not justified: *the policy definitely improved productivity* —
satisfaction and productivity are different things.

Further still, and clearly unjustified: *the policy made the company
more profitable.* Each of these three claims is a separate, larger step
away from what the sentence actually said. A careful reader notices
exactly where the evidence runs out.
:::

## "May" and "Must": Weak Possibility vs. Strong Deduction

::: example title="Matching your hedge to how strong the evidence really is"
**The lights are off, the doors are locked, and no employees are
present.**

A cautious, well-supported inference: *the office may be closed.* If the
surrounding context makes the case unusually strong, a reader might go
further: *the office must be closed.* But notice the jump — *must* is a
much stronger deduction than *may*, and it should only be used when the
evidence leaves very little room for another explanation.
:::

::: important title="'Must' has two different jobs in English"
**Obligation:** *Employees must wear protective equipment.* — this is a
rule; someone is required to comply.

**Deduction:** *The road is completely empty. It must be very late.* —
this is not a rule at all. It's a strong logical conclusion drawn from
evidence.

Context tells you which job *must* is doing in any given sentence — but
in reading comprehension, mistaking a deduction for an obligation (or
the reverse) is a genuine source of confusion, so check which one fits
before you translate it.
:::

## "Could" — One Possibility Among Several

::: example title="Could names a candidate cause, not the confirmed one"
**The system stopped working after a major software update.**

Reasonable: *the update could have caused the problem.* Not justified:
*the update caused the problem* — stated as fact. *Could have* leaves
room for other explanations (a hardware fault, a network issue, human
error) that the sentence hasn't ruled out.
:::

## The Evidence-Language Family: Suggest, Indicate, Imply

Academic and journalistic English has a small family of verbs whose
whole job is to tell you exactly how strong a piece of evidence is —
without you having to guess.

::: concept title="Suggest and indicate: evidence pointing, not evidence proving"
**Suggest:** *The findings suggest that stress affects decision-making.*
Evidence is pointing toward an interpretation — this is weaker than
*prove.*

**Indicate:** *The results indicate that demand is declining.* The
results are producing evidence that points toward declining demand —
again, weaker than *prove.*

Neither verb claims certainty. Both signal: *here is a reasonable
reading of the evidence, not the only conceivable one.*
:::

::: important title="Imply: meaning created without being stated"
**The report repeatedly emphasizes the high cost of maintaining the
system.**

Possible implication: *the author may be questioning whether the system
is economically worthwhile.* But if the report never actually
recommends replacing the system, writing *"the author recommends
replacing the system"* claims more certainty than the text supports —
that would be an overreach, not an implication.
:::

## Imply vs. Infer — Two Sides of One Exchange

::: concept title="The author implies; the reader infers"
These two verbs describe the same event from opposite directions.

**Author implies** → the author communicates something indirectly,
without stating it outright.

**Reader infers** → the reader builds a conclusion from the evidence the
author provided.

In short: *author implies → reader infers.* They are never
interchangeable — an author never "infers" their own meaning, and a
reader never "implies" a conclusion they've reached.
:::

::: example title="Watching the exchange happen in one passage"
**The company has repeatedly delayed payment to its suppliers, reduced
investment, and closed two regional offices.**

The author may be *implying*: the company is experiencing financial
difficulties. The reader can reasonably *infer*: the company may be
under financial pressure. But *"the company will definitely collapse"*
is an unsupported inference — nothing in the sentence supports a claim
that specific and that final.
:::

## "Not Necessarily" Is Not "Never"

::: warning title="A denied absolute is not the opposite absolute"
**Highly educated people do not always make better financial
decisions.**

Justified: *education alone does not guarantee better financial
decisions.*

Not justified: *education has no effect on financial decisions.*

The gap between these two is the gap between *does not always* and
*never.* Denying one extreme does not prove the opposite extreme — it
usually leaves a lot of middle ground standing.
:::

## Quantifiers Change Everything

A small set of words determines exactly how much of something a
sentence is claiming — and swapping one for another, even silently in
your own head while reading, changes the sentence's meaning
substantially.

> all · every · most · many · some · few · often · usually · sometimes ·
> rarely · never · always

::: warning title="The most common inference mistake: upgrading a quantifier"
**Some employees reported difficulties using the new system.**

Valid: *at least some employees had difficulties.* Invalid: *most
employees had difficulties.* More invalid still: *all employees had
difficulties.* Each step from *some* to *most* to *all* is a real jump
in claim size that the original sentence never made — this single
mistake, quietly upgrading a quantifier while paraphrasing a sentence in
your head, is responsible for more misreadings than almost anything else
in this lesson.
:::

::: example title="Three more quantifier pairs worth watching closely"
**Many ≠ most.** *Many people use social media* means *a large number* —
not necessarily *more than half.*

**Often ≠ always.** *People often rely on familiar information* means
*frequently, in many cases* — not *every single time.*

**Generally ≠ universally.** *People generally prefer familiar products*
describes a tendency, not a claim that *every* person prefers familiar
products.
:::

## Correlation Is Not Causation

This is one of the single most important distinctions in advanced
reading, and it shows up constantly in news, research summaries, and
business writing.

::: important title="Two things happening together is not proof that one causes the other"
**People who exercise regularly tend to report better sleep.**

Justified: *regular exercise is associated with better sleep.*

Not justified: *exercise causes better sleep* — stated as settled fact.
Other factors could be doing the real work: diet, age, stress level,
general health, lifestyle. The sentence tells you the two things travel
together; it doesn't tell you which one, if either, is pulling the
other along.
:::

::: concept title="A causal-language ladder, from strongest to weakest"
**X caused Y.** — a direct causal claim, stated as fact.

**X contributed to Y.** — X is one factor among possibly several.

**X may have contributed to Y.** — the same claim, hedged further.

**X is associated with Y.** — no causal claim at all, only a pattern.

Match the strength of your own paraphrase to whichever rung the author
actually used. Downgrading *caused* to *associated with* loses
information; upgrading *associated with* to *caused* invents it.
:::

::: example title="Applying the ladder to a real finding"
**Higher stress levels were associated with lower sleep quality.**

Valid: *people experiencing more stress tended to report poorer sleep.*
Invalid: *stress definitely caused poor sleep* — that overshoots what
*associated with* actually claims.
:::

## Reading Negative Results Carefully

Inference isn't only about building positive conclusions. A text that
reports finding *nothing* still needs careful, exact reading — arguably
more careful, because it's easy to overstate what "nothing" means.

::: example title="No difference found is not the same as no difference exists"
**The study found no significant difference between the two groups.**

Reasonable: *the study did not find evidence of a meaningful
difference.* Not justified: *the two groups are exactly identical* —
"no significant difference" is a statement about what the study
detected, not a guarantee of perfect sameness.
:::

::: important title="'No evidence' is not the same claim as 'evidence of no'"
**Researchers found no evidence that X causes Y.**

This means researchers didn't find evidence *for* causation. It does
**not** necessarily mean *"X definitely does not cause Y"* — the
evidence gathered so far might simply be insufficient to detect a real
effect. Confusing "no evidence for" with "evidence against" is a
distinction that matters enormously in science and policy reporting.

**No evidence was found to support the theory.** Here, *"the theory is
false"* overstates the finding. A more accurate paraphrase: *the
available evidence did not support the theory* — leaving open that
better evidence might, later, tell a different story.
:::

::: example title="Failing to find something is not the same as proving it absent"
**Researchers failed to find a relationship between X and Y.**

Meaning: they were not able to detect a relationship. Not necessarily:
*no relationship exists.* This distinction — between *failing to detect*
and *proving absent* — comes up constantly in scientific and academic
reading, and it's exactly the kind of gap a careless summary tends to
erase.
:::

## Inferring the Author's Attitude

Inference isn't limited to facts. Readers also have to infer an
author's *stance* — what they think about the material they're
presenting, even when they never say so directly.

::: example title="Reading between careful, qualified phrasing"
**The proposal has been described as "ambitious," although its
practical feasibility remains uncertain.**

The author never writes *"I think the proposal is unrealistic."* But
*practical feasibility remains uncertain* signals real caution — the
author is not fully endorsing the proposal's chances.
:::

::: important title="Word choice can carry a skeptical tone"
**The company claims that the new system will completely eliminate
errors. However, the report provides little evidence to support this
claim.**

Two choices reveal the author's tone: *claims* (rather than *shows* or
*proves*) and *little evidence to support this claim.* Together, they
put visible distance between the author and the company's own
statement — the tone here is skeptical, even without a single explicit
"I doubt this."
:::

## Whose Claim Is It? Attribution Matters

Before you can decide how much to trust a claim, you need to know who
is actually making it. English marks this with attribution phrases —
and confusing an attributed claim with the author's own voice is a
common, avoidable misreading.

::: warning title="Don't hand the author someone else's claim"
**The minister argued that the policy would reduce unemployment.**

This claim belongs to *the minister* — the author is reporting it, not
necessarily endorsing it. Reading this as *"the author says the policy
will reduce unemployment"* silently transfers ownership of the claim
from its actual source to the writer reporting it, which changes how
much weight the claim deserves.
:::

::: example title="Three more attribution signals and what they do"
**According to the report, productivity increased by 15%.** The claim's
source is *the report* — not necessarily an independent assertion by
the author of the text you're reading.

**Critics argue that the policy is ineffective.** *Ineffective* is the
critics' position here; the author isn't necessarily agreeing with it.

**Some researchers suggest that the effect may be temporary.** Notice
the uncertainty stacked three layers deep: *some* researchers (not all),
who merely *suggest* (not conclude), that the effect *may* be temporary
(not definitely is). Each layer weakens the overall certainty of the
claim a little further.
:::

## Four Questions Before You Commit to an Inference

::: framework title="Run every paragraph through these four questions"
- What does the author explicitly say?
- What does this logically suggest?
- How certain is that inference?
- What would be going too far?
:::

These four questions, asked in order, are what keep a reader on the
right side of the line between *implication* and *speculation*.

## Choosing the Best-Supported Inference

::: example title="When several interpretations are possible, weigh the evidence"
**The company reduced hiring, delayed expansion plans, and cut its
marketing budget.**

Four possible interpretations:

A. The company is experiencing financial pressure.
B. The company will definitely fail.
C. The CEO is incompetent.
D. Employees dislike the company.

The best-supported interpretation is **A** — every piece of evidence
given (reduced hiring, delayed expansion, cut marketing) is exactly the
kind of cost-control behavior a company under financial pressure
displays. B, C, and D each require information the passage never
provided.
:::

## Context Changes the Inference

::: important title="A sentence read alone can invite the wrong guess"
**He left the meeting early.**

Read alone, this could mean an emergency, illness, a disagreement,
another appointment, or nothing more than a normal schedule — none of
these is more supported than the others without more information.

Add one sentence of context — **He had another meeting scheduled across
town** — and the inference sharpens considerably: *he left because he
had another meeting.* The lesson: never lock in an inference from a
single isolated sentence when the surrounding text hasn't finished
speaking yet.
:::

## Pronouns and Missing Words Carry Hidden Inferences

Two grammatical habits quietly shape what a sentence lets you infer:
where a pronoun points, and what's been left unsaid because English
assumes you'll supply it.

::: example title="Following the pronoun before drawing the conclusion"
**The company introduced a new system, but it failed to meet
expectations.**

*It* = the system (not the company). So the reasonable inference is:
*the system performed below expectations.* *"The employees failed"*
isn't supported — the pronoun reference simply doesn't point there.
:::

::: note title="Ellipsis: information the reader has to supply"
**John prefers reading books, while Sarah prefers magazines.** — fully
explicit; nothing is missing.

**John prefers reading books, while Sarah does not.** — here, *does
not* silently stands in for *does not prefer reading books.* English
regularly drops repeated material like this (called **ellipsis**), and
a reader has to reconstruct the missing words correctly before making
any inference from the sentence.
:::

## Modals That Carry Their Own Inference

Certain modal verbs — *would, could have, should have, would have* —
each build in a specific kind of inference about what did or didn't
actually happen.

::: concept title="Would: a prediction, not a confirmed outcome"
**The manager warned that the changes would create difficulties.**

*Would create* is a reported prediction (future-in-the-past). It tells
you the manager *predicted* difficulties — not that difficulties
*definitely occurred.*
:::

::: example title="Could have: a possible cause among others"
**The failure could have been caused by a configuration error.**

A configuration error is a *possible* cause here, not a confirmed one.
:::

::: important title="Should have: a real gap between what happened and what was expected"
**The company should have provided better training.**

This carries the author's evaluation. The strongly implied inference:
*the company did not provide enough training.* *Should have done X*
almost always signals that X did not, in fact, happen adequately.
:::

::: example title="Would have: a counterfactual, not a universal law"
**Without additional funding, the project would have failed.**

Counterfactual inference: additional funding mattered to the project's
survival. But *"the project definitely would have failed under every
possible condition"* claims more certainty than a single counterfactual
sentence can support.
:::

## Hidden Evaluation in Descriptive Language

::: example title="Contrast words can carry a judgment you have to infer"
**The proposal is ambitious but lacks a clear implementation plan.**

Directly stated: the proposal is ambitious; it lacks a clear plan. The
implicit evaluation, carried by the contrast itself: the author sees the
proposal as potentially problematic or incomplete — a conclusion you
reach from the *tone and contrast*, not from any single explicit
sentence.
:::

## A Full Worked Passage: Explicit, Inferred, and Out of Bounds

::: example title="Read once, slowly, before checking the breakdown below"
**The company introduced a new digital platform in an effort to improve
customer service. Initial reports indicated that response times had
decreased substantially. However, several employees complained that the
system was difficult to use and that they had received insufficient
training. Management subsequently announced additional training
sessions and revised several aspects of the platform. Although the
company described the implementation as successful overall, the need
for further adjustments suggests that the transition was not entirely
smooth.**
:::

**Explicit information:** the company introduced a digital platform to
improve customer service; response times decreased; employees reported
usability problems and insufficient training; management added training
and revised the platform; the company called the implementation
successful overall; further adjustments were still needed.

**Reasonable inferences:**

1. The platform probably improved some aspect of customer service —
   because response times decreased.
2. Employees experienced real difficulty adapting to the new system —
   because it was described as difficult to use, with insufficient
   training.
3. The implementation had both successes and problems — faster response
   times, but usability and training issues alongside them.

**What we cannot infer:** *the platform was a complete failure* (response
times did improve); *employees strongly opposed the platform* (only
*several* complained — not "most" or "all"); *management was
incompetent* (no evidence supports that judgment at all).

::: important title="'Not entirely' is a carefully calibrated evaluation"
The passage's last sentence never says *the implementation failed.* It
says *the transition was not entirely smooth* — a qualified, careful
judgment, not a verdict of failure. Reading it as one or the other
matters.
:::

## Calibrating Certainty With Precise Negative Language

A cluster of hedged, negative-leaning phrases shows up constantly in
careful writing, and each one has an exact shade of meaning worth
getting right.

::: concept title="Not entirely, far from, and the evidence-weight phrases"
**Not entirely** — *The evidence is not entirely consistent* means some
evidence is consistent and some may not be; it does **not** mean the
evidence is completely inconsistent. The same pattern applies across
the board: *not entirely successful* = successful in some respects, not
completely; *not entirely clear* = somewhat unclear; *not entirely
accurate* = contains some inaccuracies; *not entirely convincing* = has
weaknesses.

**Far from** — *The problem is far from solved* signals that the
problem remains significantly unresolved — a strong negative
implication, stronger than *not entirely solved.*

**Little evidence** — *There is little evidence that the policy
improved productivity* means the supporting evidence is weak or scarce.
It does not necessarily mean *the policy definitely did not improve
productivity.*

**Limited evidence** — *There is limited evidence that X improves Y*
means the available evidence is insufficient or relatively small, so
any conclusion drawn from it should stay cautious.

**Compelling evidence** — the opposite end of the scale: *there is
compelling evidence that X affects Y* signals evidence that is
genuinely strong.
:::

## Two Ladders for Calibrating Language

Reading and writing careful English both come down to matching your
language to exactly how strong the underlying support is. These two
ladders make that matching visible.

| Evidence strength (weakest → strongest) |
|---|
| no evidence |
| limited evidence |
| some evidence |
| evidence suggests |
| strong evidence |
| compelling evidence |

| Inference strength (strongest → weakest support) |
|---|
| Strongly supported — "the evidence strongly indicates…" |
| Reasonably supported — "this suggests…" |
| Possible — "this may mean…" |
| Speculative — "this could perhaps mean…" |

When you build your own inference from a text, aim to match its
certainty level to whichever rung the evidence actually sits on — not
the rung you'd prefer it sat on.

## Reading Chunks That Signal Inference

A handful of fixed phrases do most of the work of signalling *how much*
certainty a writer intends. Recognising them on sight saves you from
having to work out the certainty level from scratch every time.

::: note title="Seven chunks worth recognising instantly"
**"This suggests that…"** — an interpretation built from evidence just
given.

**"This may indicate that…"** — a cautious interpretation.

**"This does not necessarily mean that…"** — the author is actively
blocking an overgeneralization a reader might otherwise make.

**"It is possible that…"** — simple possibility, nothing stronger.

**"It is unlikely that…"** — low probability, explicitly flagged.

**"The evidence supports the view that…"** — evidence lines up behind a
position.

**"The findings are consistent with…"** — the findings fit comfortably
with one explanation, without ruling out every other one.

**"There is little evidence to suggest…"** — weak support, explicitly
flagged as weak.
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read the passage once at normal speed, without stopping to translate
any single word — then work through explicit facts, reasonable
inferences, and unsupported speculation exactly as the framework above
describes.

> Chittagong Port Authority reported that average container dwell time
> fell by two days after the terminal introduced a new gate-automation
> system. However, several freight forwarders said the system
> frequently rejected valid documentation, forcing drivers to queue for
> manual verification. The authority subsequently added extra staff at
> the gates and released a software patch within a month of the
> system's launch. Although officials described the rollout as a
> success overall, the need for a patch so soon after launch suggests
> that the system was not fully ready at the time of its introduction.

**Explicit facts:** dwell time fell by two days after automation was
introduced; some freight forwarders reported document rejections and
manual-verification queues; the authority added gate staff; a software
patch was released within a month; officials called the rollout a
success overall.

**Reasonable inferences:** the automation system probably sped up at
least part of the cargo-clearance process (dwell time fell); some
forwarders experienced genuine operational friction, not a minor
inconvenience (rejections plus queuing were significant enough to
require extra staff); the system had real technical gaps at launch
(a patch released within a month is fast turnaround, which usually
means something needed fixing urgently).

**Not supported:** *the automation project was a total failure* (dwell
time fell — that's real evidence of some success); *officials were
being dishonest about the rollout* (nothing indicates deliberate
misrepresentation, only a qualified, "successful overall" claim); *the
port will lose business to competitors* (this is speculation the
passage gives no evidence for at all).

**Author's final position:** *"not fully ready at the time of its
introduction"* is a calibrated, qualified judgment — closer to *not
entirely smooth* than to *failed.* Exactly the same careful distance
between a partial problem and a total failure runs through this whole
lesson.

## Golden Rule

::: golden
Infer only what the evidence earns — never more, and never less.
:::

## Lesson Summary

Today's skill sits one level above ordinary comprehension: not just
"what does this sentence mean?" but "what does it license me to
conclude, and where does that license run out?" You practised separating
explicit statements from reasonable implications and from unsupported
speculation; reading hedged language — *may, might, could, must,
probably, presumably* — for the exact certainty it signals; watching
quantifiers like *some, many,* and *often* so they don't silently
inflate into *most, all,* and *always* in your own head; telling
correlation apart from causation; and tracking whose claim a sentence
is actually reporting before deciding how much weight it deserves. Every
one of these skills does the same job: keeping your conclusions exactly
as large as the evidence that supports them — no larger, and no
smaller.

::: important title="The habit that matters most"
Before accepting any conclusion a text seems to invite, ask: *is this
what the text said, what it reasonably implies, or what I've quietly
added myself?* That single check is the difference between reading
between the lines and writing your own lines instead.
:::

## Practice: Test What You've Learned

Work through every question yourself before checking anything.

::: important title="Before you start"
Resist the urge to look up a "correct answer" online — for inference
questions, your own reasoning about *why* an interpretation is or isn't
supported matters more than matching a single expected phrasing.
:::

### Part A — Explicit vs. Inference

1. Read: **The company reduced its advertising budget and postponed
   plans to open two new branches.** Write one explicit fact stated in
   the sentence.
2. Write one reasonable inference the sentence supports.
3. Write one unsupported speculation the sentence does **not** support,
   and explain what evidence would be missing.

### Part B — Strength of Inference

4. **The lights are off, the doors are locked, and no employees are
   inside.** Which option is most justified?
   A. The company has permanently closed.
   B. The office is probably closed at the moment.
   C. The company is bankrupt.
   D. The employees have resigned.

### Part C — Cause vs. Association

5. **People who exercise regularly tend to report lower stress levels.**
   Which statement is most accurate?
   A. Exercise definitely causes lower stress.
   B. Lower stress causes people to exercise.
   C. Exercise and lower stress are associated.
   D. Exercise has no relationship with stress.

### Part D — Author Stance and Attribution

6. In this sentence — **The minister claims that the new policy will
   eliminate unemployment, although the report provides limited
   evidence to support this prediction** — who is making the claim?
7. What exactly is being claimed?
8. What is the author's own stance toward that claim?
9. What relationship does *although* create between the two halves of
   the sentence?
10. What does *limited evidence* tell you about how certain the
    prediction is?

### Part E — Advanced Inference

Read this passage for questions 11-16: **The new software initially
received positive reviews because of its speed and modern interface.
However, several months after its release, users began reporting
frequent crashes and compatibility problems. The developer subsequently
released multiple updates, although some users continued to experience
difficulties. The software remains widely used, but the continuing
release of corrective updates suggests that the product has not yet
reached complete technical stability.**

11. List at least three explicit facts from the passage.
12. Write at least two reasonable inferences the passage supports.
13. Name one inference that would **not** be justified, and explain
    what's missing from the evidence.
14. Is the author's overall stance positive, negative, or mixed? Justify
    your answer from the text.
15. Why does the author write *"suggests that"* rather than a flatter
    statement like *"proves that"* or *"shows that"*?
16. Rewrite *"has not yet reached complete technical stability"* in your
    own, simpler English.
