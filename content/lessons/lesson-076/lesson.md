## Introduction

Lesson 75 asked how much to trust a piece of evidence based on its
source and type. Today's lesson — the fifth part of this course's
argument-analysis arc — asks a different, equally important question:
what evidence is an author *showing* you, and what are they *not*
showing you? A text can be entirely factual and still present a slanted
picture, through what it selects, what it leaves out, and how it frames
the numbers it does include.

## Bias Does Not Mean Falsehood

::: important title="The single most important distinction this lesson makes"
An author's stated information can be completely true, while still
producing a biased overall picture — by giving some evidence more
weight, leaving other evidence out, choosing particular words,
foregrounding one perspective, and backgrounding another. **A text can
contain true information and still present a biased picture.** Bias ≠
falsehood — a distinction worth holding onto through everything that
follows.
:::

## Selection: What Information Was Chosen?

::: concept title="True statements can still be an incomplete picture"
*The new automated billing system reduced processing time by 30%.*
This can be entirely true. But if the report never mentions that
maintenance costs increased, training was expensive, smaller terminals
saw no improvement, or error rates stayed unchanged, the reader's
overall impression can end up considerably more positive than the full
picture warrants. Ask, always: what information was selected?
:::

## Omission: What Wasn't Said?

::: important title="Missing information can change an entire interpretation"
*The company introduced automation and productivity increased by 20%.*
If the text never mentions that the company also hired 30 additional
employees during the same period, the interpretation is incomplete —
additional staffing is a genuine alternative explanation for the
productivity increase, and it has simply been left out.
:::

## Not Every Omission Is Bias

::: warning title="No text can include everything — the real test is materiality"
A 500-page book cannot include every possible piece of relevant
evidence. **Omission ≠ automatically bias.** The real question: was the
omitted information important enough to change the reader's
interpretation? If yes, the omission is **material**. If not, it's
simply the ordinary, unavoidable compression every piece of writing
requires.
:::

## What Is Framing?

::: concept title="The same underlying facts, presented to steer attention or interpretation"
**Frame A**: *the new system reduced processing time by 30%.* **Frame
B**: *the new system failed to reduce overall operating costs despite a
30% reduction in processing time.* Both frames can share the identical
underlying fact — a 30% processing-time reduction — while directing the
reader's attention in opposite directions.
:::

## Positive Versus Negative Framing

::: important title="Identical numbers, opposite psychological effect"
*95% of invoices were processed successfully.* Positive framing: a 95%
success rate. Negative framing: a 5% failure rate. Numerically
identical information — but the two framings land very differently.
The reading skill: separate the underlying fact from the framing
applied to it.
:::

## Loaded Language

::: important title="Some words carry evaluation, not just description"
Compare: *the company changed its procedures* / *the company overhauled
its procedures* / *the company disrupted its procedures.* Same
underlying event, three different tones. More pairs worth recognising
on sight: reform vs. interference, investment vs. spending, streamlined
vs. cut, cautious vs. hesitant, ambitious vs. unrealistic, efficient
vs. aggressive, traditional vs. outdated, innovative vs. experimental.
A word's connotation often reveals an author's attitude even when the
underlying fact stays constant.
:::

## Euphemism and Dysphemism

::: concept title="Softening a negative, or sharpening a neutral, through word choice"
**Euphemism** — describing something negative in softer language:
*workforce reduction* instead of *layoffs.* **Dysphemism** — describing
something neutral or ordinary in deliberately negative language:
*bureaucratic interference* instead of *regulatory oversight.* Both are
framing tools, working in opposite directions from the same underlying
event.
:::

## Labeling

::: note title="The name given to a person or group shapes how they're perceived"
Compare: *government regulators* vs. *government bureaucrats*;
*industry experts* vs. *industry lobbyists.* Each label carries its
own attitude toward the same role. Ask, whenever a group is named: what
label is being used, and what attitude does it carry?
:::

## Evidence Selection Bias

::: important title="True evidence, incompletely represented"
*Four studies found that automation improves productivity.* This can
be technically true. But if six other studies found no meaningful
improvement, citing only the four positive ones leaves an incomplete
picture. Ask: how were these particular studies selected?
:::

## Cherry Picking

::: important title="Choosing the favorable evidence, and the same caution as with omission"
**Cherry picking** means selecting only the evidence that supports a
preferred conclusion while ignoring inconvenient evidence: *the company
highlights three successful automation projects while ignoring seven
projects that failed to meet their targets.* But — exactly as with
omission — mentioning only three studies does not, by itself, prove
cherry picking. The question remains: why were those three selected?
:::

## Base Rate Neglect

::: important title="A percentage can sound excellent while hiding a very different underlying reality"
*A new security system detected 99% of suspicious transactions.*
Sounds excellent — until you factor in how rare suspicious transactions
actually are. If only 100 of 100,000 transactions are genuinely
suspicious, the number of false positives could still be substantial.
Whenever a percentage appears, ask: what is the base rate it's actually
operating against?
:::

## Denominator Matters

::: note title="Connecting back to Lesson 56's percentage-tracking work"
*Complaints increased by 50%.* Sounds like a large increase. It could
mean 2 complaints became 3. It could mean 10,000 became 15,000 — the
same 50% increase, on a completely different absolute scale. Always
identify the denominator and baseline before reacting to a percentage
change.
:::

## Relative Versus Absolute Framing

::: important title="Connecting back to Lesson 56 — the same distinction, applied to bias detection"
*Error rates fell by 50%* — if the change was 2% → 1%, the **absolute**
reduction is one percentage point, while the **relative** reduction is
50%. Conflating the two can make a genuinely modest change sound
dramatic, or the reverse — always check which framing a stated
percentage is actually using.
:::

## Positive Evidence Versus Missing Negative Evidence

::: concept title="One good metric does not make the whole picture positive"
*Customer satisfaction increased by 15%.* How many customers? Which
customers? What measurement method? What was the previous baseline?
What about dissatisfied customers, complaints, or churn? A single
positive metric reported on its own says nothing about whatever
negative metrics weren't mentioned.
:::

## Survivorship Bias, Revisited

::: note title="Connecting back to Lesson 75"
*The world's most successful startups used strategy X. Therefore,
strategy X causes startup success.* Where's the data on unsuccessful
startups that used the exact same strategy? Ask, always: who is missing
from this dataset?
:::

## Counterfactual Missing Evidence

::: important title="A genuinely powerful question for testing any causal claim"
Ask: **what would we expect to see if the author's explanation were
wrong?** Author's claim: automation caused productivity growth.
Counterfactual question: if automation were *not* the cause, what else
might we observe? Possible answers: productivity also rose in
non-automated teams; staffing changed; workload changed; management
policy changed. Each of these, if actually observed, would constitute
a genuine alternative explanation — and actively imagining them is how
a critical reader tests a causal claim rather than simply accepting it.
:::

## Perspective Bias

::: important title="The same event looks different depending on whose eyes it's seen through"
Take automation deployment: management sees cost reduction; employees
see workload and job security; the IT team sees maintenance
complexity; customers see speed and service quality; finance sees total
cost of ownership. A report drawing only on management's perspective
isn't necessarily false — it's **perspective-limited**, presenting one
genuine stakeholder's view as though it were the whole picture.
:::

## Whose Interests Matter?

::: important title="Incentive analysis, not conspiracy thinking"
Ask: who benefits if the reader accepts this conclusion? A software
vendor claiming *"our platform dramatically improves operational
efficiency"* naturally invites the follow-up questions: is there
independent evaluation? Is this only the vendor's own data? Is there a
comparison group? Are negative findings reported? Is cost data
included? A source's incentives can shape how evidence gets
interpreted and presented, without that source ever stating a single
false fact.
:::

## Conflict of Interest

::: concept title="When a source's conclusion and its financial interest point the same direction"
*A company-funded study finds that its own product is highly
effective.* This is not automatically invalid — but it deserves
additional scrutiny: was the methodology transparent? Were independent
researchers involved? Were negative results reported alongside positive
ones?
:::

## Author Bias Versus Text Bias

::: important title="A careful, defensible distinction — don't overclaim intent"
**Author bias** is a person's actual preference or viewpoint — usually
unknowable with certainty from the text alone. **Text bias** is the
effect actually produced by the final text's selection, framing,
omission, language, and evidence balance — visible directly on the
page. Prefer *"the text appears to frame the issue positively"* over
*"the author deliberately manipulated the reader"* — the second is a
far stronger claim about intent than a critical reader can usually
support from the text alone.
:::

## Tone and Evidence Together

::: important title="Check whether the language's strength actually matches the evidence's strength"
*Although the pilot produced modest improvements, management's
ambitious automation strategy represents a major step toward
modernization.* Evidence: modest improvements. Evaluative phrase: major
step. Whenever evaluative language sounds considerably stronger than
the evidence actually reported, that mismatch itself is worth flagging
explicitly — don't just notice the tone or the evidence in isolation;
check them against each other.
:::

## A Strong Critical Reading Example

::: example title="Positive evidence, positive framing, and a list of what's missing, weighed together"
**The automated billing system increased invoice-processing speed by
30% during its first year. Management therefore considers the project a
major success. Employee surveys also showed high satisfaction with the
new workflow. However, the report does not provide data on
implementation costs, long-term maintenance, or performance in smaller
terminals.**

Positive evidence: processing speed up 30%; employee satisfaction
high. Positive framing: "major success." Missing evidence:
implementation cost, maintenance cost, smaller-terminal performance.
Evaluation problem: success according to which criteria? Final
judgment: the evidence supports operational improvements, but it is
insufficient to establish overall project success.
:::

## A Bias Detection Framework

::: framework title="Ten questions for any serious text"
- Selection — which evidence was chosen?
- Omission — which relevant evidence is absent?
- Framing — which aspect is foregrounded?
- Language — which loaded or evaluative words are used?
- Perspective — whose viewpoint dominates?
- Incentives — does the source have a relevant interest?
- Counterevidence — is opposing evidence given?
- Balance — does the evidence's strength match the language's strength?
- Scope — does the conclusion exceed the evidence's scope?
- Alternative explanation — is there another interpretation?
:::

## The "Missing Column" Technique

::: important title="A concrete tool for turning a vague suspicion of bias into a specific list"
Build a two-column mental table: what the author shows, and what the
author does not show.

| Author shows | Author does not show |
|---|---|
| Processing time ↓ | Long-term cost |
| Error reports ↓ | Actual error rate |
| Satisfaction ↑ | Non-users' opinions |
| Four terminals improved | Smaller terminals |
| Six-month results | Five-year reliability |

Then ask: does the missing column materially change the conclusion? If
it does, the conclusion should be qualified accordingly.
:::

## Bias Spectrum

::: concept title="Avoid binary thinking — most texts sit somewhere in between"
Picture a spectrum: balanced → selective → strongly selective →
misleading framing. No serious text is purely 100% objective or 100%
biased; the useful question isn't "is this text biased, yes or no?" but
"where does it sit on this spectrum, and in which direction?"
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read this passage once, straight through. Then apply the missing-
column technique, name any loaded language or framing choice, and ask
the counterfactual question, before checking your reading against the
notes that follow.

> Since adopting the new scheduling software, the clinic has seen
> appointment no-shows drop by an impressive 40%. Staff describe the
> transition as a resounding success, crediting the software's
> automated reminder feature. The clinic has not published data on
> total patient volume during this period, nor on whether reminder
> calls placed by staff — a practice that also began around the same
> time — might have contributed to the improvement.

Loaded language and framing: "impressive," "resounding success" —
strongly positive evaluative language attached to a single metric.
Missing column — shown: no-show rate down 40%, staff endorsement. Not
shown: total patient volume (a lower volume alone could reduce
no-shows without the software helping at all), and the concurrent
introduction of staff reminder calls — a genuine alternative
explanation the passage names but doesn't rule out. Counterfactual
question: if the software weren't the actual cause, what would we
expect to see? Possibly the same no-show reduction, explained instead
by the reminder calls, or by a coincidental drop in patient volume.
The passage's own framing ("resounding success," crediting the
software specifically) outruns what its own reported evidence actually
establishes.

## Golden Rule

::: golden
Do not ask only "is this information true?" — ask what picture the selection, framing, language, and omission of information creates, and whether that picture is actually justified by the full evidence, always asking what is missing that could change your interpretation.
:::

## Lesson Summary

This lesson added a layer the previous four lessons in this arc hadn't
directly addressed: what an author chooses to show, and what they
leave out. You learned that bias does not mean falsehood — a text can
be entirely factual while still producing a slanted picture through
selection, omission, framing, and word choice. You worked through
loaded language, euphemism, dysphemism, and labelling as tools that
smuggle evaluation into apparently neutral description, and learned to
judge an omission by materiality rather than assuming every gap is
bias. You revisited percentage framing through base rate neglect,
denominator tracking, and relative-versus-absolute framing, and
practised the counterfactual question as a genuine test of a causal
claim. You considered perspective bias and conflict of interest as
forces shaping a text's overall shape, carefully distinguished author
bias from text bias, and closed with the "missing column" technique —
a concrete way to ask, of any argument, what is missing, and whether
that absence would actually change the conclusion.

::: important title="Five lessons, one connected skill"
Lessons 72 through 76 have built one continuous reading capability:
taking apart an argument's anatomy, naming its missing bridges, judging
its evidence sufficiency, tracing that evidence to its actual source
and quality, and now, checking what the whole picture leaves out. Held
together, these five lessons replace "the author makes a good case" or
"the author seems biased" with something far more useful — a precise
account of exactly what was shown, what wasn't, and whether the
conclusion has actually earned the confidence it's stated with.
:::

### Part A — Selection, Omission, and Materiality

1. **A company report states: 'Revenue grew 12% this year,' without mentioning that a major competitor exited the market during the same period.** Explain why this omission could be material. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">A competitor's exit is a genuine alternative explanation for the revenue growth — customers may simply have shifted to this company by default, rather than the company's own strategy or product quality driving the gain. Leaving this out lets the reader credit the 12% entirely to the company's own performance, which could change the interpretation considerably.</div></details>
2. **A 400-page history book does not mention every minor treaty signed during the period it covers.** Explain why this omission is not automatically evidence of bias. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">No text can include every possible fact, and minor treaties are unlikely to change a reader's overall interpretation of the period's major events. Since the omission isn't material — it wouldn't shift the reader's understanding if included — it's simply ordinary compression, not evidence of a slanted picture.</div></details>
3. **A product review lists five positive features and does not mention price.** Explain what additional information would help you judge whether this omission is material. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">Knowing whether the product is priced dramatically higher or lower than comparable alternatives would help — if the price is unusually high, omitting it would materially change a reader's sense of whether the product is actually worth recommending, whereas an average price would make the omission far less consequential.</div></details>

### Part B — Framing and Loaded Language

4. **Compare: 'The policy cut funding by 10%' versus 'The policy streamlined funding by 10%.'** Identify the loaded word and the attitude it carries. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">"Streamlined" is the loaded word, replacing the more neutral or negative "cut." It carries a positive, approving attitude, framing the same 10% funding reduction as an efficient improvement rather than a loss, even though the underlying fact is identical.</div></details>
5. **A report states: '80% of employees did not oppose the merger.'** Reframe this statement in a way that presents the identical fact more negatively. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">"20% of employees actively opposed the merger" presents the identical underlying fact but frames it negatively, drawing attention to a meaningful minority of resistance rather than the majority who simply did not object.</div></details>
6. **Identify whether 'whistleblower' and 'informant' are likely to be euphemisms, dysphemisms, or neither, depending on context, and explain your reasoning.** <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">"Whistleblower" tends to function as a positive label, casting the person as a principled truth-teller, which can act as a euphemism when describing someone who broke confidentiality rules. "Informant" tends to carry a more negative, suspicious connotation, closer to a dysphemism, even when describing the identical act of reporting wrongdoing to authorities. Which label applies often reveals the author's own attitude toward the person described.</div></details>

### Part C — Base Rates, Denominators, and Relative Framing

7. **A fraud-detection tool flags 95% of fraudulent transactions. Explain what additional information about the base rate you would need before judging how many false alarms this might generate.** <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">You would need to know how rare fraudulent transactions actually are relative to the total number of transactions processed. If fraud is very rare, even a small false-positive rate on the much larger pool of legitimate transactions could generate a substantial number of false alarms despite the tool's impressive 95% detection figure.</div></details>
8. **'Customer complaints rose by 200%.' Explain why this statement alone does not tell you whether this is a serious problem.** <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">Without the denominator and baseline, a 200% increase could mean 1 complaint became 3, which is trivial, or 5,000 became 15,000, which is serious. The relative percentage alone hides the absolute scale, so the statement can't be judged as a serious problem until the actual starting and ending numbers are known.</div></details>
9. **'Deaths from the disease fell by 50%,' where the rate went from 4 in 100,000 to 2 in 100,000. Identify the absolute change and the relative change separately.** <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">The absolute change is a reduction of 2 deaths per 100,000 people (from 4 to 2). The relative change is 50%, since 2 is half of the original 4. Both are accurate, but the relative framing sounds far more dramatic than the modest absolute change actually is.</div></details>

### Part D — Perspective, Incentives, and the Missing Column

10. **A press release from a software company states its own product 'sets the new industry standard.'** List two questions a critical reader should ask before accepting this claim. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">Two useful questions: is there any independent evaluation or third-party benchmark supporting this claim, rather than only the company's own data? And what criteria is "industry standard" actually being judged against — performance, adoption, price, or something else entirely?</div></details>
11. **A report on a new workplace policy quotes only senior managers about its effects.** Identify the perspective bias, and name one other stakeholder group worth hearing from. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">The perspective bias is management-only framing — the report presents senior managers' view of the policy's effects as though it were the complete picture. Frontline employees, who directly experience the policy's day-to-day impact, are a stakeholder group worth hearing from as well.</div></details>
12. **Explain, using this lesson's terms, why 'the text appears to frame the issue positively' is a more defensible statement than 'the author is lying to readers.'** <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">"The text appears to frame the issue positively" describes text bias — an effect visible directly in the words on the page, which a reader can point to and support. "The author is lying" is a claim about author bias and deliberate intent, something usually unknowable with certainty from the text alone, so it is a far stronger claim than a critical reader can typically defend.</div></details>
