## Introduction

Lesson 71 tracked how an author's claim *develops* across a whole
passage — broad opening, evidence, complication, refined final
position. Today's lesson zooms into a single argument and takes it
apart at the joints: which statement is the premise, which is
evidence, which is an inference drawn from that evidence, and which is
the conclusion the whole thing is actually building toward. This is
the core skill of critical reading — not just following an argument,
but being able to point to exactly which piece is doing which job.

## The Basic Structure of an Argument

::: concept title="Premise → evidence/support → inference → conclusion"
*Employees who receive structured training make fewer billing errors.
The new system requires employees to interpret more detailed billing
data. Therefore, organizations implementing the system should provide
additional training.* Premise: employees who receive structured
training make fewer billing errors. Supporting fact: the new system
requires employees to interpret more detailed billing data. Inference:
more interpretation demand → training becomes more important.
Conclusion: organizations should provide additional training. Four
distinct jobs, four distinct sentences.
:::

## What Is a Premise?

::: important title="The statement a conclusion is actually built on"
A premise is whatever answers the question "why should I believe the
conclusion?" *The port should upgrade its network infrastructure
because current equipment frequently causes communication failures.*
Conclusion: the port should upgrade its network infrastructure.
Premise: current equipment frequently causes communication failures.
:::

## Not Every Reason Is an Equally Good Premise

::: warning title="Count the reasons, but also weigh their quality"
*The system is expensive, difficult to maintain, and unpopular among
some employees. Therefore, it should not be adopted.* Three reasons are
offered — cost, maintenance, popularity — but they're not equally
strong. Cost can be an objective, measurable factor. Maintenance
difficulty can be an operational criterion. Unpopularity is
preference-based, and preferences alone rarely settle a question this
consequential. Reading an argument well means evaluating each reason's
evidential quality, not just tallying how many reasons were listed.
:::

## Evidence Versus Premise: A Layered Structure

::: concept title="A premise can itself rest on evidence"
*Employee training improves accuracy* functions as a premise. *A
three-year study of 15,000 employees found that structured training
reduced processing errors by 23%* is evidence supporting that premise.

```
   EVIDENCE
      ↓
   SUPPORTS
      ↓
   PREMISE
      ↓
   SUPPORTS
      ↓
  CONCLUSION
```

Read this as a layered structure — evidence holding up a premise,
which in turn holds up a conclusion — not a flat, single-level chain.
:::

## Evidence → Claim → Conclusion: A More Realistic Chain

::: important title="Evidence rarely jumps straight to a recommendation"
*A study found that automated inventory systems reduced stock
discrepancies by 18%. This suggests that automation can improve
inventory accuracy. Therefore, organizations with high levels of
inventory error may benefit from automation.* Evidence: 18% reduction
in stock discrepancies. Interpretation: automation can improve
inventory accuracy. Recommendation: organizations may benefit from
automation. Evidence doesn't lead directly to a recommendation — an
interpretation step sits in between, and skipping past it is one of the
most common ways an argument gets misread as stronger than it is.
:::

## "This Suggests": The Inference Bridge

::: important title="The phrase that carries you from observed fact to interpretation"
*Processing errors declined after employees received additional
training. This suggests that employee capability was an important
factor in the improvement.* Observed fact: errors declined. Inference:
employee capability was important. The inference is not itself an
observation — it's a step the author is asking you to take, one that
should be checked rather than absorbed automatically.
:::

## "Therefore": The Conclusion Bridge

::: note title="The phrase that carries you from premise to conclusion"
*Employee training improved data interpretation. Therefore, training
should be included in the implementation plan.* First statement:
premise or reason. "Therefore": inference marker. Second statement:
recommendation or conclusion. Two bridges, two different phrases —
"this suggests" typically carries evidence to interpretation;
"therefore" typically carries premise to conclusion.
:::

## Reason Markers: Because, Since, Given That, Considering That

::: concept title="Four ways of introducing the 'why' behind a claim"
*The company delayed deployment because the security review was
incomplete.*

```
     WHY?
      ↓
Security review incomplete
      ↓
   THEREFORE
      ↓
  Deployment delayed
```

*Since the available data are incomplete, the results should be
interpreted cautiously* — "since" here means "because"; watch context
carefully, since "since" can also mark time (*the system has improved
since the update was deployed*). *Given that the sample was relatively
small, the findings should be interpreted cautiously* foregrounds a
premise formally. *Considering that maintenance costs have increased,
the company should review the system's long-term value* works the same
way — a formal way of flagging the consideration driving a
recommendation.
:::

## Basis Markers: Based On, On the Basis Of, In Light Of

::: concept title="Naming what a conclusion rests on"
*Based on the available evidence, the researchers concluded that the
intervention had limited effects.*

```
AVAILABLE EVIDENCE
       ↓
   BASED ON
       ↓
RESEARCHER CONCLUSION
```

*On the basis of these findings, the committee recommended further
testing* is a more formal version of the same move, common in academic
and policy documents. *In light of the recent failures, the
organization decided to revise the procedure* signals that new,
relevant information influenced a decision.
:::

## "Accordingly" and "Consequently"

::: note title="Signalling action or result flowing from prior reasoning"
*The existing process was found to be unreliable. Accordingly, a new
verification procedure was introduced* — unreliable → accordingly → new
procedure: action following from a stated finding. *The input data were
incomplete. Consequently, several calculations had to be repeated* —
cause → consequence. Both can signal a causal relationship, but as with
every connector in this lesson, verify the author's actual reasoning
rather than trusting the connector word alone.
:::

## "This Means That…"

::: concept title="Drawing out a consequence or interpretation"
*The system requires manual verification for exceptional cases. This
means that full automation is not currently possible.* The inference —
full automation isn't currently possible — is drawn directly from the
stated requirement (manual verification for exceptions), not
introduced as a separate, independently supported fact.
:::

## "In Other Words" Is Not New Evidence

::: warning title="Connecting back to Lesson 71's restatement markers"
*The process is highly dependent on data quality. In other words, poor
input data can produce unreliable results.* The second sentence
restates the first — it is not new evidence, and shouldn't be counted
as independent support when you're weighing how much backing an
argument actually has. Restatement ≠ independent support.
:::

## Example Is Not Evidence

::: important title="An illustration is not proof of a general claim"
*Many successful companies use automation. For example, Company X
automated its billing process.* Company X's example is offered — but
"one company succeeded, and it used automation" does not establish that
automation *caused* that success. An example illustrates a claim; it
doesn't, by itself, prove one.
:::

## Case Study Is Not General Proof

::: important title="What worked in one place doesn't automatically generalise"
*A port implemented a digital tracking system and reduced truck waiting
times by 30%.* Fair conclusion: the system worked in that port, under
those conditions. Unsupported leap: the system will reduce waiting
times by 30% in every port. This is a scope mismatch — the case study's
actual scope (one port, one set of conditions) is narrower than the
generalised claim being built on top of it.
:::

## Analogy Is Not Evidence

::: note title="Connecting back to Lessons 59 and 60 — an analogy builds intuition, not proof"
*Managing data is like managing cargo: both require careful tracking.*
This can help a reader understand a concept — but the analogy itself is
not empirical evidence for any claim about data management's actual
effectiveness.
:::

## Authority Is Not Automatic Proof

::: important title="An expert's opinion is a claim, not proof of that claim"
*A leading expert argues that AI will transform logistics.* An expert
opinion has been offered — but "an expert said it" does not
automatically make a claim true. The critical question remains: what
evidence does the expert actually provide for this specific claim?
:::

## Hidden Inference

::: concept title="A conclusion the author expects you to draw, without stating it outright"
*The new system requires employees to learn several additional
procedures. Training time has already increased substantially during
the pilot phase.* The author never states outright: *implementation
will require additional training resources.* But the inference is
clearly invited: more procedures + increased training time →
additional training resources likely needed. Reading well means
noticing when a conclusion is implied rather than stated, and being
able to state it explicitly yourself.
:::

## The Argument Gap

::: important title="The missing bridge between premise and conclusion"
*The software is widely used. Therefore, it is the best software for
our organization.* Gap: "widely used" → "best for our organization" —
this relationship is never actually established. The missing premise
that would close the gap: *the needs of our organization are similar to
those of organizations using the software.* That missing piece is the
argument's **hidden assumption**, and naming it explicitly is often the
single fastest way to evaluate whether an argument actually holds up.
:::

## Assumptions, Revisited

::: note title="Building on this course's earlier work on assumptions"
*Automation should be adopted because it reduces labor costs.* Hidden
assumption: reducing labor cost is sufficiently important to justify
adoption. *Automation should be adopted because competitors are using
it.* Hidden assumption: competitors' adoption indicates that automation
is appropriate for our organization too. Every argument with a gap has
an assumption filling that gap, whether the author states it or not.
:::

## Necessary Assumption Versus Helpful Assumption

::: important title="One kind of assumption an argument can survive without; one it can't"
A **helpful assumption** makes an argument stronger, but the argument
can survive without it. A **necessary assumption** is one the argument
collapses without — if it's false, the conclusion no longer follows.
*The company should adopt the software because it reduces processing
time.* Necessary assumption: processing time is an important criterion
for this company's objective. If processing time turns out to be
essentially irrelevant to the company's actual goals, the conclusion
falls apart — that's what makes this assumption necessary rather than
merely helpful.
:::

## Testing an Argument: "If This Is True, Then…"

::: important title="A powerful, simple test for whether a premise actually supports its conclusion"
Ask: if the premise is true, does the conclusion automatically follow?
*The system is faster* → *the system is better.* Better in what
respect? If the relevant criterion really is speed, the argument holds
up reasonably well. If the relevant criteria also include reliability,
cost, security, and maintainability, speed alone isn't enough to
support "better" — the argument is weaker than it first appears.
:::

## Missing Criteria Weaken an Argument

::: note title="Connecting back to Lesson 65's evaluation framework"
*Solution A is better than Solution B.* Critical questions: better in
terms of what — cost, speed, reliability, security, scalability,
usability? An argument built on an unqualified "better" is exactly the
incomplete evaluation Lesson 65 trained you to catch — here, applied to
judging the argument's own soundness.
:::

## Scope Shift

::: important title="A conclusion quietly claims a broader scope than its premise actually covers"
*Most employees prefer the new system.* Then: *therefore, the new
system is better.* Scope shift: preference → overall quality. What was
the preference actually based on? Maybe ease of use — in which case,
usability is supported, but reliability, security, and cost remain
completely unaddressed. The conclusion has quietly expanded past what
the premise actually established.
:::

## Time Shift

::: important title="Connecting back to Lesson 62 — sequence in time is not causation"
*Performance improved after the new system was introduced.* Conclusion:
*the new system caused the improvement.* Problem: after ≠ because of.
Training, staffing changes, process redesign, or seasonal variation
could plausibly have occurred around the same time — the time shift
from "improved after X" to "caused by X" is exactly the sequence-versus-
causation gap Lesson 62 first built.
:::

## The Correlation-to-Causation Gap

::: important title="Connecting back to Lesson 56 — several explanations can fit the same association"
*Employees who use the new system perform better.* Possible
explanations: the system genuinely improves performance; better
employees are simply more likely to use the system; trained employees
use the system more; management selectively assigns the system to
already high-performing teams. Association ≠ causation — one of the
most frequently violated principles in argument writing, precisely
because several very different explanations can produce the identical
observed pattern.
:::

## "Therefore" Doesn't Certify Validity

::: warning title="Connecting back to Lessons 64 and 71"
*The system is expensive. Therefore, it is ineffective.* "Therefore" is
present — but the reasoning gap (expensive → ineffective) is never
actually closed. A system can easily be expensive and highly effective,
or expensive and cost-effective. "Therefore" signals the author
believes a conclusion follows; it never certifies that it actually
does.
:::

## "Because" Doesn't Certify Causal Truth

::: warning title="Connecting back to Lesson 62's causal-strength ladder"
*The project failed because employees resisted the change.* This is
the author's causal claim — not a proven fact. A critical reader asks
what evidence supports it, and considers alternatives: poor
implementation, inadequate training, technical problems, unrealistic
deadlines, budget limitations. "Because" signals a causal claim; it
does not prove the claim.
:::

## Rating Argument Strength

::: framework title="Four levels for judging how well an argument holds together"
- Strong — evidence directly supports the conclusion, assumptions are reasonable, and scope matches
- Moderate — evidence supports the conclusion, but real limitations exist
- Weak — evidence is relevant but insufficient
- Unsupported — the conclusion substantially exceeds the available evidence
:::

## A Full Worked Example

::: example title="Evidence, scope, qualification, inference, limitation, and a carefully qualified conclusion"
**A six-month pilot found that automated billing reduced average
processing time by 28%. The reduction was observed across four
operational teams, although the teams differed in workload and staffing
levels. These findings suggest that automation can improve processing
efficiency. However, the pilot did not measure long-term maintenance
costs or the effect of employee training. Therefore, while the results
provide support for wider implementation, they do not by themselves
establish that full-scale deployment would be cost-effective.**

Evidence: 28% reduction. Scope: four teams. Qualification: teams
differed in workload and staffing. Inference: automation can improve
processing efficiency. Limitations: long-term maintenance not measured;
training effect not measured. Conclusion: evidence supports wider
implementation. Qualification of the conclusion: cost-effectiveness not
established. The author never claims "automation is definitely
cost-effective" — only that evidence supports wider implementation,
while cost-effectiveness specifically remains unestablished. That's a
genuinely qualified conclusion, not an overreach.
:::

## Argument Compression

::: note title="Connecting back to Lessons 47, 63, and 71's compression skill"
The paragraph above compresses to one sentence without losing its real
shape: *a multi-team pilot found meaningful processing-time improvements
from automation, supporting further implementation, but insufficient
evidence exists to establish its long-term cost-effectiveness.* Notice
what survives: evidence, scope, benefit, recommendation, and
limitation — exactly the parts a careless summary drops first.
:::

## An Argument Tracking Template

::: framework title="A note-taking structure for any chapter-length argument"
- Claim — what does the author want me to believe?
- Premise — why?
- Evidence — what supports the premise?
- Inference — what does the author infer?
- Assumption — what must be true for the inference to work?
- Counterargument — what challenges it?
- Response — how does the author answer?
- Qualification — what limits the claim?
- Conclusion — what does the author finally conclude?
:::

## An Advanced Reading Algorithm

::: framework title="Eight steps for any argument-heavy paragraph"
- Find the claim — what is being asserted?
- Find the reasons — why?
- Find the evidence — what supports it?
- Find the inference — what conclusion is drawn from the evidence?
- Find the hidden assumption — what bridge is required?
- Find the counterargument — what could challenge it?
- Find the qualification — under what conditions is it true?
- Find the final conclusion — what does the author actually want me to accept?
:::

## Master Argument Model

::: framework title="The full architecture behind a fully developed argument"
- Claim
- Premise
- Evidence
- Interpretation
- Inference
- Hidden assumption
- Counterargument
- Response
- Qualification
- Refined claim
- Conclusion
- Implication / recommendation
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read this passage once, straight through. Then identify its premise,
its evidence, its inference, its hidden assumption, and its final
qualified conclusion, before checking your reading against the notes
that follow.

> Warehouses that adopted barcode scanning reported fewer picking
> errors than those relying on manual entry, based on a survey of 40
> mid-sized distribution centers. This suggests that scanning technology
> can reduce human error in order fulfillment. However, the surveyed
> warehouses also tended to have more experienced staff and lower
> turnover than the industry average, so staffing stability may have
> contributed to the difference as well. Because the survey did not
> control for staff experience, it would be premature to conclude that
> barcode scanning alone accounts for the lower error rates. Therefore,
> while the association supports further investigation, it does not by
> itself justify treating scanning technology as a guaranteed fix for
> picking errors.

Premise/evidence: warehouses with barcode scanning reported fewer
picking errors, based on a 40-center survey. Inference bridge, "this
suggests": scanning technology can reduce human error. Competing
explanation surfaced: surveyed warehouses also had more experienced,
stable staff — a confound the passage names explicitly. Explicit
scope-check: "because the survey did not control for staff experience,
it would be premature to conclude" — directly blocking the
correlation-to-causation leap this lesson has repeatedly warned about.
Final qualified conclusion: the association supports further
investigation, but does not justify treating scanning as a guaranteed
fix — a considerably narrower claim than the opening sentence might
suggest on its own.

## Golden Rule

::: golden
Never jump directly from evidence to conclusion — always ask: what does the evidence actually show, what is the author inferring from it, what assumption connects that inference to the conclusion, and is the conclusion stronger than the evidence actually earns?
:::

## Lesson Summary

This lesson took a single argument apart into its working parts. You
separated premise from evidence from inference from conclusion, and
practised reading "this suggests" and "therefore" as two distinct
structural bridges rather than interchangeable connectors. You learned
that an example, a case study, an analogy, and an appeal to authority
are none of them, by themselves, genuine evidence for a general claim.
You found the gap between a premise and an overreaching conclusion, and
named the hidden assumption filling that gap — distinguishing a
necessary assumption (without which the argument collapses) from a
merely helpful one. You caught scope shift and time shift as two
recurring argument weaknesses, and practised treating "because" and
"therefore" as signals of a claim, never as proof the claim is sound.
You closed by rating a full worked argument's strength and compressing
it into one sentence without losing its evidence, scope, or
qualification.

::: important title="The core question this lesson trains you to ask"
Every argument you read from now on deserves the same question: what
exactly supports the author's conclusion, and what part am I merely
being asked to assume? Holding that question in mind, sentence by
sentence, is the difference between following an argument and actually
evaluating one.
:::
