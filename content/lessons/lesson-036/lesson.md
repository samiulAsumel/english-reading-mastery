## Introduction

Real books, journalism, business writing, history, philosophy, and
academic texts share one demand that a dictionary alone can never meet:
**a word's meaning isn't fixed inside the word — context builds it.**
The same word can mean entirely different things depending on what
surrounds it, and the same sentence can honestly support more than one
reading until context narrows it down. Today's lesson builds the skill
of letting context do that work for you, instead of memorizing one
Bangla equivalent per English word and hoping it always fits.

## What Is Context?

::: concept title="The surrounding information that shapes a word's meaning"
**Context** = the information surrounding a word, sentence, or idea
that helps determine its meaning.

**The company is under pressure.** — *pressure* here means difficulty,
demand, or stress, not a physical force. **The pressure inside the
container increased.** — here *pressure* is physical/scientific. Same
word, different meaning, entirely dependent on context.
:::

## Dictionary Meaning vs. Contextual Meaning

::: warning title="One English word rarely maps onto exactly one fixed meaning"
Take **issue**: dictionary meanings include *problem, topic,
edition/publication, to officially provide, a matter under
discussion.* **The company has an issue with its billing system** =
*problem.* **The article discusses the issue of workplace automation**
= *topic.* **The bank issued a new card** = *officially provided.*

Don't memorize *one English word = one Bangla meaning.* Instead: *word
+ context + collocation = actual meaning.*
:::

## Polysemy and Homonymy

::: important title="Related multiple meanings, or unrelated meanings sharing a form"
**Polysemy** — a word's several meanings are conceptually related.
*Head*: head of a person, head of a department, head of a table, head
of a queue — all connected by the idea of *top/leading position.*

**Homonymy** — a word's meanings are unrelated, sharing only spelling
and pronunciation. *Bank*: a financial institution, or a river bank.
**The bank increased interest rates** (financial institution) vs. **the
village lies on the bank of the river** (riverside) — without context,
meaning can't be determined at all.
:::

## Four Layers of Context

::: framework title="Check meaning against increasingly wide layers"
- Immediate context: the words right beside the unknown one
- Sentence context: the whole sentence
- Paragraph context: the paragraph's overall argument
- Global context: the whole article or book's topic
:::

The wider the layer you check a meaning against, the more accurate that
meaning is likely to be.

## Collocation Meaning

::: example title="A word's natural partners often carry most of its meaning"
**Strong argument** = convincing argument. **Strong evidence** =
convincing/reliable evidence. **Strong coffee** = concentrated/intense.
**Strong wind** = powerful. **Strong relationship** = close/robust.

Learning *strong = শক্তিশালী* alone isn't enough — learn the
collocations *strong evidence*, *strong argument*, *strong
relationship* as chunks. **Verb + noun collocations** work the same
way: *make a decision, reach a conclusion, draw an inference, raise a
concern, address a problem, pose a question* — translate these word by
word and they turn awkward; learn them as fixed chunks instead.
:::

## Context Can Change a Word's Evaluative Force

::: important title="The same core word, neutral or loaded depending on its neighbor"
**Claim** on its own can be a neutral report of a proposition. **An
unsupported claim** carries negative evaluation. **A reasonable claim**
carries positive/qualified evaluation. A word's meaning isn't only
lexical — it can carry contextual evaluation too.
:::

## Ambiguity

::: concept title="More than one plausible reading of the same expression"
**Ambiguity** = an expression that has more than one plausible
interpretation.

**I saw the man with the telescope.** Meaning 1: *I used a telescope
to see the man.* Meaning 2: *I saw the man who had a telescope.* The
sentence's structure genuinely supports both readings — context
outside the sentence usually resolves which one is intended.
:::

## Structural, Lexical, Referential, and Scope Ambiguity

::: example title="Four different places ambiguity can hide"
**Structural:** *The manager discussed the report with the
employees.* — did the manager discuss it *together with* the
employees, or is the report *about* the employees? Context usually
resolves it.

**Lexical:** *The company opened a new branch.* — *branch* = office, or
tree branch? Business context makes this easy: *office.*

**Referential:** *The manager spoke to the supervisor after he
reviewed the report.* — who does *he* refer to, the manager or the
supervisor? Without more context, genuinely uncertain — this is exactly
Lessons 10 and 23's reference-tracking skill, now applied to a genuinely
ambiguous case.

**Scope:** *Only the manager approved the new system* (nobody else
approved it) vs. *The manager approved only the new system* (the
manager approved nothing else) — *only*'s position changes the whole
meaning, echoing Lesson 25's warning about this exact word.
:::

## Negation Scope

::: warning title="Not all is not the same claim as none"
**Not all employees support the policy.** = *some employees do not
support it* — this is **not** the same as *no employees support it.*
*Not all ≠ none*, exactly parallel to *not necessarily ≠ definitely
not.*

**Not uncommon:** *the problem is not uncommon* means *fairly common* —
the opposite of *the problem is rare.* **Not impossible:** *it is not
impossible that the policy could succeed* means *success is possible*,
with low certainty — not the same claim as *the policy will succeed.*
Academic English uses these careful double-negative-style constructions
constantly.
:::

## Little vs. A Little, Few vs. A Few

::: important title="A tiny article changes the whole claim"
**Little evidence** = almost insufficient; very limited. **A little
evidence** = some evidence exists. *There is little evidence that AI
caused the improvement* (evidence is very sparse) vs. *there is a
little evidence that AI caused the improvement* (some evidence exists).

**Few employees** = negative implication, very small number. **A few
employees** = some employees, neutral. *Few employees attended the
training* (attendance was low) vs. *a few employees attended the
training* (some did attend).
:::

## Some, Most, Generally, and Typically

::: warning title="Quantifiers and frequency words never reach for the extreme automatically"
**Some** employees support the policy (a general subset) vs. **some
of** the employees support the policy (a specific, defined group).

**Most** = more than half, exact proportion unknown; weaker than
**almost all** = nearly everyone.

**Generally** ≠ **always** — *employees generally follow the
procedure* means *usually*, not *every single employee, every time*
(direct callback to Lesson 17's modality). **Typically** works the same
way: *the system typically processes requests within five minutes*
leaves room for exceptional cases. Don't read *generally, typically,
usually, often* as universal claims.
:::

## Context-Dependent Modal Verbs

::: example title="Can, may, would, should, must — each with several jobs"
**Can:** ability (*the system can process 1,000 requests per hour*),
general possibility (*errors can occur during data transfer*), or a
characteristic tendency (*poor communication can create delays* — not
*necessarily* creates delays).

**May:** possibility (*the policy may reduce costs*) or permission
(*employees may work remotely on Fridays*).

**Would:** hypothetical (*if the system were upgraded, it would perform
better*), reported expectation (*managers expected the system would
reduce costs*), past repeated behavior (*when demand was high,
employees would work late*), or polite/conditional language (*I would
suggest reviewing the data*).

**Should:** recommendation (*the company should improve its monitoring
system*) or expectation (*the shipment should arrive tomorrow*) — two
genuinely different meanings.

**Must:** obligation (*employees must follow the procedure*) or
deduction (*the system must have failed* — a strong inference, not a
rule).
:::

## Meaning Through Contrast, Restatement, and Example

::: concept title="Three patterns that hand you a definition without calling it one"
**Contrast:** *The policy is not ineffective; rather, its benefits are
limited to specific contexts.* Neither *"completely ineffective"* nor
*"universally effective"* — the contrast itself locates the actual
position: *partially useful.*

**Restatement:** signalled by *in other words, that is, namely,
specifically, more precisely, put differently, by this I mean.* *The
system lacks robustness; in other words, it performs poorly under
unexpected conditions* — the second half defines the first.

**Example:** signalled by *for example, for instance, such as,
including, particularly, specifically.* *The system has several
limitations, such as poor performance under high workloads* — the
example lets you infer what *"limitations"* means here.
:::

## Meaning Through Cause and Contrastive Definition

::: example title="Two more patterns worth recognising instantly"
**Cause-based definition:** *the system is considered unreliable
because it frequently produces inconsistent results* — *unreliable*
here contextually means *inconsistent/not dependable.*

**Contrastive definition (X, unlike Y):** *unlike a centralized
system, a decentralized system distributes decision-making across
multiple units* — *decentralized* is defined directly by contrast with
*centralized.* This *X, unlike Y* pattern is one of the most useful
vocabulary-inference tools available.
:::

## Seven Context Clues for an Unknown Word

::: framework title="What to look for around any unfamiliar word"
- Definition: "X means…"
- Synonym: "X, or…"
- Contrast: "unlike X…"
- Example: "for example…"
- Cause/effect: "X leads to…"
- Apposition: "X, a process that…"
- Repetition: the same concept restated differently in the next sentence
:::

::: example title="Three worked examples of context-clue inference"
**Definition clue:** *The system is highly resilient, meaning that it
can continue operating despite unexpected disruptions.* → *resilient =
able to withstand/recover from disruption.*

**Contrast clue:** *Unlike fragile systems, resilient systems continue
operating during unexpected disruptions.* → *resilient* is the opposite
of *fragile* in this context.

**Example clue:** *The company uses several redundant mechanisms, such
as backup servers and duplicate communication channels.* → *redundant
= additional/duplicate systems that provide backup.*

None of these required opening a dictionary.
:::

## When to Stop and Look Up a Word

::: important title="Not every unknown word deserves the same response"
Real book-reading breaks down if you stop for every unfamiliar word.
Use three tiers: **Tier 1 — ignore temporarily**, if the sentence still
makes sense without it. **Tier 2 — infer**, using context clues.
**Tier 3 — look it up**, only if the word recurs often, matters for the
main idea, or is genuinely necessary for following the argument.
:::

## Context Changes Meaning Across Disciplines

::: warning title="A term's everyday meaning and its technical meaning can be very different"
**Model:** business model, scientific model, AI language model, fashion
model, mathematical model — context determines the conceptual meaning
entirely.

**Theory:** everyday English treats *theory* as a *guess/idea*; in
academic contexts, *theory* means a *systematically developed
explanatory framework* — so *theory ≠ mere guess* when you're reading
science writing.

**Significant:** everyday = *important*; academic/statistical context
often means *statistically significant* — a technical meaning, not
automatically *"very important."*

**Bias:** everyday = *unfair preference*; in statistics/research =
*systematic deviation/error*; in machine learning, a related but
distinct technical sense depending on context.

**Validity:** everyday = *correctness/legitimacy*; in research =
*whether a measure or design supports its intended interpretation*; in
logic = *whether a conclusion follows from its premises* (Lesson 33).
Different fields, different technical meanings for the same word.
:::

## A Deep Reading Passage

::: example title="Read once, slowly, before checking the analysis below"
**The term "efficiency" is frequently used in discussions of automated
logistics systems, but its meaning is not always consistent. In some
studies, efficiency refers primarily to processing speed, whereas in
others it incorporates labor costs, error rates, resource utilization,
and service quality. Consequently, a system that processes transactions
more quickly cannot automatically be considered more efficient overall.
The interpretation depends on which dimensions of performance are
included in the measurement.**
:::

**Sentence 1** — flags that a key term's meaning isn't fixed.
**Sentence 2** — *whereas* contrasts a narrow definition (processing
speed) with a broader one (labor costs, error rates, utilization,
service quality). **Sentence 3** — *consequently*: because the
definition varies, *faster processing ≠ automatically more overall
efficiency.* **Sentence 4** — final clarification: meaning depends on
which measurement dimensions are included.

## Why This Matters for Critical Reading

::: important title="Never treat a technical term as having one universal meaning"
Especially watch: *efficiency, productivity, effectiveness,
performance, significant, risk, success, quality, reliability,
sustainability, innovation.* Each of these can be defined narrowly or
broadly, and a text's argument can shift underneath you if the
definition quietly shifts too.
:::

## Definition Drift and Equivocation

::: warning title="When a key term subtly changes meaning mid-argument, the logic breaks"
**Definition drift:** a text defines *efficiency = processing speed*
early on, then later treats *efficiency = overall profitability*,
then concludes *"therefore, the system improved efficiency"* — the
term shifted meaning without anyone noticing.

**Equivocation** is the named fallacy for exactly this pattern: **The
system is efficient because it processes data quickly. Efficient
systems reduce total operating costs. Therefore, this system reduces
total operating costs.** *Efficient* means *speed* in the first
sentence and *cost* in the second — a logical problem hiding inside a
single repeated word.
:::

## Ambiguity vs. Vagueness

::: concept title="Two or more distinct meanings, or one meaning with unclear edges"
**Ambiguity** — multiple *distinct* interpretations (*bank* = financial
institution or riverside). **Vagueness** — the meaning's boundary is
simply unclear (*efficient* — how efficient, exactly? *large company* —
how large, exactly?). Vague is not automatically ambiguous.

Vague language isn't automatically bad writing — words like
*approximately, generally, relatively, substantial, considerable* are
often used honestly, when an exact number simply isn't available. The
real question: *is the vagueness appropriate for the claim being
made?*
:::

## A Master Framework for Resolving Meaning

::: framework title="Working out a word's meaning, or an ambiguous sentence's best reading"
- Check the word's immediate collocation
- Check the whole sentence
- Check the surrounding paragraph
- Check for a contrast, example, or definition clue nearby
- Check which academic discipline or domain you're reading in
- Check the author's overall purpose
- Choose the most likely, best-supported meaning
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read this passage once for its overall meaning, then use context clues
to work out the meaning of each bolded word before checking the notes
below — don't look anything up until you've tried.

> Port authorities increasingly describe their operations in terms of
> **throughput**, meaning the volume of cargo a terminal can move
> through its gates and berths within a given period. A terminal's
> throughput is not the same as its **capacity**: capacity refers to
> the maximum volume the infrastructure could theoretically handle,
> whereas throughput reflects what is actually achieved once
> congestion, staffing, and equipment reliability are taken into
> account. A terminal can therefore have high capacity but
> **chronically** low throughput if these operational factors are
> poorly managed — a distinction that becomes especially important
> when comparing terminals of different sizes.

**Throughput** — defined directly (*"meaning the volume of cargo…
within a given period"*): a definition clue. **Capacity** — defined by
contrast with throughput (*"is not the same as… capacity refers to the
maximum volume… theoretically"*): a contrastive-definition clue.
**Chronically** — inferable from context as *persistently/consistently
over time*, supported by the surrounding claim that poor management
keeps throughput low on an ongoing basis, not just occasionally: a
cause/effect and repetition clue working together.

## Golden Rule

::: golden
Meaning is not always stored inside a word — it is often built by the words, sentence, paragraph, and discipline around it.
:::

## Lesson Summary

Today's lesson replaced a common but flawed habit — *"what's the Bangla
meaning of this word?"* — with a more powerful question: *"what does
this word mean here, and which words around it are doing the defining
work?"* You practised distinguishing polysemy from homonymy, resolving
structural, lexical, referential, and scope ambiguity using grammar and
context together, and reading quantifier pairs like *little/a little*
and *few/a few* precisely rather than interchangeably. You also learned
to watch for definition drift and equivocation — the same word quietly
changing meaning partway through an argument — and to use seven
concrete context-clue types to infer an unfamiliar word's meaning
without breaking your reading flow to reach for a dictionary.

::: important title="The question that replaces a dictionary lookup"
Before reaching for a dictionary, ask: *what does this word mean HERE?
Which words around it help define it?* And for an ambiguous sentence:
*what are the possible readings, and which one best fits the context?*
That habit is this entire lesson.
:::
