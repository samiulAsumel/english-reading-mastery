## Introduction

Today's goal is to notice when several ideas inside one sentence are
deliberately built from the same grammatical shape — a signal you'll
meet constantly in books, essays, reports, business writing, history,
philosophy, and technical documentation.

## What Is Parallel Structure?

::: concept title="Matching grammatical forms, side by side"
**Parallel structure** means putting similar grammatical elements into
the same grammatical form.

**She likes reading, writing, and speaking.**

All three: *reading, writing, speaking* — all V-ing. This is parallel.

**She likes reading, to write, and speaking.** — awkward, because the
forms don't match.
:::

## Why This Matters for Reading

::: example title="Matching forms make a list of ideas easy to spot"
**Good leaders need to communicate clearly, make difficult decisions,
and manage conflicts effectively.**

Three actions, all base verbs: *communicate, make, manage.* Once you
notice the matching shape, the sentence's logical structure — three
related duties — becomes immediately visible.
:::

## The Basic Pattern: X, Y, and Z

> reading, writing, and speaking
> planning, organizing, and managing
> knowledge, experience, and judgment
> analyze, compare, and evaluate
> identify problems, examine evidence, and reach conclusions

In the last example, each item is a full verb phrase: *identify
problems · examine evidence · reach conclusions.*

## Noun Parallelism

::: example title="Three nouns, one list of related concepts"
**The decision requires knowledge, experience, and judgment.**

Structure: noun + noun + noun. The author is listing three related
concepts.
:::

## Verb Parallelism

::: example title="Three parallel verb + object pairs"
**Researchers collect data, analyze evidence, and present findings.**

Three research actions: *collect data · analyze evidence · present
findings.*
:::

## Infinitive Parallelism

::: example title="to + verb, repeated — or with 'to' dropped after the first"
**The goal is to understand, to analyze, and to evaluate
information.**

The repeated *to* is often dropped: **The goal is to understand,
analyze, and evaluate information.** — still parallel.
:::

## Gerund Parallelism

::: example title="Four -ing forms, one list of activities"
**Learning a language requires reading, listening, speaking, and
writing.**

All four are V-ing — easy to spot as one related list of activities.
:::

## Adjective Parallelism

::: example title="Three adjectives at the same grammatical level"
**The system is fast, reliable, and efficient.**

*fast · reliable · efficient* — all adjectives, all describing the
system equally.
:::

## "Not Only … But Also …"

An important academic and formal pattern.

::: concept title="not only X but also Y = X, and Y too"
**Technology affects not only communication but also education.**

Meaning: not just X — Y as well.
:::

## Another Example

::: example title="Parallel nouns on either side"
**The policy affects not only employees but also managers.**

*employees* and *managers* — both nouns, at the same level.
:::

## With Verbs

::: example title="Two verb phrases, combined into one sentence"
**The system not only reduces costs but also improves efficiency.**

Two core ideas combined: *The system reduces costs.* + *The system
improves efficiency.*
:::

## "Both … And …"

::: example title="Two equally weighted items"
**The course develops both reading skills and vocabulary.**

**Good communication requires both clarity and accuracy.**

Both pairs are nouns, both equally weighted.
:::

## "Either … Or …"

::: example title="Two alternatives"
**You can either read the original book or listen to the audiobook.**

Two alternatives: *read the original book* or *listen to the
audiobook.*
:::

## "Neither … Nor …"

::: example title="Neither of two things is true"
**The explanation is neither simple nor complete.**

Parallel adjectives: *simple* and *complete.* Meaning: not X, and not Y
either.
:::

## "Whether … Or …"

Connecting back to Lesson 16's embedded questions:

::: example title="Two possible outcomes under examination"
**Researchers examined whether stress affects memory or improves
attention.**

Two alternative possibilities: *affects memory* vs. *improves
attention.*
:::

## Comparison: "More … Than"

::: example title="A > B"
**This method is more effective than the previous method.**

Meaning: Method A > Method B.
:::

## "Less … Than"

::: example title="A < B"
**The new system is less expensive than the old system.**

Meaning: the new system costs less.
:::

## "As … As"

::: example title="A = B"
**The new method is as effective as the old method.**

Meaning: the two are at the same level of effectiveness.
:::

## The Negative "As … As" — Read Carefully

::: important title="not as...as reverses the whole comparison"
**The new method is not as effective as the old method.**

Meaning: new method < old method.

*as effective as* and *not as effective as* are exact opposites — this
is a genuinely easy detail to miss while reading quickly.
:::

## Comparison Beyond Adjectives

::: example title="Verbs can be compared too"
**Researchers in Country A conduct more studies than researchers in
Country B.**

Here *more studies than* compares quantity, not quality.
:::

## Noun Comparison

> This approach requires more time than the previous approach. · The
> new system uses less energy than the old system. · The study
> involved as many participants as the earlier experiment.

**Patterns:** more + noun · less + noun · as many + plural noun + as.

## "Compared With / Compared To"

::: example title="A formal comparison signal"
**Compared with previous studies, the new research provides stronger
evidence.**

Meaning: *relative to previous studies…* Treat this phrase as a signal
that a comparison is coming.
:::

## "In Contrast"

Connecting back to Lesson 8:

::: example title="Setting up an explicit contrast"
**The first method is relatively simple. In contrast, the second
method is highly complex.**

Method 1 vs. Method 2 — the author is contrasting them deliberately.
:::

## "Similarly"

::: example title="Signalling a shared pattern"
**The first study found a strong relationship. Similarly, the second
study reported a similar pattern.**
:::

## "Unlike"

::: example title="Unlike X, Y..."
**Unlike traditional systems, digital platforms can process information
rapidly.**

Meaning: in contrast to X, Y is true.
:::

## "Whereas"

::: example title="A formal contrast connector"
**Traditional systems depend on manual processes, whereas digital
systems rely on automation.**

Two sides: traditional systems → manual processes. Digital systems →
automation. *whereas* signals the contrast.
:::

## "While" — Two Meanings

::: important title="Time or contrast — check which"
**Time:** While I was reading, he called me. — *while* = *during the
time that.*

**Contrast:** While traditional systems are reliable, they can be
expensive. — *while* = *although/on the other hand.*

Context decides which reading applies — the same warning as in Lesson
8.
:::

## "Rather Than"

::: example title="Prioritizing one thing over another"
**The researchers focused on evidence rather than assumptions.**

Meaning: evidence was prioritized, not assumptions.

**The company chose to improve the existing system rather than replace
it.** — two alternative actions: *improve* vs. *replace.*
:::

## "Instead Of"

::: example title="A pattern connected to this course's own reading strategy"
**Instead of translating every word, try to understand the sentence as
a whole.**

**Structure:** instead of + noun / V-ing. ❌ *instead of to translate* —
always the -ing form here, never the infinitive.
:::

## "The More…, The More…"

::: concept title="An advanced but very useful correlation pattern"
**The more you read, the more vocabulary you recognize.**

**Structure:** The more X, the more Y.

**The more complex the sentence, the more carefully you should analyze
its structure.**
:::

## "The Less…, The Less…"

::: example title="Both sides decreasing together"
**The less you rely on translation, the faster you will understand
English directly.**

Less reliance on translation → faster direct comprehension.
:::

## "The More…, The Less…"

::: example title="An inverse relationship"
**The more information people receive, the less certain they may
become.**

Here the relationship runs in opposite directions: more X → less Y.
:::

## "Not Only … But Also …" + Parallel Structure, Combined

::: example title="Two main verbs, both parallel"
**Good readers not only recognize individual words but also understand
how those words function within larger structures.**

*not only* → recognize individual words. *but also* → understand how
those words function within larger structures. Two main verbs:
*recognize* and *understand* — this is parallelism at work.
:::

## A Long Sentence, Decoded

::: example title="A nested list inside the second half of a not only...but also"
**Effective communication requires not only the ability to express
ideas clearly but also the willingness to listen carefully, consider
alternative perspectives, and respond appropriately.**

Main subject: *Effective communication.* Main verb: *requires.* Object:
*not only X but also Y.*

X: *the ability to express ideas clearly.*
Y: *the willingness to listen carefully, consider alternative
perspectives, and respond appropriately* — and inside Y, three more
parallel verbs: *listen, consider, respond.*

**Core:** Communication requires ability and willingness. Then the
details attach.
:::

## Another Complex Example

::: example title="Four parallel actions after one main verb"
**Successful organizations need to identify problems, evaluate
alternatives, make informed decisions, and implement effective
solutions.**

Main subject: *Successful organizations.* Main verb: *need.*
Complement: *to identify…*, followed by four parallel actions:
*identify problems · evaluate alternatives · make informed decisions ·
implement effective solutions.*
:::

## Parallel Structure + Nominalization

Combining with Lesson 18:

::: example title="Four parallel noun phrases, each hiding an action"
**The successful implementation of a policy requires careful planning,
effective communication, continuous evaluation, and appropriate
adjustment.**

Four parallel noun phrases: *careful planning · effective communication
· continuous evaluation · appropriate adjustment.*

Hidden actions: *plan carefully · communicate effectively · evaluate
continuously · adjust appropriately.*
:::

## Comparison + Nominalization

::: example title="Avoiding repetition with 'that of'"
**The effectiveness of the new system is significantly greater than
that of the previous system.**

*effectiveness of the new system* vs. *that of the previous system* —
*that* stands in for *effectiveness*, avoiding repetition.

Simple meaning: the new system is more effective than the previous
system.
:::

## "That Of" and "Those Of"

::: important title="A very common academic device for comparing two noun phrases"
**Singular:** The population of Germany is larger than that of
Denmark. — *that* = population. Meaning: Germany's population >
Denmark's population.

**Plural:** The economic conditions of developed countries differ from
those of developing countries. — *those* = economic conditions.

This connects directly to Lesson 10's reference tracking — *that/those*
here are simply pronouns standing in for a repeated noun.
:::

## "Respectively"

::: example title="Matching two lists, in order"
**John and Sarah scored 80 and 90, respectively.**

Meaning: John → 80. Sarah → 90 — matched in the order given.

**The two systems require 4 GB and 8 GB of memory, respectively.**

System 1 → 4 GB. System 2 → 8 GB.
:::

## "The Former / The Latter"

::: example title="Referring back to two previously named things"
**Technology and education are closely related. The former influences
the latter.**

*former* = the first one mentioned = technology. *latter* = the second
one = education. Whenever you meet these words, look back for the two
most recently named nouns.
:::

## Comparison's Logical Meaning

Not every comparison means the same kind of thing:

| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| Quantity | more resources than |
| Degree | more effective than |
| Frequency | more frequently than |
| Time | earlier than |
| Importance | more important than |
| Similarity | as important as |
| Difference | different from |

## "More" Doesn't Always Mean "Better"

::: important title="Don't add interpretation the sentence doesn't state"
**The new system generates more data than the old system.** — only a
quantity claim. This does *not* automatically mean *the new system is
better.*

**The new process takes more time.** — only a time claim. Never quietly
add your own interpretation on top of what a comparison actually
states.
:::

## "Higher" vs. "More"

::: note title="A recognisable adjective pattern"
> higher costs · higher performance · higher quality

but

> more effective · more reliable · more efficient

The rule behind the pattern: **higher** pairs with a noun (*higher
costs*), while **more** pairs with an adjective or adverb (*more
effective*). Recognising which one a word needs is a small but
genuinely helpful reading habit.
:::

## "Increasingly"

Connecting to Lesson 9:

::: example title="A trend over time, not a fixed intensity"
**Technology is becoming increasingly important.**

Meaning: importance is growing over time. *increasingly complex ·
increasingly difficult · increasingly common* — this signals a trend,
not the same thing as a plain *very.*
:::

## "Relatively"

Connecting back to Lesson 9: you met *relatively* there as a degree
word; here it's the comparison itself that matters.

::: example title="A comparison-dependent claim, once more"
**The new system is relatively simple compared with the old system.**

Meaning: simple *by comparison* — not an absolute claim.
:::

## A Reading Strategy for Parallel Structures

::: framework title="Five steps for any sentence built from parallel elements"
- Notice which parts are separated by commas
- Look for and / or / but / both / either / neither / not only ... but also
- Check the grammatical form on each side
- Find the core verb or noun in each item
- If there's a comparison, write it out as X vs. Y
:::

## A Mental Diagram for Comparison

::: example title="Visualising a multi-dimensional comparison"
**The new approach is cheaper, faster, and more reliable than the old
approach.**

```
The old approach is the baseline.
The new approach beats it on three separate dimensions:
  - cost:        cheaper
  - speed:       faster
  - reliability: more reliable
```

**Core:** New approach beats the old approach across three separate
dimensions.
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read the paragraph once, slowly, spotting every parallel structure and
comparison.

> Modern organizations increasingly rely on digital technologies to
> improve efficiency, reduce costs, and support communication. These
> technologies not only allow employees to access information more
> quickly but also enable them to collaborate across geographical
> boundaries. Compared with traditional communication systems, digital
> platforms can provide greater flexibility and faster access to
> information. However, greater technological capability does not
> necessarily lead to better organizational performance. Effective
> organizations must not only adopt appropriate technologies but also
> develop the skills, processes, and policies required to use them
> effectively. The more complex the technology becomes, the more
> important training and organizational support may become.

**1** — Three parallel verbs: *improve efficiency · reduce costs ·
support communication.* Core: organizations use technology to do three
things.

**2** — *not only allow…* but also *enable…* — two parallel verbs.

**3** — *Compared with traditional systems…* → comparison signal.
Digital platforms: *greater flexibility · faster access.*

**4** — *does not necessarily lead to better performance* — Lesson
17's hedge: greater capability doesn't guarantee better performance.

**5** — *must not only adopt…* but also *develop…* — parallel verbs
again.

**6** — *The more complex… the more important…* — a correlation:
complexity ↑, importance of training/support ↑.

## Golden Rule

::: golden
Whenever you see and, or, but, both, either, neither, or not only...but also, check that every joined item shares the same grammatical shape. That shared shape is exactly what tells you they're meant to be read as one list of equal, related ideas.
:::

## Lesson Summary

Today you learned to spot parallel structure — matching grammatical
forms joined by *and, or, but,* or the correlative pairs *both…and,
either…or, neither…nor,* and *not only…but also* — as a signal of a
deliberate list of equal, related ideas. You practised reading *more…
than, less…than,* and *as…as* accurately, including the easily-missed
negative *not as…as*, told *while's* two meanings apart, met *that of /
those of* as a device for comparing noun phrases without repetition,
and learned not to read extra meaning (like "better") into a plain
quantity comparison.

## Practice: Test What You've Learned

Work through every question yourself before checking anything.

::: important title="Before you start"
Do not use Google Translate or any other translation tool for these
questions. Missing a hidden "not" in a comparison (as vs. not as) is an
easy, natural mistake — read each comparison twice before answering.
:::

### Part A — Identify the Parallel Elements

1. Reading, writing, and speaking are essential language skills. — which three elements are parallel?
2. Good managers need to plan carefully, communicate clearly, and make informed decisions. — what are the three parallel verb phrases?
3. The system is fast, reliable, and efficient. — what are the three parallel adjectives?

### Part B — Comparison

4. Explain the meaning of: The new system is more efficient than the old system.
5. Explain the meaning of: The new method is not as reliable as the previous method.
6. Explain the meaning of: The more you practice, the more confident you become.

### Part C — Sentence Decoding

7. Decode this sentence: **Successful organizations must not only identify operational problems but also evaluate alternative solutions, make informed decisions, and implement effective strategies.** Identify: the main subject, the main verb, the "not only … but also" structure, every parallel verb, and the object/complement.

### Part D — Advanced Reading

8. Decode this sentence: **The effectiveness of modern communication technologies depends not only on their technical capabilities but also on the ability of users to understand, adapt to, and effectively apply them in different organizational contexts.** Identify: the main subject, the main verb, "not only X but also Y," the X part, the Y part, the parallel verbs inside Y, what "them" refers to, and what "in different organizational contexts" modifies.

### Part E — Direct Comprehension

9. Explain, in your own words, the meaning of: **The availability of more information does not necessarily result in better decisions; rather, the ability to evaluate, compare, and interpret that information may be more important.** Pay particular attention to *does not necessarily*, *rather*, *evaluate, compare, and interpret*, and *may be* — together, these build the author's actual logical position.
