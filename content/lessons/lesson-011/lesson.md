## Introduction

You now have a full toolkit: Subject → Verb → Object, phrase versus
clause, prepositions, conjunctions, adjectives and adverbs, and pronoun
reference. Today, all of it gets used together, to answer one question:
how does an author pack so much information into a single sentence, and
how do you take it apart?

## What Is a Relative Clause?

A relative clause gives additional information about a noun. The common
relative words: *who, which, that, whose, where, when.*

::: concept title="A relative clause attaches to one specific noun"
**The researcher who conducted the study published the results.**

Main sentence: *The researcher published the results.* Additional
information: *who conducted the study* — describing *researcher*.
:::

## WHO

::: example title="who — for people"
**The manager who leads the team has extensive experience.**

Main noun: *manager.* Relative clause: *who leads the team* — meaning,
the manager that leads the team.
:::

## WHICH

::: example title="which — for things, objects, ideas, studies, systems"
**The system which we developed reduced processing time.**

Main noun: *system.* Relative clause: *which we developed.*
:::

## THAT

*that* can introduce a relative clause for either people or things:

> The book that I bought is useful. · The researcher that conducted the
> study…

::: note title="who is still the more natural choice for people in formal writing"
Both are grammatically fine, but *who* reads more naturally when the
head noun is a person, especially in formal English.
:::

## WHOSE

::: example title="whose — possession or relationship"
**The researcher whose study changed the field became famous.** — whose
study? *the researcher's.*

**The company whose employees receive regular training has better
performance.** — *whose* links *company* to *employees* as a
relationship of belonging.
:::

## WHERE

::: example title="where — a place or location"
**The city where I was born is beautiful.**

Main noun: *city.* Relative clause: *where I was born.*

Technical writing uses it just as naturally: **The environment where
people work can affect productivity.**
:::

## WHEN

::: example title="when — a time or period"
**The year when the company was founded was important.**

Main noun: *year.* Relative clause: *when the company was founded.*

**There are periods when people experience significant stress.**
:::

## The Basic Patterns

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| noun + who + verb | The researcher who studied the problem… |
| noun + which + subject + verb | The system which the company developed… |
| noun + that + subject + verb | The method that researchers used… |
| noun + whose + noun + verb | The researcher whose work influenced psychology… |
| noun + where + subject + verb | The environment where people work… |

## Subject Relative Clause vs. Object Relative Clause

::: important title="Check what comes right after the relative word"
**A — subject relative clause:** The researcher who conducted the study
published the results.

*who conducted* — *who* is the **subject**, because *who* itself is the
one doing the conducting.

**B — object relative clause:** The study that the researcher conducted
was successful.

*that the researcher conducted* — *that* is the **object**, because
inside this clause the subject is *the researcher*, the verb is
*conducted*, and *the study* is what was conducted.
:::

## The Decoding Trick

::: framework title="What follows the relative word tells you its job"
- Relative word + verb directly → the relative word is the subject
- Relative word + noun + verb → the relative word is the object
:::

**who conducted the study** — *who* + verb → subject.
**which the researcher conducted** — *which* + noun + verb → object.

## Removing a Relative Clause to Find the Core

::: example title="Set the relative clause aside first"
**The researcher who conducted the experiment in 2025 published the
findings.**

Remove the relative clause: **The researcher published the findings.**
— that's the core. Then reattach: *who conducted the experiment in
2025* — extra information about the researcher.
:::

## A Sentence With Two Relative Clauses

::: example title="Each relative clause attaches to its own noun"
**The researcher who conducted the experiment in 2025 published the
findings that challenged several widely accepted assumptions.**

Core: *The researcher published the findings.*

*who conducted the experiment in 2025* → describes *researcher.*
*that challenged several widely accepted assumptions* → describes
*findings.*

```
The researcher
   ↓ who conducted the experiment
published
   ↓
the findings
   ↓ that challenged several assumptions
```
:::

## Nested Relative Clauses

::: example title="A relative clause can sit inside another relative clause's noun"
**The researcher who conducted the experiment that examined
decision-making published the findings.**

Main noun: *researcher.* Relative clause 1: *who conducted the
experiment…* → describes *researcher.* Relative clause 2: *that examined
decision-making* → describes *experiment*, which is itself inside
relative clause 1.

```
researcher
   ↓ who conducted
experiment
   ↓ that examined
decision-making
```
:::

## A Method for Finding the Core

::: framework title="Six steps for any long, layered sentence"
- Find the main noun
- Find the main verb
- Temporarily remove every relative clause
- Temporarily remove every prepositional phrase
- Read the main sentence that's left
- Add the details back in, one at a time
:::

## A Fully Worked Example

::: example title="Every layer of a genuinely long sentence"
**The experienced manager who joined the company after completing an
advanced business program carefully reviewed the financial report that
the accounting department had prepared for the board.**

**Main subject:** manager. **Main verb:** reviewed. **Object:** report.

**Core:** The manager reviewed the report.

Then the layers: *who joined the company* → manager · *after completing
an advanced business program* → the timing/context of joining · *that
the accounting department had prepared* → report · *for the board* →
the purpose/recipient of that preparation.

**Final meaning, with every layer back in place:** An experienced
manager, who had joined the company after completing an advanced
business program, carefully reviewed the financial report that the
accounting department had prepared for the board.
:::

## Finding the Main Verb Among Several Verb Forms

::: important title="Only one verb belongs to the main subject"
The sentence above contains four verb-related forms: *joined,
completing, reviewed, had prepared.* Only **reviewed** is the main
verb — because it's the one action performed directly by the main
subject, *manager.* The rest belong to their own clauses or phrases.
:::

## Finite Verb vs. -ing Form

::: concept title="An -ing form is not automatically a main verb"
**The manager reviewed the report after completing the meeting.**

Main verb: *reviewed.* *completing* is an -ing form inside a
prepositional phrase — not the sentence's main, finite verb.

**The manager who completed the meeting reviewed the report.**

Here *completed* is the verb of the relative clause; the main verb is
still *reviewed.*
:::

## Past Perfect Inside a Relative Clause

::: example title="had prepared marks the earlier of two past events"
**The report that the team had prepared was submitted yesterday.**

Main: *The report was submitted yesterday.* Relative clause: *that the
team had prepared* — Past Perfect, because the preparation happened
before the submission.
:::

## Commas and Two Kinds of Relative Clause

::: concept title="Essential vs. non-essential information"
**Essential:** The employees who completed the training received
certificates. — *who completed the training* narrows down exactly which
employees; it's essential to identifying them.

**Non-essential:** John, who completed the training, received a
certificate. — *who completed the training* is extra detail; *John* is
already fully identified without it.
:::

## A Non-Essential Relative Clause in Practice

::: example title="Commas often mark added, not identifying, information"
**The company, which was founded in 1995, now operates
internationally.**

Core: *The company now operates internationally.* Extra: *which was
founded in 1995.*
:::

::: note title="A callback to Lesson 10's 'comma + which'"
Recall from Lesson 10: **The researchers repeated the experiment, which
produced similar results.** A comma before *which* can signal that it's
commenting on the whole preceding event, not just the nearest noun —
exactly the same pattern seen here.
:::

## Relative Clause vs. Noun Clause

::: important title="A relative clause describes a noun; a noun clause acts as one"
**Relative clause:** The book that I bought is useful. — *that I
bought* describes *book* — which book?

**Noun clause:** I know that the book is useful. — *that the book is
useful* isn't describing any noun; it's acting as the entire object of
*know*.
:::

## Relative Clause vs. Adverb Clause

::: example title="Three different jobs, one similar-looking shape"
**Relative:** The researcher who conducted the study… — information
about *researcher.*

**Reason:** The researcher stopped because the equipment failed. — why
did they stop? Because the equipment failed.

**Time:** The researcher stopped when the equipment failed. — when did
they stop? When the equipment failed.
:::

## Everything at Once

::: example title="A single sentence carrying five different structures"
**The researchers who conducted the study in several hospitals found
that patients who received regular support showed better outcomes
because they were more likely to follow the recommended treatment.**

**Main subject:** researchers. **Relative clause:** *who conducted the
study in several hospitals* → describes researchers. **Main verb:**
found. **Noun clause** (the object of *found*): *that patients… showed
better outcomes.* Inside it, a second **relative clause**: *who received
regular support* → describes patients. **Reason clause:** *because they
were more likely…* — *they* → patients.

```
Researchers
   ↓ who conducted the study
found
   ↓
that patients
   ↓ who received regular support
showed better outcomes
   ↓ because they were more likely
to follow the treatment
```

**Core:** Researchers found that patients showed better outcomes. Every
other piece layers information onto that backbone, one clause at a
time.
:::

## This Is How You Read Difficult Books

::: framework title="The complete decoding engine, built across this course so far"
- Main Subject
- Main Verb
- Main Object or Complement
- Relative Clause(s)
- Reason / Time / Condition Clause(s)
- Prepositional Phrases
- Modifiers
:::

A forty- or sixty-word sentence is never one impossible problem — it's
this same short list, applied once.

## Guided Reading Practice

Read the paragraph once, slowly, finding each sentence's backbone
before its relative clauses.

> Researchers who study human decision-making often examine the factors
> that influence the choices people make in situations where information
> is incomplete. Although individuals may believe that they are making
> rational decisions, the experiences they have had in the past can
> affect the way they interpret new information. This is particularly
> important when people are faced with complex problems that require
> them to evaluate several competing alternatives.

**Sentence 1** — Core: *Researchers examine factors.* Extra: *who study
human decision-making* → researchers · *that influence the choices
people make* → factors · *in situations where information is incomplete*
→ situations.

**Sentence 2** — Core: *the experiences can affect the way.* Full
structure: *Although individuals may believe…* (contrast) · *the
experiences they have had in the past* (relative clause on
*experiences*) · *can affect the way they interpret new information*
(the effect itself).

**Sentence 3** — Core: *This is important.* Additional: *particularly*
(emphasis) · *when people are faced with complex problems* (time/context)
· *that require them to evaluate several alternatives* (describes
*problems*).

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="conduct" pos="verb" meaning="to carry out or organize an activity (পরিচালনা করা)" example="The team conducted a study across several hospitals."
:::

::: vocabulary word="interpret" pos="verb" meaning="to explain or understand the meaning of something (ব্যাখ্যা করা/অর্থ বোঝা)" example="People interpret new information differently depending on past experience."
:::

::: vocabulary word="evaluate" pos="verb" meaning="to judge the value or quality of something carefully (মূল্যায়ন করা)" example="Readers must evaluate several competing alternatives before deciding."
:::

::: vocabulary word="alternative" pos="noun" meaning="one option among two or more possible choices (বিকল্প)" example="The report considered several alternatives before reaching a conclusion."
:::

::: vocabulary word="outcome" pos="noun" meaning="the result or effect of an action or process (ফলাফল)" example="Patients who received support showed a better outcome."
:::

::: vocabulary word="challenge" pos="verb" meaning="to question whether something is true or correct (প্রশ্নবিদ্ধ করা)" example="The new findings challenge a widely accepted assumption."
:::

::: vocabulary word="assumption" pos="noun" meaning="something accepted as true without being proven (অনুমান/ধারণা)" example="The experiment was designed to test a common assumption."
:::

::: note title="Chunks worth learning as whole units"
*conduct a study · examine a factor · influence a choice · make a
decision · interpret information · evaluate alternatives · receive
support · achieve an outcome · provide evidence · challenge an
assumption · require someone to do something · be faced with a problem ·
be associated with · be influenced by*

Especially useful: **be faced with** — *People are often faced with
difficult decisions.*
:::

## Golden Rule

::: golden
However many clauses stack up in a sentence, it has exactly one backbone — find the main subject and main verb first, then attach every clause to whichever noun or idea it actually describes.
:::

Never translate a long sentence word by word. Instead, work through it
in order: What is the main subject? What is the main verb? What is the
object or complement? Which information describes which noun? Which
clause explains a reason, time, or condition? Only then: what is the
complete meaning?

## Lesson Summary

Today you combined everything this phase has taught you into a single
decoding skill. You learned all five relative words and the pattern each
one follows, how to tell a subject relative clause from an object
relative clause, how commas distinguish essential from non-essential
information, and how a relative clause differs from both a noun clause
and a reason/time adverb clause. Most importantly, you practised finding
the one backbone inside a genuinely long, nested sentence — the skill
that makes a forty- or sixty-word sentence from a real book feel exactly
as manageable as a short one.

### Part A — Identify the Relative Clause

1. The researcher who conducted the study published the results. — which words make up the relative clause?
2. The book that I bought yesterday explains human behavior. — which words make up the relative clause?
3. The company whose employees receive regular training has better performance. — what relationship does *whose* show?
4. The city where the company was founded has changed significantly. — which noun is *where* connected to?

### Part B — Find the Core Sentence

5. The experienced manager who joined the organization last year carefully reviewed the financial report that the accounting department had prepared. — what is the core sentence?
6. Researchers who study human behavior often examine social factors that influence people's decisions. — what is the core sentence?

### Part C — Identify the Main Verb

7. The researcher who conducted the experiment analyzed the results carefully. — what is the main verb?
8. The report that the team had prepared was submitted yesterday. — what is the main verb, and which clause does *had prepared* belong to?

9.
