## Introduction

Today's goal is to quickly identify exactly who, or which idea, an
author is talking about. You can know every word in a sentence and still
find it hard to follow:

> People develop beliefs from their experiences, and these can strongly
> influence their decisions.

Two real questions sit inside that sentence: *these* — what, exactly? and
*their* — whose? Both point back to something already said. Today we
learn to resolve these questions systematically.

## What Is a Pronoun?

A pronoun stands in for a noun or noun phrase, so a writer doesn't have
to keep repeating it.

**Personal:** I, you, he, she, it, we, they
**Demonstrative:** this, that, these, those
**Relative:** who, which, that
**Possessive:** my, your, his, her, its, our, their

## Why Do Writers Use Pronouns?

::: example title="Pronouns replace repetition with a chain of references"
**Repetitive:** The researcher conducted an experiment. The researcher
analyzed the experiment. The researcher published the researcher's
findings.

**Natural:** The researcher conducted an experiment. He analyzed it and
published his findings.

*he* → researcher · *it* → experiment · *his* → researcher.

That chain of connections — *he, it, his,* each pointing back to
something already named — is exactly what **reference tracking** means.
:::

## What Is a Reference?

The noun or idea a pronoun points back to is called its **antecedent**,
or simply its **reference**.

> **The manager made a decision because he had enough information.** —
> *he* → the manager.
> **The manager reviewed the report because it contained important
> information.** — *it* → the report.

## The First Rule of Reading a Pronoun

::: important title="Don't translate a pronoun before you resolve it"
Before reaching for a Bangla equivalent, always ask first: **what does
this pronoun refer to?** Build this into a genuine habit — it matters
more than the pronoun's dictionary meaning ever will.
:::

## HE / SHE

::: example title="Personal pronouns for people"
**The manager explained the problem because he understood it clearly.**
— *he* → manager.

Books also very commonly use singular *they* for gender-neutral
reference: **A person may change their opinion after receiving new
information.** — *their* → a person.
:::

## THEY

::: example title="Plural — and sometimes singular"
**Researchers conducted several experiments. They found interesting
results.** — *they* → researchers (plural).

**If a student wants to improve, they need regular practice.** — *they*
→ a student (singular they). Context tells you which reading applies.
:::

## IT

::: concept title="it can point to a noun, a whole situation, or nothing at all"
Most simply, *it* refers to a singular thing, situation, idea, or
previously mentioned concept:

**The book is difficult, but it is useful.** — *it* → book.

But *it* can also point to an entire situation rather than one noun:

**People often make emotional decisions. It can be difficult to
recognize this tendency.** — here, context decides exactly what *it*
covers.

There's also a **dummy it**, which doesn't stand in for any noun at all:

**It is important to practice.** — the real subject is *To practice*;
*it* is simply a grammatical placeholder. We'll look at this more
closely later in the course.
:::

## THIS

::: example title="this can point to a noun — or to a whole preceding idea"
**This book is useful.** — *this* is directly attached to *book*.

**People often make decisions emotionally. This can lead to
mistakes.** — here *this* refers to the whole previous idea: *making
decisions emotionally.*
:::

## THAT

::: example title="that as a reference, and that as a relative pronoun"
**The researcher rejected the explanation. That surprised many
people.** — *that* → the researcher's act of rejecting the explanation.

**The method that the researchers developed was highly effective.** —
here *that* is a relative pronoun; it refers to *method*.
:::

## THIS vs. THESE

::: example title="Singular vs. plural"
**This:** The study produced an unexpected result. This surprised
researchers. — singular.

**These:** The study produced several unexpected results. These
surprised researchers. — plural.
:::

## THAT vs. THOSE

::: important title="those can also stand in for a previously mentioned plural noun"
**That:** That idea is interesting. — singular.
**Those:** Those ideas are interesting. — plural.

Academic writing often uses *those* this way:

**The opinions of younger employees may differ from those of older
employees.** — *those* → opinions. This exact structure — *X of A…
those of B* — is extremely common in academic comparisons, so it's worth
recognising on sight.
:::

## Possessive Pronouns

> my · your · his · her · its · our · their

::: example title="Possessives point back to an owner"
**Researchers analyzed their data.** — *their* → researchers.
**The company changed its policy.** — *its* → company.
**People often defend their beliefs.** — *their* → people.
:::

## The Most Important Rule: Match the Number

::: important title="Singular pronoun, singular antecedent; plural pronoun, plural antecedent"
**The researcher** → he/she/they · **The company** → it
**researchers** → they · **companies** → they

**The researcher published their findings.** — singular *they* is
possible here.
**The researchers published their findings.** — plural *they* here.

Context tells you which reading is intended — but checking number first
narrows the candidates dramatically.
:::

## WHO

::: example title="who describes a person"
**The researcher who conducted the study published the results.**

Main noun: *researcher.* Relative clause: *who conducted the study* —
meaning, the researcher that carried out the study.
:::

## WHICH

::: example title="which for things, animals, or ideas"
**The study which examined human behavior produced interesting
results.** — *which* → study.
:::

## THAT, Once Again

*that* can introduce a relative clause for either people or things:

> The researcher that conducted the study… · The study that examined
> human behavior…

In formal writing, though, *who* is the more natural choice for people.

## WHOSE

::: example title="whose shows possession or relationship"
**The researcher whose work influenced the field received an award.** —
*whose* → the researcher's (possession/relationship). Meaning: the
researcher, and it was *their* work that influenced the field.

**The company whose products are widely used expanded rapidly.** —
*whose* → company.
:::

## Relative Clauses, Reconnected to Reference Tracking

::: example title="A relative pronoun is itself a reference"
**The book that I bought yesterday explains human behavior.**

Main sentence: *The book explains human behavior.* Relative clause:
*that I bought yesterday.* Reference: *that* → book.
:::

## When "Which" Refers to a Whole Preceding Idea

::: important title="which doesn't always point at the nearest noun"
**The researchers repeated the experiment, which produced similar
results.**

Here, *which* likely refers not to the nearest noun (*the experiment*)
but to the entire preceding event: *researchers repeated the
experiment.* Only context can settle exactly how much of the previous
clause *which* is picking up.
:::

## A Four-Step Method for Resolving a Reference

::: framework title="Resolving a pronoun's reference"
- Identify the pronoun (this / they / it / their / which...)
- Look back at what came before it
- Find the most likely candidate noun or idea
- Test it: does substituting this candidate make the sentence logical?
:::

::: example title="Testing candidates against logic"
**Researchers studied several communities. They found that social
relationships strongly affected behavior.**

Candidates for *they*: *communities* or *researchers*.

Test: *Communities found...* — doesn't make sense. *Researchers
found...* — logical. So **they → researchers.**
:::

::: example title="A second worked test"
**Researchers studied several communities. These showed significant
differences in social behavior.**

Test: *communities showed differences* — logical. So **these →
communities.**
:::

## THIS vs. THEY, Compared

::: important title="they picks up an entity; this often picks up an event"
**They** usually points to a plural entity: **Researchers conducted a
study. They analyzed the data.** — *they* → researchers.

**This** often points to the previous idea or event itself: **Researchers
conducted a study. This provided new evidence.** — *this* → the act of
conducting the study, or the study itself as an event.
:::

## THEIR vs. ITS

::: example title="Plural organization vs. singular organization"
**The researchers changed their approach.** — *researchers* is plural →
*their.*

**The organization changed its approach.** — *organization* is singular
→ *its.*
:::

## Pronouns Combined With Logical Connectors

Connecting back to Lesson 8:

::: example title="Logic and reference, read together"
**Researchers found unexpected results. Therefore, they repeated the
experiment.**

*Therefore* signals the logic (a conclusion/result); *they* signals the
reference (→ researchers). Reading a sentence well means catching both
at once.
:::

## A Complex Worked Example

::: example title="Multiple references inside one sentence"
**Researchers who study human behavior often examine social
environments because these can strongly influence the way people think
and act.**

Main subject: *Researchers* · relative clause: *who study human
behavior* (*who* → researchers) · main verb: *examine* · object: *social
environments* · reason clause: *because these can strongly influence…*
(*these* → social environments) · *the way people think and act* is
itself a noun phrase, followed by its own clause.
:::

## An Even More Complex Example

::: example title="A whole chain of references in a single sentence"
**People develop beliefs from their experiences, and these beliefs can
influence how they interpret information, which may affect the
decisions they make.**

*their* → people's · *these beliefs* → the beliefs just mentioned · *how
they interpret information* → a noun clause, *they* → people · *which*
→ the previous idea (people's beliefs influencing how they interpret
information) · *they make* → *they* → people.

One sentence, several linked references — reading it well means walking
this chain one link at a time.
:::

## A Reading Trick: The Pronoun-Replacement Test

::: important title="When confused, substitute the noun back in"
**Original:** People develop beliefs from experience, and these can
influence decisions.

**Test:** People develop beliefs from experience, and these **beliefs**
can influence decisions.

The meaning becomes immediately clear. You don't need to know the formal
name for this — call it the **pronoun replacement test** — but practise
it deliberately whenever a reference feels unclear.
:::

::: note title="Chunks worth learning as whole units"
*refer to something · describe something · represent something ·
indicate something · identify something · distinguish between · relate
to · be associated with · depend on · be based on · point to · lead to ·

Especially useful: **refer to** — *The word "this" refers to the
previous idea.*
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read the paragraph once, slowly, tracing every pronoun back to the noun
it stands for.

> A researcher conducted a long-term study of decision-making. The
> researcher collected information from several groups of participants
> and compared their responses over time. These results showed that
> people often changed their decisions when their circumstances changed.
> This suggests that decision-making is influenced not only by personal
> beliefs but also by external conditions. However, the researcher noted
> that these findings should be interpreted carefully because the study
> had several limitations.

Tracking every reference:

**The researcher** → the same researcher throughout.
**their responses** → participants.
**These results** → the results of the comparison just described.
**their circumstances** → people.
**This suggests** → the previous finding, as a whole idea.
**these findings** → the study's results.
**the study** → the long-term study mentioned at the start.

The author never repeats the same noun twice in a row — instead, a whole
system of pronouns carries the reader from sentence to sentence.

## The Reference Chain, Visualised

```
A researcher
   ↓
The researcher
   ↓
their responses
   ↓
These results
   ↓
This suggests
   ↓
these findings
   ↓
the study
```

That's what reference tracking actually looks like across a real
paragraph.

## Why This Matters for Reading Real Books

::: note title="Vocabulary alone is not enough"
In psychology, technology, business, history, or science writing, an
author will rarely repeat the same noun sentence after sentence. A
single concept might travel through a paragraph like this: *system → it
→ this system → its → which → these → they.* Lose track of that chain,
and you can lose the paragraph's meaning even while understanding every
individual word.
:::

## A Reminder Worth Repeating

If a pronoun's reference isn't immediately obvious, don't panic —
return to the same order every time: **pronoun → nearby noun → previous
sentence → previous idea → logical test.** This is exactly the four-step
method from earlier in this lesson; it's worth having ready as an
automatic habit, not something to work out fresh each time.

## A Mini Master Example

::: example title="A full reference chain, tracked start to finish"
**The company introduced a new system to improve efficiency. It was
initially difficult for employees to use, but they gradually became
familiar with it. This improvement allowed the organization to reduce
processing time significantly.**

**It** → the new system.
**they** → employees.
**it** (second time) → the new system.
**This improvement** → employees becoming familiar with the system, and
the resulting improvement.
**the organization** → the company.

Track each one, and the paragraph becomes far easier to follow than it
first appeared.
:::

## Golden Rule

::: golden
Never let "it", "this", "they", or "which" slide past untested — mentally replace it with the noun it stands for. If the sentence stops making sense, you've picked the wrong reference.
:::

## Lesson Summary

Today you learned that a pronoun is only fully understood once you've
traced it back to its antecedent — and that doing this well means
checking number (singular vs. plural), testing candidates against
logic, and recognising that *this*, *these*, and *which* can refer to an
entire preceding idea, not just the nearest noun. You practised the
pronoun-replacement test and walked a full reference chain across a real
paragraph. This single skill — reference tracking — is often the real
difference between reading every word of a paragraph and actually
understanding it.

## Practice: Test What You've Learned

Work through every question yourself before checking anything.

::: important title="Before you start"
Do not use Google Translate or any other translation tool for these
questions. If a reference feels ambiguous, say which candidate you
chose and why — that reasoning is exactly what's being tested.
:::

### Part A — Identify the Reference

1. The manager reviewed the report. He found several errors. — He → ?
2. The company introduced a new system. It improved efficiency. — It → ?
3. Researchers interviewed several participants. They recorded their responses. — They → ? their → ?
4. The study produced unexpected results. These surprised the researchers. — These → ?
5. People often make decisions emotionally. This can lead to mistakes. — This → ?

### Part B — Relative Pronouns

6. The researcher who conducted the experiment published the results. — who → which noun does it refer to?
7. The study that examined social behavior produced useful evidence. — that → which noun does it refer to?
8. The researcher whose work influenced the field received an award. — whose → what relationship does it show?

### Part C — Decode the Sentence

9. **The researchers developed a new method, which they tested on several participants before they used it in a larger study.** Identify: *which* → ? *they* (first) → ? *they* (second) → ? *it* → ? — and check carefully whether the two occurrences of *they* share the same reference.

### Part D — Advanced Reading

10. Identify every pronoun's reference in this paragraph: *A company recently changed its hiring process because it wanted to attract more qualified employees. The new process requires applicants to complete several assessments before they receive an interview. These are designed to measure skills that are important for the position. Although this approach may require more time, it could help the company identify candidates who are better suited to the job.* Find: *its* → ? *it* → ? *they* → ? *These* → ? *that* → ? *this approach* → ? *who* → ?

### Part E — Deep Reading

11. Explain the complete reference structure of this sentence, then write its main idea in your own, simple English: *People who regularly receive useful feedback tend to improve their performance because it helps them recognize weaknesses that they might otherwise overlook.* Identify: *who* → ? *it* → ? *them* → ? *that* → ? *they* → ?
