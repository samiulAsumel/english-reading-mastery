## Introduction

Today's grammar point is passive voice — but the real goal isn't the
grammar itself. It's learning to look at a passive sentence and see
exactly what information the author is putting in focus. This matters
enormously, because academic books, research papers, history, science,
technology documentation, and business writing all lean on passive voice
constantly.

## Active Voice vs. Passive Voice

Compare two sentences with nearly the same underlying meaning:

> **Researchers conducted the study.**
> **The study was conducted by researchers.**

Both describe: *Researchers → conducted → the study.* But their focus is
different.

::: concept title="Same event, different focus"
**Active:** Researchers conducted the study.
Focus: **who did the action?** → Researchers.
Structure: Subject + Verb + Object.

**Passive:** The study was conducted by researchers.
Focus: **what happened to the study? what was done?** → The study.
Structure: Object → be + past participle (+ by + doer).

In passive voice, the thing an action was *done to* moves into the
sentence's subject position.
:::

## The Core Passive Formula

**Subject + be + V3** (V3 = past participle)

| Active | Passive |
|---|---|
| Researchers conduct the study. | The study is conducted by researchers. |
| Researchers conducted the study. | The study was conducted by researchers. |
| Researchers have conducted the study. | The study has been conducted by researchers. |
| Researchers will conduct the study. | The study will be conducted by researchers. |

Notice: the form of *be* changes with tense, but the main verb always
stays in its V3 (past participle) form.

## Recognising Passive Voice

Watch for the shape **be + V3**:

> is influenced · are affected · was developed · were conducted · has
> been studied · have been observed · will be examined · can be
> explained · may be influenced · should be considered

::: example title="Passive in real sentences"
**Human behavior is influenced by many factors.**
**The experiment was conducted in 2024.**
**Several factors have been identified.**
**The problem can be solved through careful analysis.**
:::

## "By + Doer" Is Optional

::: important title="A passive sentence doesn't have to name who did it"
**The experiment was conducted by researchers.** — the doer is named.

**The experiment was conducted.** — the doer is not named, and this is
extremely common.

Why? The writer may feel the doer isn't important, is already obvious,
is unknown to them, or that the result matters far more than who
produced it — a common priority in academic writing.
:::

## Example: Research Writing

::: example title="Passive lets a writer keep the data in focus"
**Active:** Researchers collected data from 500 participants.

**Passive:** Data were collected from 500 participants.

Both are grammatically correct. But the second sentence keeps the
reader's attention on *data* — who did the collecting becomes
secondary. This is exactly why research writing relies so heavily on
passive voice.
:::

## Reading Trick: Convert Passive to Active in Your Head

::: important title="Mentally rebuild the active sentence to check the core meaning"
**The participants were asked to complete a questionnaire.**

Don't let the passive shape confuse you. Convert it mentally: *Someone
asked the participants to complete a questionnaire.*

Core meaning: participants completed a questionnaire *because* someone
asked them to. The real information here: *participants → were asked →
to complete a questionnaire.*
:::

## Another Worked Example

::: example title="The passive can deliberately leave the doer out of focus"
**Several factors were identified during the study.**

*were identified* → passive verb. *Several factors* → subject. Who
identified them? The sentence doesn't say.

An active equivalent would be: *Researchers identified several factors
during the study.* But the author chose not to foreground the
researchers — the focus stays on *several factors were identified,*
because the finding itself matters more here than who found it.
:::

## Passive + Modal

Very common in books and documentation. **Pattern: modal + be + V3**

::: example title="Reading modal + passive together"
**The problem can be solved.** — solving it is possible.
**The results may be interpreted differently.** — interpretation could
vary.
**The evidence should be considered carefully.** — careful
consideration is recommended.
**The information must be verified.** — verification is necessary.
:::

## Passive + Perfect

**Pattern: has/have been + V3**

::: example title="Present Perfect combined with passive"
**The relationship has been studied extensively.**

*has* → present perfect · *been* → passive marker · *studied* → main
verb. Meaning: a great deal of research has already gone into this
relationship.

**Several explanations have been proposed.**

Active: *Researchers have proposed several explanations.* Passive:
*Several explanations have been proposed.* — focus stays on the
explanations, not the researchers.
:::

## Passive + Past Perfect

::: example title="Lesson 5's Past Perfect, combined with today's passive"
**The data had been collected before the experiment began.**

*had* + *been* + *collected.* Meaning: the data collection was finished
before the experiment started.

```
earlier:  Data collected
later:    Experiment began
```
:::

## Passive Inside a Relative Clause

::: example title="A passive verb can sit inside a relative clause, describing a noun"
**The study that was conducted in Germany produced interesting
results.**

Main subject: *The study.* Main verb: *produced.* Relative clause: *that
was conducted in Germany* — and inside it, *was conducted* is passive.

**Core:** The study produced interesting results. **Extra:** the study
in question is the one that was conducted in Germany.
:::

## Passive Voice and Information Focus

This is today's central idea.

::: important title="Where a sentence starts tells you what the author wants in focus"
**A:** The researchers analyzed the data carefully. — focus:
*researchers.*

**B:** The data were analyzed carefully. — focus: *data.*

**C:** The results were analyzed carefully. — focus: *results.*

**D:** The data were analyzed carefully by the researchers. — focus is
still mostly on *data*; *by the researchers* is simply additional,
secondary information.
:::

## A Reading Principle Worth Remembering

::: example title="What opens a sentence often carries the paragraph's current focus"
**Researchers discovered a significant pattern.** — new focus:
*researchers.*

**The pattern was later examined in detail.** — the author has now
shifted focus onto *pattern.*

Watching this shift is a direct window into how the author is guiding
the reader's attention across a paragraph.
:::

## Information Flow Across Sentences

::: example title="Old information first, then new information, sentence after sentence"
**Researchers conducted an experiment. The experiment was designed to
examine memory.**

Sentence 1: *Researchers* (new) · *an experiment* (new).
Sentence 2: *The experiment* (now known) → then new information about
it.

**Researchers identified several problems. These problems were later
examined in detail.**

*These problems* carries forward exactly what the previous sentence
introduced — directly connected to Lesson 10's pronoun and reference
tracking.
:::

## Three Questions to Ask When You See a Passive

::: framework title="Reading any passive sentence"
- What happened?
- Who or what received the action?
- Is the doer important here, or has it been deliberately left out?
:::

::: example title="Applying the three questions"
**The building was constructed in 1890.**

What happened? → the building was constructed. Who constructed it? →
not stated. Is the doer important? → probably not, for this sentence's
purpose. Focus: the building and its construction.
:::

## Myth: "Passive Always Means the Doer Is Unknown"

::: warning title="Not true — passive is a choice about focus, not about missing information"
**The law was introduced by the government.**

Here the doer is stated clearly. Passive voice was chosen simply because
the focus of the sentence is *the law*, not because the writer didn't
know who introduced it.
:::

## Myth: "Passive Always Means Formal English"

::: note title="Passive shows up constantly in everyday English too"
**My phone was stolen. · The window was broken. · I was invited to the
meeting. · He was given a warning.**

Academic and scientific writing simply uses it *more frequently* than
casual speech — it isn't exclusive to formal contexts.
:::

## Passive vs. Adjective

::: important title="Not every be + past-participle-looking word is passive"
**He is tired.** — *tired* functions here as an adjective describing a
state, not a passive verb.

**He was invited to the meeting.** — *was invited* is genuinely
passive: someone invited him.
:::

## A Trickier Case

::: example title="Some sentences genuinely allow both readings"
**The door was closed by the guard.** — clearly passive, since *The
guard closed the door* makes complete sense as the active version.

**The door was closed.** — this could be read as passive (someone
closed it) or as a simple description of its current state (an
adjective-like reading). Context has to decide; the formula alone won't
always settle it.
:::

## A Master Decoding Method for Passive Sentences

::: framework title="Seven steps for any passive sentence"
- Find the main subject
- Find the verb phrase
- Check whether it fits be + V3
- Understand who or what is receiving the action
- Check whether "by + doer" is present
- Ask why the author placed that particular noun at the front of the sentence
- Identify the sentence's overall information focus
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read the paragraph once, slowly, for structure — not word by word.

> Human behavior is influenced by a wide range of factors. Some of these
> factors can be observed directly, while others are difficult to
> identify. In many studies, participants are asked to complete specific
> tasks, and their behavior is carefully recorded. The collected data
> are then analyzed to determine whether particular patterns can be
> identified. Although researchers may propose several explanations for
> their findings, these explanations must be supported by evidence. As a
> result, conclusions should be interpreted carefully, especially when
> the available evidence is limited.

**Sentence 1** — *is influenced* → passive. Core: human behavior ←
factors influence it.

**Sentence 2** — *these factors* → refers back to the factors just
mentioned. *can be observed* → passive. *to identify* → infinitive.

**Sentence 3** — Two passives: *are asked* and *is recorded.* Focus:
*participants* and *their behavior.*

**Sentence 4** — *are analyzed* → passive. *can be identified* → also
passive. Main focus: *data → patterns.*

**Sentence 5** — *Although* → contrast. *may propose* → possibility.
*must be supported* → passive + modal. Focus: explanations must be
supported by evidence.

**Sentence 6** — *As a result* → conclusion. *should be interpreted* →
passive + modal. *when* → time/condition. Main message: with limited
evidence, conclusions should be interpreted carefully.

## Golden Rule

::: example title="Reading a passive sentence for its focus, not just its meaning"
**The results were interpreted differently by different researchers.**

Structure it mentally:

```
The results
   ↓ were interpreted
   ↓ by different researchers
```

Then convert to active: *Different researchers interpreted the results
differently.*

Then ask the real question: **why did the author choose to open with
"The results"?** — almost certainly because *results* is the paragraph's
current focus.
:::

::: golden
When you see a passive sentence, ask not only "who did this?" but "why did the author put the receiver of the action at the front?" That second question is what turns a grammar learner into a strong English reader.
:::

## Lesson Summary

Today you learned to recognise passive voice — *be + V3*, with modals
and perfect aspect layered on top — and to convert it mentally to active
whenever the core meaning feels unclear. You learned that "by + doer" is
optional and its absence doesn't mean the doer is unknown, that passive
isn't reserved for formal writing, and that a *be + adjective* shape can
look like a passive without being one. Most importantly, you practised
reading passive voice for what it reveals about an author's focus —
which noun they've chosen to place at the front of the sentence, and
why.
