## Introduction

This first lesson is not really about grammar rules. It is about giving
you a completely different way to look at an English sentence — one that
will make every lesson after this one easier.

By the end of today you will be able to:

- explain how English creates meaning, and why that is different from how Bangla does it
- tell the difference between a word, a phrase, a clause, and a sentence
- find the Subject, the Verb, the Object, and the Complement in a sentence
- explain why English word order is strict, and what happens when you break it
- take a long, intimidating sentence apart and find its core meaning in seconds
- understand why translating word by word from Bangla creates mistakes
- begin reading the way a confident, native-level reader reads

## The Real Goal of Learning English

::: concept title="Two very different ways to learn a language"
Most learners, without realising it, follow this path:

**English word → Bangla meaning → Bangla translation of the sentence**

It feels natural, because it is how a dictionary works. But it quietly
keeps you dependent on Bangla forever — every English sentence has to
pass through a Bangla detour before it means anything to you.

This course trains a different, faster path:

**English words → English structure → English meaning → the idea itself**

Notice what disappears from that second path: Bangla. You go directly
from the words on the page to the idea behind them.
:::

That shift is the difference between two levels of ability:

> *"I understand English."*

and

> *"I understand what the author means."*

The first is knowing a language. The second is thinking in it. Everything
in this course is built to move you from the first sentence to the
second, one lesson at a time — and it starts with seeing a sentence the
way its structure actually works, not the way a word-for-word translation
would suggest.

## From Word to Sentence

English is built in layers. Before we talk about grammar terms, look at
how four short building blocks stack on top of each other.

### Word

A single unit of meaning, on its own.

> **people**

### Phrase

Several words that work together as one unit, but still without a
complete thought.

> **intelligent people**

*"Intelligent"* is simply describing *"people"* — together they form one
noun phrase, one unit.

### Clause

A phrase becomes a clause the moment it has a Subject and a Verb.

> **people understand**

Now there is someone (*people*) doing something (*understand*). That is
the beginning of a thought.

### Sentence

A clause becomes a complete sentence when it expresses a whole, finished
thought.

> **People understand psychology.**

Nothing is missing. The idea is complete.

Every English sentence you will ever read is built from these same four
layers. Once you can see them separately, long sentences stop being
frightening — they are simply several of these layers, stacked.

## The Core Sentence Pattern: Subject + Verb

At its simplest, an English sentence is just two things: **someone or
something**, and **what they do**.

::: example title="Subject + Verb, three times"
**People think.**
*People* = Subject, *think* = Verb

**Birds fly.**
*Birds* = Subject, *fly* = Verb

**Children learn.**
*Children* = Subject, *learn* = Verb
:::

That is the smallest complete sentence English allows: one Subject, one
Verb. Everything else you will learn — Objects, Complements, long
modifying phrases — is added *on top of* this pattern. It never replaces
it.

## What Is a Subject?

The Subject is the sentence's main participant — the person or thing the
sentence is actually about. Ask yourself one question:

> **Who or what is the sentence about?**

::: example title="Finding the Subject"
**People think.**
Who is the sentence about? → *People*. So **People = Subject**.

**The brain processes information.**
What is doing the processing? → *The brain*. So **The brain = Subject**.

**Good leaders listen carefully.**
Who is listening? → *Good leaders*. So **Good leaders = Subject**.
:::

Notice that a Subject can be one word (*People*) or several words acting
as one unit (*Good leaders*, *The brain*). What makes something a Subject
is its job in the sentence, not how many words it has.

## What Is a Verb?

The Verb is the sentence's action, process, or state — what the Subject
does, or what is true about it.

Many verbs describe physical or mental action:

> think · learn · communicate · decide · change

But a verb does not have to describe a physical action at all:

::: example title="Verbs of state, not just action"
**I know him.**

**She believes him.**

**They understand the problem.**

*Know*, *believes*, and *understand* are all verbs — none of them
describe a physical movement, but each one tells you something the
Subject is doing mentally.
:::

## Adding an Object

Once you have a Subject and a Verb, English often adds a third piece: the
**Object** — the thing that receives the action.

::: example title="Subject + Verb + Object"
**People read books.**
Who reads? → *People* (Subject). What do they read? → *books* (Object).

**Humans study psychology.**
*Humans* = Subject, *study* = Verb, *psychology* = Object.

**Leaders manage teams.**
*Leaders* = Subject, *manage* = Verb, *teams* = Object.
:::

The pattern here — **Subject + Verb + Object** — is the single most common
sentence pattern in English. You will see it everywhere.

## Not Every Sentence Needs an Object

::: note title="A complete sentence can stop at the Verb"
**People sleep.**

*People* = Subject, *sleep* = Verb — and that is already a complete
sentence. There is nothing to add an Object to; you cannot "sleep
something."

**Subject + Verb** is already whole on its own. Many verbs simply do not
take an Object, and a sentence built from just those two parts is not
missing anything.
:::

## What Is a Complement?

A Complement looks a little like an Object, but it does a different job:
instead of receiving an action, it describes or renames the Subject.

::: example title="Object vs. Complement"
**She is intelligent.**
*She* = Subject, *is* = Verb, *intelligent* = Complement.

*Intelligent* is not something *she* is doing to it — there is no action
being received. It is simply describing what *she* is like.

**He became a leader.**
*He* = Subject, *became* = Verb, *a leader* = Complement.

*A leader* is telling you what *he* has become, not something he is
acting upon.
:::

A useful test: ask *"the Subject [verb]ed what?"* — if the word after the
verb answers that question as a separate thing being acted on (*People
read **books***), it is an Object. If instead it answers *"what is the
Subject like, or what has the Subject become?"* (*She is **intelligent**;
He became **a leader***), it is a Complement — it renames or describes
the Subject rather than naming something the Subject acted on.

## English Word Order Is Fixed

::: important title="Word order carries meaning in English"
English almost always follows one order:

**Subject → Verb → Object**

**People understand psychology.**

Bangla allows much more flexibility in word order — you can rearrange a
Bangla sentence and often keep the same meaning. English cannot. If you
rearrange the words above, the sentence breaks:

**Psychology understand people.**

This is not just awkward — it is grammatically broken, and an English
reader's mind starts assigning grammatical roles the instant it reads the
first word. Get the order wrong, and you get the meaning wrong.
:::

This single fact — that English relies on *position* to show meaning,
where Bangla relies more on *word endings and context* — is one of the
main reasons literal translation between the two languages goes wrong so
often.

## The Sentence-Decoding Pipeline

Here is the single most valuable habit this lesson will give you: **stop
translating the moment you see a sentence. Decode it instead.**

Before you try to understand the whole sentence, look for three things,
in this order:

1. the main Subject
2. the main Verb
3. the Object or Complement

Only after that do you look at the modifying words wrapped around them.

::: framework title="How to decode any sentence"
- Sentence
- Clause
- Main Subject
- Main Verb
- Object or Complement
- Modifiers
- Overall Meaning
:::

Let's use this pipeline on three sentences, each harder than the last.

::: example title="Example 1 — a short sentence with extra description"
**Intelligent people carefully analyze complex problems.**

At first glance this looks long. But strip away the description and the
core is small:

**People analyze problems.**

Everything else is just adding detail to that core:

- *intelligent* describes **people**
- *carefully* describes **how** they analyze
- *complex* describes **problems**

Core first, decoration second. That is the whole method.
:::

::: example title="Example 2 — a longer sentence"
**Successful leaders carefully consider the opinions of their team members before making important decisions.**

Do not try to translate this in one pass. Find the core first.

Who considers? → **Successful leaders** (Subject)
What do they do? → **consider** (Verb)
What do they consider? → **the opinions** (Object)

Core sentence:

**Successful leaders consider the opinions.**

Now the rest simply answers follow-up questions about that core:

- *carefully* — **how** do they consider?
- *of their team members* — **whose** opinions?
- *before making important decisions* — **when**?
:::

::: example title="Example 3 — an academic-style sentence"
**Human behavior is shaped by a complex interaction between biological, psychological, social, and environmental factors.**

This is the kind of sentence that stops most learners cold. Use the same
method.

Core:

**Human behavior is shaped.**

Then the rest answers, one piece at a time:

- *by a complex interaction* — **how** is it shaped?
- *between biological, psychological, social, and environmental factors*
  — **which** factors are interacting?

Once you have the core, a long academic sentence is just the core plus a
chain of answers to *how*, *by what*, and *which*. Nothing to fear.
:::

This is why the skill matters: the sentences you will meet in real
articles, textbooks, and books are almost always Example 1's simple core
wrapped in Example 3's amount of decoration. If you can find the core,
you can read anything — one layer at a time.

## Phrases in Detail

A phrase is a group of words acting as a single unit inside a sentence.
You already met one at the start of this lesson; here are a few more
shapes a phrase can take.

::: example title="Three kinds of phrase"
**very intelligent people** — a noun phrase. The core noun is *people*;
*very intelligent* describes it.

**in the workplace** — a prepositional phrase, telling you *where*.

**with great confidence** — another prepositional phrase, telling you
*how*.
:::

Phrases are the "decoration" pieces from the decoding pipeline above —
once you can recognise one as a single unit, you stop trying to translate
it word by word and start reading it as one idea.

## Clauses in Detail

A clause is, at minimum, a Subject paired with a Verb.

::: example title="Two clauses"
**people think**
*people* = Subject, *think* = Verb. A clause.

**because people want approval**
*people* = Subject, *want* = Verb. Also a clause — this one happens to
start with *because*, which changes its job in the sentence but not the
fact that it is a clause.
:::

Later lessons will go much deeper into clause types — independent
clauses, dependent clauses, relative clauses, noun clauses, and adverb
clauses. Recognising a clause on sight is the skill that all of those
later lessons build on, so it is worth being comfortable with it now.

## Meaning Beyond Individual Words: Phrasal Verbs

::: warning title="Word-by-word translation can mislead you badly"
**He gave up.**

Translate each word separately and you get nonsense: *give* = দেওয়া (to
give), *up* = উপরে (up/above). Neither of those has anything to do with
the sentence's real meaning.

**give up** together means *to quit, to stop trying* — a single unit of
meaning that has almost nothing to do with either word alone.

The same is true of:

**figure out** — to understand or solve something

**look after** — to take care of something or someone

These are called **phrasal verbs**, and English has hundreds of them.
This is exactly why word-by-word translation is unreliable: some English
meanings are carried by a *group* of words, not by any single word in it.
:::

## One Word, Many Forms: Context and Collocation

::: note title="A word's meaning shifts with its form and its company"
Take the word **decision**:

**make a decision** — সিদ্ধান্ত নেওয়া (to reach a decision)

**decision-making** — the *process* of reaching decisions

**decisive** — able to make firm decisions quickly

Three related words, three different jobs in a sentence. This is why
vocabulary in this course is never taught as a bare equation —

*word = Bangla meaning*

— but always as a fuller picture:

**word + meaning + how it is used + the words it appears with + context**
:::

## How to Read Every Passage

From this lesson onward, use this four-step process every time you sit
down to read something in English.

::: note title="The four-step reading process"
**First reading** — no dictionary. Read for the overall meaning only.

**Second reading** — identify the important words you do not know.

**Third reading** — analyze the sentence structure: find the Subject,
Verb, and Object or Complement in the sentences that gave you trouble.

**Fourth step** — say the main idea back to yourself, in your own words.
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read the paragraph below slowly. Do not use a dictionary.

> People make decisions every day. Some decisions are simple, while
> others are complex. Our decisions are influenced by many factors,
> including our emotions, experiences, beliefs, and social environment.
> People often believe that they make completely rational decisions, but
> research shows that human judgment can be affected by unconscious
> biases. Understanding these biases can help people make better
> decisions.

Now let's decode it, sentence by sentence, using exactly the method you
just practised.

**Sentence 1 — People make decisions every day.**
Core: *People make decisions.* Extra: *every day* tells you when.

**Sentence 2 — Some decisions are simple, while others are complex.**
This sentence actually holds two small ideas: *Some decisions are
simple* and *others are complex*. The word *while* is showing you the
relationship between them — a contrast.

**Sentence 3 — Our decisions are influenced by many factors.**
Core: *Our decisions are influenced.* Extra: *by many factors* tells you
the source of the influence.

**Sentence 4 — People often believe that they make completely rational
decisions, but research shows that human judgment can be affected by
unconscious biases.**
This is the longest sentence in the passage, and later lessons will show
you how to break sentences like this apart clause by clause. For now,
just notice its shape: *believe* … *but* … *research shows* — the author
is setting up a contrast between what people believe and what research
actually shows.

## Golden Rule

You do not need to become a native speaker today. Your first goal is much
smaller, and far more achievable:

**Stop fearing a sentence just because it is long.**

Every time a sentence looks intimidating, run it through the pipeline you
learned above: find the sentence, find its clauses, find the main
Subject, find the main Verb, find the Object or Complement — and only
then look at the modifiers. The overall meaning falls into place last,
not first.

::: golden
Never translate word by word — find the Subject and the Verb first. Everything else is detail hanging off them.
:::

## Lesson Summary

This lesson replaced translation with structure. English builds its
meaning through a fixed word order — Subject, then Verb, then Object or
Complement — rather than through the word-ending flexibility Bangla
relies on, which is exactly why word-by-word translation from Bangla
keeps producing broken English. You can now recognise the four layers of
English — word, phrase, clause, sentence — and identify the Subject,
Verb, Object, and Complement inside any sentence you meet. Hold onto the
single habit this lesson was really about: find the core of a sentence
before you try to understand the whole thing. Every lesson from here
builds on it.
