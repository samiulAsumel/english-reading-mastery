## Introduction

Lesson 44 gave you the architecture a paragraph builds from topic to
conclusion. Today's lesson works at a smaller scale, but the payoff is
just as large: instead of reading English one word at a time, you'll
start recognizing the fixed word-groups — **collocations** — that
academic English reuses constantly. A fluent reader doesn't decode
*conduct* and then *research* separately; they recognize *conduct
research* as a single unit of meaning. Building that recognition is
what this lesson trains.

## What Is a Collocation?

::: concept title="Words that habitually travel together"
A **collocation** is a word combination that English speakers use
naturally together, even when an equally logical-sounding alternative
doesn't get used. We say *make a decision*, not *do a decision*; we say
*conduct research*, not *make research*. A fluent reader processes
*conduct research* as one semantic unit rather than two separately
translated words — and that single habit is a major source of faster,
easier reading. The same verb can mean something different depending
on its noun partner: *provide* alone means little, but *provide
evidence*, *provide support*, and *provide an explanation* each carry
a distinct, learnable meaning.
:::

## Core Academic Verb + Noun Families

::: framework title="Four verbs, and the nouns they habitually pair with"
- Make: a decision, an assumption, a comparison, a distinction, an argument, a recommendation
- Take: into account, into consideration, a position, an approach, action, responsibility
- Reach: a conclusion, an agreement, a decision
- Draw: a conclusion, an inference, a distinction, attention to
:::

## Provide, Raise, Address, and Pose

::: example title="Four verbs whose academic meaning depends entirely on their noun partner"
**Provide** + evidence/information/support/an explanation/an
example/a basis for/an opportunity: *the findings provide evidence for
the proposed explanation* — read *provide evidence for* as one chunk.
**Raise** + a question/a concern/an issue/doubts/the possibility: *these
findings raise questions about the reliability of the system* — the
findings are the *cause* of new questions arising. **Address** (not
physical) + a problem/an issue/a concern/a question/a limitation means
*deal with* or *respond to*. **Pose** + a risk/a challenge/a
threat/a problem means *create* or *present*: *poor data quality poses
a significant risk to automated decision-making.*
:::

## Play a Role, Have an Impact, Exert Influence

::: important title="Three formal ways of saying 'affects'"
**Play a role in:** *technology plays a role in organizational
change; training plays an important role in successful implementation.*
**Have an impact on / have an effect on / have implications for:**
*automation may have a significant impact on employment patterns.*
**Exert influence on** (most formal): *organizational culture can
exert a strong influence on employee behavior* — plainly, *culture
strongly influences behavior.*
:::

## "Account For" — Two Distinct Meanings

::: warning title="The same phrase, two unrelated jobs"
**Explain:** *the difference may be accounted for by differences in
training* = differences in training may explain the difference.
**Constitute/represent a proportion:** *transport costs account for
30% of total expenditure* = transport costs make up 30%. Context alone
decides which job *account for* is doing in any given sentence.
:::

## The Causal Verb Family

::: concept title="Five ways to say 'causes,' each with a different strength"
**Contribute to** signals partial causation, never complete: *training
contributed to the improvement* means training was *one factor*, not
the whole story. **Result in** points cause-to-effect (X results in
Y); **result from** reverses the arrow (Y results from X) — mixing
these two up flips the causal direction entirely. **Lead to** works
like *result in*: *poor planning can lead to significant delays.*
**Give rise to** is a more formal synonym for *create/cause*: *rapid
technological change can give rise to new organizational challenges.*
**Bring about** is a formal causal verb often used for policy-driven
change: *the policy was introduced to bring about improvements in
efficiency.*
:::

## Undergo and Be Subject To

::: example title="Two verbs describing something happening to a subject, not caused by it"
**Undergo** + change/reform/transformation/evaluation/testing: *the
system underwent extensive testing before implementation* — the system
went through the testing process. **Be subject to** + change/
regulation/limitations/review: *the results are subject to several
limitations* means limitations apply to the results — a very common,
important hedge in academic writing.
:::

## Based On, Consistent With, Associated With, Attributed To

::: warning title="Four stance-bearing bundles, each with a distinct logical strength"
**Be based on** + evidence/assumptions/historical data: states what a
decision or claim rests on. **Be consistent with** + the evidence/
previous findings/the theory: *the results are consistent with
previous studies* means the results are *compatible with*, not
*proof of* — consistent with ≠ proves. **Be associated with** + higher
costs/improved performance/increased risk: signals correlation, not
causation, exactly as Lesson 32 established. **Be attributed to** +
automation/improved training, often softened with *partly*: *the
improvement was partly attributed to better data management* — the
word *partly* limits causal certainty deliberately.
:::

## Relevant To and Dependent On

::: example title="Two adjective + preposition bundles worth automatic recognition"
**Be relevant to** + the question/the analysis/policy decisions:
*these findings are directly relevant to the debate about automation.*
**Be dependent on** + data quality/context/implementation: *the
effectiveness of the system is highly dependent on data quality* —
naming the specific condition the claim's truth rests on.
:::

## Likely, Unlikely, Expected, Intended, and Designed To

::: important title="Five 'to' bundles that each carry a different kind of commitment"
**Be likely/unlikely to:** probability, not certainty — *poor-quality
data are likely to reduce system reliability.* **Be expected to:** a
prediction, not a guarantee — *demand is expected to increase over the
next decade.* **Be intended to** and **be designed to** both name
**purpose**, not necessarily **outcome**: *the policy was intended to
reduce unnecessary costs; the system was designed to detect unusual
patterns.* An intended or designed result is not automatically an
achieved one — this gap matters enormously when evaluating whether a
policy actually worked.
:::

## Purpose Chunks: In Order To, With the Aim Of, For the Purpose Of

::: example title="Three levels of formality, one underlying meaning: purpose"
**In order to** (neutral): *the company introduced automation in order
to reduce processing time* — equivalent to plain *to reduce processing
time.* **With the aim of** + V-ing (formal): *the policy was introduced
with the aim of reducing costs.* **For the purpose of** + V-ing (very
formal): *the data were collected for the purpose of evaluating the
new system.*
:::

## Scope Prepositional Chunks, Recapped From Lesson 41

::: note title="Directly connected to the prepositional compression chunks in Lesson 41"
**With respect to** and **in terms of** both restrict a claim to one
named dimension — *with respect to reliability, the new system
performed better* says nothing about cost, speed, or usability.
**In relation to:** interpret while considering something else — *the
findings should be interpreted in relation to the study's
limitations.* **On the basis of** = based on: *the decision was made
on the basis of limited evidence.* **In light of** = considering (a
logical relationship, not a physical one): *in light of the new
evidence, the researchers revised their conclusion.*
:::

## Formulaic Language and Lexical Bundles

::: concept title="Fixed multi-word sequences that recur constantly across academic English"
A **lexical bundle** (or formulaic sequence) is a recurring word group
— *as a result of, on the basis of, in terms of, with respect to, the
extent to which, it is important to note that, there is evidence
that, a number of factors, in the context of.* A beginner decodes *it
is important to note that the findings are based on a limited sample*
word by word; an advanced reader recognizes *it is important to note
that…* as a single framing chunk and moves straight to the real
proposition: *the findings are based on a limited sample.*
:::

## "There Is Evidence That" and Its Weaker Cousins

::: important title="A hedged claim about evidence is not the same as a stated fact"
**There is evidence that** X ≠ *X is true* — it means evidence exists
*supporting* the possibility of X. **There is little evidence that** X
means the supporting evidence is limited — not the same as *X is
false.* **There is no evidence that** X means available evidence
doesn't establish X — as Lesson 40 stressed, this is not the same
claim as *X definitely did not happen.* All three bundles differ in
strength, and none of them collapses into the others.
:::

## Quantity Bundles: A Number Of, A Range Of, A Significant Proportion Of

::: example title="Formal alternatives to plain quantifiers, each still vague on the exact amount"
**A number of** factors = *several* factors (not one exact number).
**A range of** approaches/outcomes = a variety, a spread. **A wide
range of** applications signals breadth explicitly. **A significant
proportion of** employees supported the change is a formal alternative
to *many* — but still gives no exact percentage. None of these bundles
should be silently upgraded to *most* or *all*.
:::

## The Extent To Which, and Five Related Formal Frames

::: framework title="Six formal noun-phrase frames and their plain-English equivalents"
- the extent to which X = how much/how far X is true
- the degree to which X = how much X is true
- the way in which X = how X happens
- the process by which X = how X happens (step by step)
- the reason why X = why X happens
- the question of whether X = whether X is true
:::

*The extent to which the policy is effective remains uncertain* asks,
plainly, *how effective is the policy?* — recognizing these six frames
on sight converts a wall of formal noun phrases into a short, plain
question underneath.

## Using Bundles as Instant Reading Shortcuts

::: important title="Substitute the plain form mentally, without slowing down to translate"
On seeing *on the basis of*, mentally substitute *based on*. Seeing
*with respect to*, substitute *regarding*. Seeing *the extent to
which*, substitute *how much/how far*. Seeing *in the absence of*,
substitute *without*. Seeing *as a result of*, substitute *because
of/caused by*. *The policy was introduced with the aim of reducing
administrative costs* becomes, mentally, *the policy was introduced to
reduce administrative costs* — the same underlying claim, arrived at
instantly rather than word by word.
:::

::: warning title="Not every chunk has an exact one-word substitute"
*In terms of cost* cannot be flattened to *because of cost* — *in
terms of* names a **dimension/aspect** being discussed, while
*because of* names a **cause**. The two serve entirely different
logical functions. Recognizing a chunk is only step one; identifying
exactly what job it's doing in the sentence is the step that actually
matters.
:::

## Collocation Carries Stance, Not Just Meaning

::: concept title="The same topic, opposite authorial positions"
*Raise a concern* and *dismiss a concern* both involve the word
*concern*, but the author's position toward it is completely opposite
— one treats it as worth taking seriously, the other treats it as
unworthy of serious attention. A collocation is never purely
descriptive; it very often signals where the author actually stands.
:::

## Reporting Verb Bundles: Suggest vs. Demonstrate

::: warning title="The reporting verb changes the strength of everything that follows it"
Academic books lean on reporting bundles constantly: *evidence
suggests that…, findings indicate that…, researchers argue that…,
studies demonstrate that…, authors claim that…, critics contend
that…, analysis reveals that….* **Evidence suggests that X** = evidence
points toward X, a moderate claim. **Evidence demonstrates that X** =
much stronger support is being claimed. Reading only the proposition
*that X* and ignoring the reporting verb that introduces it loses
exactly the information that tells you how confident the claim
actually is.
:::

## Academic Noun Bundles: Impact, Role, Influence, Relationship

::: example title="A family of 'noun of X (on/in/between Y)' patterns, all reducible to plain verbs"
**The role of X in Y** = how X contributes to Y: *the role of
technology in organizational change.* **The influence of X on Y** =
how X influences Y. **The impact of X on Y** = how X affects Y: *the
impact of automation on employment.* **The relationship between X and
Y** = how X and Y are related. **The effectiveness of X** = how well X
works. **The assessment/evaluation of X** = evaluating X. Every one of
these noun-heavy academic phrases reduces to a short, plain question
once you recognize the pattern.
:::

## Verb, Adjective, and Noun + Preposition Families

::: framework title="Learn these as fixed units — the preposition is not interchangeable"
- Verb + preposition: depend on, contribute to, result in, result from, account for, consist of, relate to, refer to, lead to, arise from, stem from, focus on, rely on, respond to, apply to, differ from, compare with, distinguish between
- Adjective + preposition: relevant to, consistent with, dependent on, associated with, related to, similar to, different from, responsible for, capable of, likely to, unlikely to, subject to, based on
- Noun + preposition: evidence for, evidence against, impact on, influence on, relationship between, difference between, reason for, approach to, response to, solution to, contribution to, access to, effect on
:::

::: warning title="The preposition is part of the meaning, not a free choice"
*Reason for the change* and *reason why the change occurred* express a
similar idea through different grammar, but *approach to the problem*
cannot become *approach for the problem* — the preposition is fixed to
its noun or adjective. Treat *noun + preposition* and *adjective +
preposition* pairs as single units to memorize, not as a noun/adjective
plus a freely chosen connector.
:::

## A Reading Strategy for Unfamiliar Words Inside a Collocation

::: framework title="Six questions for an unfamiliar word sitting inside a chunk"
- What sits immediately next to this word?
- Is it verb + noun?
- Is it adjective + noun?
- Is it verb + preposition?
- Is it noun + preposition?
- What is the whole chunk's function in the sentence?
:::

*The findings raise serious questions about the reliability of the
system* lets you infer *reliability* even without knowing the word: the
chunk *raise questions about reliability* signals that reliability is
something whose *dependability* is in doubt. *The policy poses a
significant challenge to small organizations* lets the chunk *pose a
challenge to* tell you *poses* means *creates/presents*, without ever
opening a dictionary.

## Building a Chunk-Based Vocabulary Notebook

::: important title="Record collocations, not isolated words"
Instead of writing *contribute = অবদান রাখা*, record: *contribute to
improved performance; contribute to a reduction in costs; contribute
to the development of….* Layer word families with their typical
collocations: *decide → make a decision; analyze → conduct an
analysis; implement → implementation of a policy; evaluate → evaluate
effectiveness / evaluation of effectiveness.* This single habit change
expands usable academic vocabulary far faster than memorizing isolated
definitions.
:::

## Chunking Is the Foundation of Reading Fluency

::: important title="The single habit that separates a slow reader from a fluent one"
Read academic English chunk by chunk, not word by word — *on the basis
of*, *as a result of*, and *the extent to which* are each one unit of
meaning, not four or five separate words to decode and translate
individually.
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read this passage once for its overall meaning, then underline every
collocation and lexical bundle before checking your reading against
the notes that follow.

> Several factors appear to contribute to the effectiveness of
> automated inventory systems. There is evidence that accurate sensor
> data plays a critical role in reducing stock discrepancies, although
> the extent to which this benefit depends on staff training remains
> unclear. On the basis of results from a small number of warehouses,
> the improvement cannot be attributed solely to the technology; rather,
> it appears to be associated with a combination of hardware quality
> and operational practices.

*Contribute to* signals partial causation. *There is evidence that*
hedges the claim about sensor data. *Plays a critical role in* is a
verb-noun collocation for *strongly affects*. *The extent to which…
remains unclear* leaves the training question genuinely open. *On the
basis of* introduces the evidence's foundation. *Cannot be attributed
solely to* and *associated with* both deliberately avoid a single-cause
claim — exactly the pattern this lesson trained you to spot instantly.

## Golden Rule

::: golden
Read academic English chunk by chunk, not word by word — on the basis of, as a result of, and the extent to which are each one unit of meaning, not several separate words to translate.
:::

## Lesson Summary

This lesson moved vocabulary study from the single word to the chunk:
verb-noun families like *make a decision* and *raise a concern*, the
causal verb ladder from *contribute to* through *bring about*, stance-
bearing bundles like *be consistent with* and *be attributed to*, and
formal lexical bundles like *on the basis of* and *the extent to
which* that recur constantly across serious academic English. You
practised converting each bundle to its plain-English equivalent while
watching for the cases — *in terms of* versus *because of* — where no
exact substitute exists and the chunk's specific logical function has
to be identified instead.

::: important title="The progression this lesson is building toward"
Word → Chunk → Meaning → Function → Argument. A beginner sees *provide*
+ *evidence*. An advanced reader sees *provide evidence*. A more
advanced reader sees *provide evidence for X*. A critical reader knows
that *provide evidence for X* is not the same claim as *prove X* — all
four layers working together at once.
:::

### Part A — Verb + Noun Collocations

1. **The committee reached a decision after considering the evidence.** Identify the verb-noun collocation, and explain why "made a decision" would also be acceptable here while "did a decision" would not. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">The collocation is "reached a decision." "Made a decision" is also acceptable because "make" is one of the verbs that habitually pairs with "decision" in English, but "did a decision" is not, since "do" is simply not a collocational partner for that noun, even though it is logically no less sensible than "make."</div></details>
2. **The report raises serious concerns about data security.** Explain what "raise" contributes to this sentence's meaning beyond its literal sense of lifting something. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">In the collocation "raise a concern," "raise" means to bring something into existence or attention, it signals that the report is the cause of new concerns arising, not that anything is being physically lifted. Reading "raise" as one unit with "concerns" is faster and more accurate than translating "raise" on its own.</div></details>

### Part B — The Causal Verb Family

3. **Better training contributed to the improvement in accuracy.** Explain why this sentence does not claim that training was the only cause. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">"Contribute to" signals partial causation, never complete causation, so the sentence only claims that training was one factor among possibly several. It leaves open that other factors, such as better equipment or process changes, may also have played a part in the improvement.</div></details>
4. **The reduction in costs resulted from process improvements.** Identify which noun phrase is the cause and which is the effect, and explain how the sentence would change meaning if "resulted in" were used instead. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">The cause is the process improvements; the effect is the reduction in costs, since "resulted from" names the effect first and the cause second. Swapping in "resulted in" would reverse the causal arrow entirely, making "the reduction in costs resulted in process improvements" claim that lower costs caused the process changes, the opposite relationship.</div></details>

### Part C — Stance-Bearing Bundles

5. **The results are consistent with the researchers' original hypothesis.** Explain why this is a weaker claim than "the results prove the hypothesis." <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">"Be consistent with" means the results are compatible with the hypothesis, not that they establish it as true; other explanations could still be equally compatible with the same results. "Prove" claims definitive confirmation, a much stronger and more exposed assertion than mere compatibility.</div></details>
6. **Evidence suggests that the policy may reduce costs.** vs. **Evidence demonstrates that the policy reduces costs.** Explain the difference in strength between these two sentences. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">"Suggests" plus "may" makes a moderate, doubly hedged claim, evidence points toward a possibility, not a certainty. "Demonstrates" claims much stronger support, and dropping "may" states the cost reduction as an established fact rather than a possible one, making the second sentence a considerably stronger claim than the evidence in the first would justify.</div></details>

### Part D — Formal Lexical Bundles

7. **There is little evidence that the new system improves reliability.** Explain why this is not equivalent to "the new system does not improve reliability." <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">"There is little evidence that X" means the supporting evidence for X is limited, not that X has been shown to be false. "The new system does not improve reliability" makes a positive claim of no effect, which is a stronger and different assertion than simply noting that proof is currently scarce.</div></details>
8. **With respect to cost, the new supplier was preferable.** Identify the scope of this claim, and explain what it does not tell you about the supplier. <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">The scope is limited to cost alone. It tells you nothing about the supplier's reliability, delivery speed, quality, or any other dimension, and it should never be read as a claim that the new supplier was preferable overall.</div></details>
