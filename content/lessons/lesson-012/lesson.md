## Introduction

This lesson matters enormously, because psychology, science, business,
history, philosophy, economics, and technology writing constantly says
things like: *if X happens, Y might follow… X would not happen without
Y… if the situation had been different… this could have happened… had
they known earlier…* Today's topic — condition, result, possibility, and
hypothetical thinking — is what lets you read all of these accurately.

## What Is a Conditional Sentence?

A conditional sentence expresses a condition and its result.

**Basic structure:** If X, Y.

> **If people practice regularly, they improve.**

Condition: *people practice regularly.* Result: *they improve.*

## The IF Clause

The clause containing *if* states the condition.

> **If you study regularly, you will improve.**

Condition: *If you study regularly.* Result: *you will improve.* The
order can flip with no change in meaning: **You will improve if you
study regularly.**

## Zero Conditional

Used for a general truth, rule, or repeated relationship.

**Structure:** If + present simple, present simple

> **If people do not practice, they make mistakes.**
> **If water reaches 100°C, it boils.**

Neither describes a specific, one-time future event — both describe a
general relationship.

## Reading a Zero Conditional

::: example title="Read this shape as a general pattern, not a prediction about one person"
**If people receive useful feedback, they often improve their
performance.**

This is not a prediction about a specific individual — it's a general
pattern true of people in general.
:::

## First Conditional

Used for a realistic future possibility.

**Structure:** If + present simple, will + base verb

> **If you practice regularly, you will improve.**
> **If the company invests in training, employee performance will
> improve.**

## Will Doesn't Always Just Mean "The Future"

::: important title="In a conditional, will marks the expected result of a met condition"
**If demand increases, prices will rise.**

Logic: *demand increases → prices rise.* This is a future prediction, but
its real job here is showing the expected consequence of the condition
being satisfied.
:::

## UNLESS

**unless = if not**

> **You will not improve unless you practice.** = You will not improve
> if you do not practice.
> **Unless the evidence changes, the conclusion will remain the same.**
> = If the evidence does not change, the conclusion will remain the
> same.

## IF vs. UNLESS

::: example title="Positive condition vs. negative condition"
**If you practice, you will improve.** — positive condition.
**Unless you practice, you will not improve.** — negative condition.

Whenever you meet *unless*, try mentally converting it to *if not.*
:::

## Second Conditional

Now we move into hypothetical territory.

**Structure:** If + past simple, would + base verb

> **If I had more time, I would read more books.**

The speaker is not claiming to have more time — this is hypothetical.

## The Core Idea of the Second Conditional

::: concept title="An unlikely, hypothetical, or imaginary present/future alternative"
**If people understood their biases better, they would make better
decisions.**

Present reality (implied): people may not fully understand their
biases. Hypothetical: *if they understood them better…* Possible
result: *they would make better decisions.*
:::

## First vs. Second Conditional

::: important title="Realistic possibility vs. hypothetical possibility"
**First:** If I study regularly, I will improve. — a realistic future
possibility, as the speaker sees it.

**Second:** If I studied regularly, I would improve. — hypothetical, or
less certain.

Rough rule of thumb: **First = realistic. Second = hypothetical/less
likely.** Not a rigid mathematical rule, but genuinely useful for
reading.
:::

## COULD

::: example title="could signals possibility or ability inside a hypothetical"
**If people understood the problem better, they could make better
decisions.** — they *would be able to*, or *it would be possible for
them to.*

Compare: *would* → an expected hypothetical result. *could* → a possible
hypothetical result.
:::

## MIGHT

::: example title="might signals even more uncertainty"
**If the company changed its strategy, it might improve performance.**
— improvement is possible, but not certain.

**Rough comparison:** would → strongest expected result. could →
possible result. might → uncertain possibility. Always let context
confirm the exact shade.
:::

## Third Conditional

Now we move into hypothetical situations set in the *past*.

**Structure:** If + past perfect, would have + past participle

> **If I had studied earlier, I would have passed the exam.**

Reality: *I did not study earlier.* Consequence: *I did not pass.* But
the speaker constructs an alternative past: *If I had studied earlier…
I would have passed…*

## The Core Idea of the Third Conditional

::: example title="An alternative outcome for something that already happened"
**If the researchers had collected more data, they would have reached a
different conclusion.**

Implied reality: they did not collect enough data. Hypothetical: *If
they had collected more…* Alternative outcome: *they would have reached
a different conclusion.*
:::

## Zero, First, Second, and Third, Side by Side

| Type | Condition | Result | Main idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zero | present | present | general truth |
| First | present | will | realistic future |
| Second | past | would/could/might | hypothetical present/future |
| Third | past perfect | would/could/might have | hypothetical past |

## The Same Idea, All Four Ways

::: example title="Sleep and energy, across all four conditionals"
**Zero:** If people sleep poorly, they often feel tired. — general
pattern.

**First:** If people sleep poorly tonight, they will probably feel
tired tomorrow. — specific future possibility.

**Second:** If people slept better, they would probably feel more
energetic. — hypothetical situation.

**Third:** If people had slept better, they would have felt more
energetic yesterday. — past hypothetical.
:::

## Important: A Past Verb Form Does Not Always Mean Past Time

::: important title="In the second conditional, a past-tense verb marks 'hypothetical', not 'earlier'"
**If I were rich, I would travel more.**

*were* is a past form, but there's no past time here at all — this
describes a hypothetical *present* situation. When reading English,
don't assume a past-tense verb always means "in the past" — check
whether you're inside a conditional first.
:::

## WERE

In formal hypothetical English:

> If I were… · If he were… · If she were… · If it were…

::: example title="were for hypothetical identity or state"
**If I were the manager, I would change the system.**

The speaker is not the manager. Hypothetical: *if I were the manager…*
:::

## WOULD, in Conditional Reading

::: example title="would = hypothetical or expected result under a stated condition"
**If people had better information, they would make better
decisions.**

Condition: *better information.* Result: *better decisions.*
:::

## COULD vs. WOULD vs. MIGHT

::: example title="Possible, expected, or genuinely uncertain"
**If we had more data, we could make a better decision.** — a better
decision would be *possible.*

**If we had more data, we would make a better decision.** — a better
decision would be *expected.*

**If we had more data, we might reach a different conclusion.** — a
different conclusion is *possible, but genuinely uncertain* — the
weakest commitment of the three.
:::

## Conditionals Without "If"

::: concept title="Formal inversion drops if entirely"
**Had the researchers collected more data, they might have reached a
different conclusion.**

Equivalent to: *If the researchers had collected more data, they might
have reached a different conclusion.*

**Had + subject + past participle** is a formal inversion pattern that
replaces *if.*
:::

## Another Inversion Example

> **Were the evidence stronger, the conclusion would be more
> convincing.** = If the evidence were stronger, the conclusion would be
> more convincing.

This pattern turns up in formal academic writing, so it's worth
recognising on sight even though it looks unusual at first.

## AS LONG AS

**Meaning: provided that / if**

> **You can improve as long as you practice consistently.**

Condition: *practice consistently.* Result: *improve.*

## PROVIDED THAT

::: example title="A formal way to state a condition"
**The method can be effective provided that it is applied correctly.**

Meaning: the method will be effective *if* it is applied correctly. Very
common in research and business writing.
:::

## IN CASE

::: important title="in case is not the same thing as if"
**Take an umbrella in case it rains.**

This isn't a condition-result pair — it's *preparation for a
possibility.* Rain might not happen at all; the umbrella is a precaution
regardless.
:::

## WHETHER

::: concept title="whether isn't exactly a condition — it's an alternative or an uncertainty"
**Researchers examined whether the treatment was effective.** — meaning:
*was the treatment effective, or not?*

**Whether the method succeeds depends on how it is implemented.** — here
*whether the method succeeds* works like a noun, as the subject of
*depends*.
:::

## Hypothetical Thinking in Non-Fiction

::: example title="Reality, hypothesis, and possible result, laid side by side"
**People may make poor decisions when information is incomplete. If they
had more reliable information, they might make different choices.**

Reality: *information incomplete.* Hypothesis: *if more reliable
information.* Possible result: *might make different choices.*
:::

## Conditionals Combined With Logical Connectors

Connecting back to Lesson 8:

::: example title="Multiple signals working together in one passage"
**If people understand their biases, they may make better decisions.
However, this does not guarantee that their decisions will always be
rational.**

*If* → condition · *may* → possibility · *However* → contrast. A single
sentence or short paragraph can carry several of these signals at once.
:::

## An Advanced Worked Example

::: example title="Condition, possibility, contrast, and limitation, all in one sentence"
**If people were fully aware of the factors that influence their
decisions, they might be able to reduce some forms of bias; however,
this would not necessarily mean that they could eliminate bias
completely.**

**Condition:** If people were fully aware… — hypothetical present.
**Result:** they might be able to reduce… — possibility.
**Contrast:** however.
**Second result:** this would not necessarily mean… — a limitation.
**Limitation:** could eliminate bias completely — the author is
deliberately avoiding an absolute claim here.

This is what sophisticated English reading looks like in practice.
:::

## "Not Necessarily"

::: important title="One of academic English's most important phrases"
**X does not necessarily mean Y.**

Meaning: X happening does not *guarantee* that Y happens.

**More information does not necessarily lead to better decisions.** —
having more information doesn't automatically produce a better
decision.
:::

## "Even If"

::: concept title="A condition that doesn't change the outcome"
**even if** = even under this condition, the result stays the same, or
the author is building a stronger concession than a plain *if* would.

**Even if people have more information, they may still make poor
decisions.** — having more information doesn't prevent poor decisions.
:::

## EVEN IF vs. IF

::: example title="A plain possibility vs. a concession"
**If people have more information, they may make better decisions.** —
a plain possibility.

**Even if people have more information, they may still make poor
decisions.** — the author is saying: *the problem can persist despite*
having more information.
:::

## UNLESS vs. EVEN IF

::: important title="Two genuinely different logics"
**Unless people practice, they may not improve.** — without practice,
improvement is unlikely.

**Even if people practice, they may not improve immediately.** — even
*with* practice, improvement isn't guaranteed.

These describe opposite situations — don't let their surface similarity
blur that difference.
:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="guarantee" pos="verb" register="formal" meaning="to promise that something will definitely happen or be true (নিশ্চয়তা দেওয়া)" collocation="guarantee delivery, cannot guarantee, money-back guarantee" example="The carrier will not guarantee a delivery date if the cargo misses the vessel's cut-off time."
:::

::: vocabulary word="reliable" pos="adjective" register="neutral" meaning="able to be trusted to be accurate or to work correctly (বিশ্বাসযোগ্য/নির্ভরযোগ্য)" collocation="reliable source, highly reliable, reliable service" example="The forecasting model is only as reliable as the vessel arrival data that feeds it."
:::

::: vocabulary word="accurate" pos="adjective" register="neutral" meaning="correct and true in every detail (নির্ভুল)" collocation="accurate information, highly accurate, accurate record" example="Accurate weight declarations are essential for safe stowage planning."
:::

::: vocabulary word="unreliable" pos="adjective" register="neutral" meaning="not able to be trusted to be accurate or consistent (অবিশ্বাস্য/অনির্ভরযোগ্য)" collocation="unreliable data, prove unreliable, unreliable source" example="The audit rejected the survey because the response rate was low and the sample data proved unreliable."
:::

::: vocabulary word="interpret" pos="verb" register="formal" meaning="to explain or decide the meaning of something (ব্যাখ্যা করা)" collocation="interpret results, interpret correctly, interpret as" example="Analysts interpreted the drop in bookings as a sign that customers were switching to a rival terminal."
:::

::: note title="Chunks worth learning as whole units"
*if necessary · if possible · if appropriate · if required · unless
otherwise stated · provided that · as long as · even if · even though ·
not necessarily · be likely to · be unlikely to · could potentially ·
might lead to · would result in · would have resulted in*
:::

## A Few Combinations Worth Learning Specifically

::: example title="be likely to / be unlikely to / lead to / result in / result from"
**be likely to:** People are likely to make mistakes when they are
tired. — a higher probability.

**be unlikely to:** People are unlikely to change their beliefs
immediately. — a lower probability.

**lead to:** Stress can lead to poor decisions.

**result in:** Poor planning can result in serious problems.

**result from:** The problem resulted from poor planning.

These five patterns are the backbone of cause-and-effect reading.
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read the paragraph once, slowly, marking each conditional's type before
you read the sentence-by-sentence breakdown below.

> If people had unlimited access to information, they might assume that
> they could always make better decisions. However, more information
> does not necessarily lead to better judgment. If the available
> information is unreliable or difficult to interpret, additional
> information could actually make decision-making more complicated. Even
> if people have access to accurate information, they may still
> interpret it differently because their previous experiences and
> beliefs influence the way they understand it. Therefore, better
> decisions require not only access to information but also the ability
> to evaluate and interpret it effectively.

**Sentence 1** — *If people had unlimited access…* → second
conditional, a hypothetical situation. *they might assume…* →
possibility.

**Sentence 2** — *However* → contrast. Main point: more information
does not automatically mean better judgment.

**Sentence 3** — *If the available information is unreliable…* → first
conditional, a realistic condition. Result: *could make decision-making
more complicated* → possibility.

**Sentence 4** — *Even if people have access…* → concession. *may still
interpret…* → possibility. Reason: *because previous experiences and
beliefs influence…*

**Sentence 5** — *Therefore* → conclusion: good decision-making requires
both access to information *and* the ability to evaluate and interpret
it.

## Golden Rule

::: framework title="Identifying a conditional's type on sight"
- Check the verb after "if": present tense → zero or first
- Check what follows the comma: present simple → zero; "will" → first
- Past-tense verb after "if" → second (hypothetical present/future)
- Past perfect after "if" → third (hypothetical past)
:::

Every conditional this lesson covered collapses into one small map:

```
ZERO      If + present  → present                        general truth
FIRST     If + present  → will                            realistic future
SECOND    If + past     → would / could / might            hypothetical present/future
THIRD     If + past perfect → would / could / might have   hypothetical past

UNLESS       = if not
EVEN IF      = the condition is met, but the result may stay the same
NOT NECESSARILY = X does not guarantee Y
```

::: golden
A past-tense verb after "if" almost never means past time — it means the situation is hypothetical, not real. Read the verb form for likelihood, not for when.
:::

## Lesson Summary

Today you learned to sort conditional sentences into four types — zero
(general truth), first (realistic future), second (hypothetical
present/future), and third (hypothetical past) — by their verb-tense
pattern alone, and to read *would, could,* and *might* as three
different strengths of hypothetical result. You met *unless* (if not),
*even if* (a condition that doesn't change the outcome), formal
inversion without *if* (*Had the researchers…, Were the evidence…*), and
the essential academic phrase *not necessarily.* Most importantly, you
practised reading a past-tense verb after *if* as a signal of
hypothetical thinking, not of past time.

### Part C — Could / Would / Might

Choose the most appropriate word, and explain your reasoning.

8. If we had more evidence, we ___ make a stronger argument. — could / would / might <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">Would fits best as the default, expected-result reading: given more evidence, a stronger argument is presented as the natural consequence. 9. If the evidence were reliable, researchers ___ probably accept the conclusion. — could / would / might <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">Would is the best fit, because probably already supplies the hedge; would probably describes the expected outcome under that hedge, while could probably or might probably would stack two uncertainty markers awkwardly on top of each other.</div></details>
10. If the researchers had collected more data, they ___ have reached a different conclusion. — could / would / might (more than one may be defensible in context — explain your choice) <details class="answer"><summary>Show answer</summary><div class="answer-body">Might have is the most cautious and often the most realistic choice for research writing, since it presents a different conclusion as merely a possibility. ### Part E — Deep Reading

12. Build a logic map for this paragraph: **Even if people have access to accurate information, they may make poor decisions if they are unable to evaluate that information effectively.
