## Introduction

Lesson 63 gave you a full architecture for reading problem-solution
writing. Today's lesson turns to a closely related but distinct kind of
text: writing that tells you not what happened, but what *should*
happen, what *must* happen, what is *permitted*, and what conditions
govern a decision. This is the language of recommendations, policies,
rules, and professional decision-making — and it shows up constantly in
technology documentation, business writing, port operations, logistics,
management, government policy, workplace rules, and technical
recommendations.

The core skill this lesson builds is a habit of never reading a modal
verb — should, must, may, might, could — as an ordinary word. Each one
carries a specific claim about obligation, permission, possibility, or
certainty, and confusing them changes what a sentence is actually
telling you to do, or not do.

## Recommendation Is Not the Same as Fact

::: concept title="The same idea, six different strengths of claim"
*The system should be monitored regularly* tells you what the author
thinks *ought* to happen — a recommendation, not a fact. *The system is
monitored regularly* is a plain statement of what actually happens.
Line up the variations and the differences become clear:

| Sentence | Function |
|---|---|
| The system is monitored regularly. | Fact / description |
| The system should be monitored regularly. | Recommendation |
| The system must be monitored regularly. | Requirement / obligation |
| The system may need to be monitored regularly. | Possibility / recommendation |
| The system could be monitored regularly. | Possible option |
| The system is designed to be monitored regularly. | Intended design |

Reading advanced text well means slowing down at exactly this kind of
modal verb, every time, rather than skimming past it as filler.
:::

## Common Signals of Recommendation

::: framework title="Recommendation strength, grouped from strongest to weakest"
- Strong: should, ought to, need to, must, it is essential to, it is important to, it is necessary to, it is advisable to
- Moderate: may want to, might consider, could consider, would benefit from, it may be useful to, it may be preferable to
- Weak / optional: could, might, one option is to, an alternative would be to, it is worth considering
:::

::: example title="A recommendation, unpacked"
*Managers should review the billing data before approving the
invoice.* This is not description — it's a recommendation, telling
managers what they ought to do, and in what order: review first, then
approve.
:::

## The Many Meanings of "Should"

::: important title="'Should' is not always 'ought to' — check which job it's doing"
**A. Recommendation** — *Employees should receive additional
training* means employees ought to receive it. **B. Expectation** —
*The package should arrive tomorrow* carries no "ought to" at all; it
means the package is *expected* or likely to arrive tomorrow. **C.
Logical expectation** — *If the calculation is correct, the total
should be 500* means the total is expected to come out at 500, given a
correct calculation. Before translating "should" as "উচিত," check
whether the sentence is actually recommending an action, predicting an
outcome, or drawing a logical expectation from a stated condition.
:::

## "Must" Versus "Should"

::: warning title="Two different strengths of claim — and 'must' has a second job besides"
*Employees must wear protective equipment* states a requirement or
obligation. *Employees should wear protective equipment* states a
recommendation or advice. In general: **must** signals a stronger
obligation, while **should** signals a recommendation or expected
outcome. But context still matters — *the system must be configured
correctly* is likely a technical requirement, while *the system must be
experiencing some form of configuration error* uses "must" for a
**strong logical deduction**, closer to "almost certainly is," not an
instruction to configure anything.
:::

## Reading Policy Text

::: concept title="A policy passage's typical structure"
Policy text commonly moves through: Problem → Objective → Rule → Scope
→ Exception → Enforcement → Review.

**Example:** *To reduce billing errors, all invoices must be reviewed
before they are issued. However, invoices below a specified threshold
may be processed automatically. The policy applies to all billing
departments and will be reviewed annually.*

Objective: to reduce billing errors. Main rule: all invoices must be
reviewed. Exception: invoices below a specified threshold may be
processed automatically. Scope: all billing departments. Review:
reviewed annually. Reading a policy paragraph well means never stopping
at vocabulary comprehension — you still need to answer: What is the
rule? Who does it apply to? When does it apply? Are there exceptions?
What happens if the rule isn't followed?
:::

## Rule Versus Principle

::: concept title="A specific instruction is a different kind of claim from a general standard"
A **rule** states a specific required action: *Employees must submit
the report by Friday.* A **principle** states a broader idea or
standard: *Decisions should be based on reliable evidence.* A rule
typically reads as "do X" or "don't do X"; a principle states that
decisions should follow a certain standard or value, without specifying
one exact action.
:::

## Scope: Who Does the Rule Apply To?

::: important title="The same rule shape can cover very different populations"
*Employees working in the billing department must complete the
training* — scope: employees working in the billing department, not
everyone. *All employees must complete the training* — scope: all
employees, without restriction. *Employees who handle customer payments
must complete the training* — scope narrower still. Before accepting
that a rule applies to "you" or to "everyone," locate its exact scope
phrase.
:::

## Finding Exceptions

::: framework title="Common exception signals in policy and rule text"
- except, unless, otherwise, except when
- unless otherwise stated, with the exception of
- in cases where, where applicable, where necessary
- subject to, provided that, as long as
:::

::: example title="Converting a rule-plus-exception into a mental rule"
*Employees must submit the form electronically unless the system is
unavailable.* Main rule: employees must submit the form electronically.
Exception: system is unavailable. Convert this into two conditions:
normal condition → electronic submission; system unavailable →
exception applies. The exception, often just a few words at the end of
a sentence, is frequently the most operationally important part of the
whole policy.
:::

## "Unless"

::: important title="'Unless X, do Y' means 'if X does not happen, do Y'"
*Unless the payment is verified, the invoice should not be finalized.*

```
Payment verified?
→ YES → invoice can be finalized
→ NO  → don't finalize
```

"Unless" functions as "if not" — reading it as a simple contrast word
instead of a conditional loses its actual logical structure.
:::

## "Provided That" and "As Long As"

::: note title="Two phrases that both introduce a condition attached to permission"
*The system can operate automatically provided that the required data
is available* — automatic operation depends on data availability.
*Employees may work remotely as long as performance requirements are
met* — remote work is allowed on the condition that performance
requirements hold. Both build the same mental structure: condition →
permission or outcome.
:::

## "Subject To"

::: important title="A very common piece of formal policy and business language"
*The proposal was approved subject to further review* — the proposal
is approved, but conditionally, pending further review. *Access is
granted subject to security requirements* — access is granted, under
the condition of security requirements. Pattern: *X is allowed /
approved / provided subject to Y* means X is allowed, but Y's
conditions still apply — the approval is real, but not unconditional.
:::

## Finding a Recommendation's Reason

::: concept title="A well-built recommendation usually comes with its own justification chain"
Structure: Problem → Evidence → Recommendation → Expected benefit.
*Billing errors have increased significantly over the past year.
Because many errors originate from manual data entry, the organization
should introduce automated validation checks. This would reduce the
number of incorrect invoices.* Problem: billing errors increased.
Cause: manual data entry. Recommendation: introduce automated
validation. Expected benefit: reduce incorrect invoices.
:::

## Recommendation Is Not a Guaranteed Result

::: warning title="Four sentences, four very different levels of certainty"
*The company should introduce automated validation to reduce billing
errors.* Here, "should introduce" is a recommendation, and "to reduce"
names an intended objective — the sentence never claims automated
validation *will definitely* eliminate errors. Compare four related
claims directly: *the system is designed to reduce errors* (intention),
*the system reduces errors* (present, ongoing fact), *the system has
reduced errors* (a completed, evidenced outcome), *the system will
eliminate errors* (a strong future prediction). All four sound similar
at a glance — their certainty is not the same at all.
:::

## "Designed To" Versus "Proven To": A Certainty Hierarchy

::: important title="A six-step ladder from stated intention to strong evidence"
*The system is designed to improve efficiency* states the system's
purpose — it doesn't prove actual success. *The system has been shown
to improve efficiency* claims real evidence of improvement. *The system
has consistently improved efficiency* is a stronger empirical claim
still. Ranked from weakest to strongest: designed to (intended
purpose) → aimed at (intended goal) → helps to (a contribution) → has
been shown to (evidence-supported) → demonstrates (a stronger evidence
claim) → proves (a very strong claim, worth evaluating critically
rather than accepting outright).
:::

## Reading Decision-Making Text

::: concept title="Situation, options, criteria, evaluation, trade-off, decision"
*The organization can either replace the existing system or upgrade
it. Replacement would provide greater flexibility but would involve
substantial implementation costs. Upgrading would be less expensive but
would preserve some of the system's existing limitations. Given the
current budget constraints, upgrading may therefore be the more
practical option.*

Situation: existing system needs improvement. Options: replace or
upgrade. Criterion: cost, flexibility, and limitations together.
Trade-off — replacement: more flexibility, higher cost; upgrade: lower
cost, existing limitations remain. Decision: upgrading may be more
practical. Notice "may therefore be" — this is not an absolute
decision; it's a qualified recommendation.
:::

## "Best" Is Rarely Absolute

::: warning title="Ask immediately: best according to which criterion?"
*This is the best solution for reducing costs* names cost reduction as
the specific dimension — not "best" in every possible respect. *This is
the best solution under the current budget constraints* names a
condition the "best" claim depends on. Whenever "best" appears, ask:
best according to what criterion?
:::

## Recognising Trade-offs in Decision Text

::: concept title="Connecting back to Lesson 63 — the same trade-off pattern, now inside a decision"
*Increasing automation may improve speed at the expense of
flexibility* — speed may rise, flexibility may fall: benefit ↔ cost.
*The approach offers greater accuracy, but this comes at the cost of
higher processing time* builds the same structure: accuracy ↑,
processing time ↑. A decision passage built around a trade-off is not
a simple recommendation — the author is explicitly weighing a gain
against a cost, not just proposing one clean improvement.
:::

## "Rather Than" for Decision Contrast

::: note title="Naming the preferred option and the rejected one in a single clause"
*The organization should improve the existing workflow rather than
replace it entirely.* Main recommendation: improve the existing
workflow. Rejected alternative: replace it entirely. Whenever "rather
than" appears in a decision passage, identify both halves explicitly:
preferred X rather than Y.
:::

## "Instead Of"

::: note title="A close cousin of 'rather than' — with the same caution attached"
*Instead of relying entirely on manual verification, the company
should introduce automated checks.* Preferred: automated checks.
Alternative/rejected: entirely manual verification. As with "rather
than" in Lesson 63, "instead of X" does not mean X was impossible — it
only means Y was chosen over X in this case.
:::

## A Recommendation Strength Map

::: framework title="From very strong to weak/optional, in one reference list"
- Very strong: must, need to, is essential to
- Strong: should, ought to, it is important to
- Moderate: may want to, might consider, could consider
- Weak / optional: could, one option is to, it may be worth considering
:::

::: note title="Strength can still shift with context"
This ladder is a reliable default, not a fixed law — the same word can
carry slightly more or less force depending on the surrounding
sentence and genre.
:::

## Policy, Exception, and Enforcement, Together

::: example title="A full policy paragraph, decoded"
**All cargo records must be verified before billing. Records that
contain incomplete information should be returned to the relevant
department for correction. In exceptional cases, however, billing may
proceed if the missing information does not affect the calculation of
charges. Any such decision must be documented and reviewed by a
supervisor.**

Rule: all cargo records must be verified before billing. Problem
handling: incomplete information → return for correction. Exception:
billing may proceed — but only if the missing information does not
affect charge calculation. Additional requirement: the decision must be
documented. Oversight: a supervisor reviews it. Compressed: verify →
correct incomplete records → exception only if calculation unaffected →
document → supervisor review.
:::

## "May" in Policy Text: Permission or Possibility?

::: warning title="The single most context-dependent modal verb in policy writing"
*Employees may work remotely* — permission: employees have the right
to. *Employees may experience delays* — possibility, not permission.
Compare directly: *employees may submit the form online* (permission)
versus *employees may experience delays when submitting the form
online* (possibility). Always check which job "may" is doing in each
specific sentence.
:::

## "Can" Versus "May"

::: note title="Ability/practical possibility versus permission"
*Employees can submit the form online* usually points to ability or
practical possibility. *Employees may submit the form online* usually
points to permission. In policy and documentation writing, this
distinction is worth holding onto carefully, even though everyday
speech often blurs it.
:::

## "Need Not" Versus "Must Not"

::: warning title="Two negative modals that are opposites of each other, not synonyms"
*Employees need not submit the form* means submitting it isn't
required — "you don't have to." *Employees must not submit the form*
means submitting it is forbidden — "you're not allowed to." These are
opposite claims dressed in similar-looking sentences, and mixing them up
inverts the actual rule.
:::

## Reading "If" Inside a Recommendation

::: important title="A recommendation attached to a condition is not an unconditional recommendation"
*If the existing system can be upgraded at a reasonable cost, the
organization should consider upgrading it rather than replacing it.*
This recommendation is not unconditional. Structure: IF condition →
THEN recommendation. Condition: upgrade possible at reasonable cost.
Recommendation: consider upgrading. Alternative: replacement.
Summarising this as "the organization should upgrade the system" drops
the condition and overstates the claim; the accurate version keeps it:
*the organization should consider upgrading the system if upgrading is
reasonably affordable.*
:::

## "It Is Recommended That…"

::: note title="A formal, passive recommendation structure common in technical writing"
*It is recommended that all users enable multi-factor authentication*
means all users are recommended to enable MFA. *It is strongly
recommended that backups be tested regularly* uses a formal subjunctive
structure — "backups be tested," not "backups are tested." The pattern
— *it is recommended / essential / important that + subject + base
verb* — appears constantly in technical and professional English: *it
is essential that the system remain available; it is important that
users follow the procedure.*
:::

## Seven Hidden Questions Behind Every Recommendation

::: framework title="Ask these of any recommendation you encounter"
- What is being recommended?
- Who should do it?
- Why?
- Under what conditions?
- What alternative is being rejected?
- What trade-off exists?
- How certain is the recommendation?
:::

## A Full Professional Recommendation Passage, Traced

::: example title="Problem, recommendation, qualification, risk, refined recommendation, and conditions, in sequence"
**Because manual verification has become increasingly time-consuming,
the organization should consider automating routine checks. However,
automation should not be introduced without first evaluating the
quality of the underlying data. Where data quality is poor, automated
processes may simply reproduce existing errors at a larger scale. A
phased implementation may therefore be preferable, beginning with
low-risk processes and expanding gradually as reliability improves.**

Problem: manual verification is time-consuming. Recommendation:
consider automation. Qualification: don't automate without first
checking data quality. Risk: poor data → automated reproduction of
errors, at larger scale. Refined recommendation: use phased
implementation. Condition: start with low-risk processes. Expansion
condition: expand as reliability improves.
:::

## "However" as Qualification, Not Just Contrast

::: important title="Connecting back to Lesson 51's argument-chain reading"
In the passage above, *however, automation should not be introduced
without first evaluating…* is not a simple contrast. It's a
**qualification** of the recommendation that came before it: first
claim — automation is desirable; then — but there is a prerequisite.
Structure: Recommendation → Limitation → Refined recommendation. Reading
"however" here as a plain contrast connector, rather than as the pivot
that reshapes the whole recommendation, loses the paragraph's actual
argument.
:::

## "Therefore," "Thus," and "Hence"

::: warning title="A conclusion signal is not proof the conclusion actually follows — connecting back to Lessons 31 and 33"
*Data quality is poor. Therefore, full automation may be
inappropriate.* Structure: evidence/problem → therefore → conclusion.
But "therefore" does not automatically make a conclusion logically
valid — the critical-reading question from earlier lessons still
applies here: does the evidence actually support this conclusion, or is
the author asserting a stronger link than the evidence justifies?
:::

## The Ultimate Signal-Word Skill

::: important title="A dense cluster of signal words means the author has shifted modes"
When a passage clusters words like should, must, may, might, could,
recommended, advisable, necessary, essential, unless, provided that,
subject to, except, otherwise, rather than, at the expense of,
therefore, or however, the author is very likely no longer just
reporting information. They may be recommending, creating a rule,
granting permission, imposing a restriction, stating a condition,
carving out an exception, comparing options, showing a trade-off,
justifying a decision, or qualifying their own earlier recommendation.
Noticing this shift in mode — from description to prescription — is
often the difference between reading a passage accurately and missing
its actual instruction entirely.
:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="obligation" pos="noun" meaning="a requirement to act in a certain way, typically signalled by 'must' or 'need to' (বাধ্যবাধকতা)" example="'Employees must wear protective equipment' states an obligation, not a suggestion."
:::

::: vocabulary word="permission" pos="noun" meaning="authorization to do something, one of the two common meanings of 'may' alongside possibility (অনুমতি)" example="'Employees may work remotely' grants permission; 'employees may experience delays' states a possibility instead."
:::

::: vocabulary word="policy scope" pos="noun phrase" meaning="the specific population or set of situations a rule or policy is stated to apply to (নীতির পরিধি)" example="The training requirement's policy scope was narrowed from 'all employees' to 'employees who handle customer payments.'"
:::

::: vocabulary word="exception clause" pos="noun phrase" meaning="the part of a rule or policy that states when the main rule does not apply (ব্যতিক্রম ধারা)" example="The exception clause allowed billing to proceed if the missing information did not affect the charge calculation."
:::

::: vocabulary word="qualification" pos="noun" meaning="a limitation or condition added to a claim or recommendation that narrows how strongly or widely it applies (শর্তাধীনতা)" example="'However, automation should not be introduced without first evaluating data quality' is a qualification of the recommendation before it."
:::

::: vocabulary word="evidentiary strength" pos="noun phrase" meaning="how strongly a piece of language is backed by proof, ranging from a stated intention to a demonstrated result (প্রমাণের শক্তি)" example="'Designed to improve efficiency' has far weaker evidentiary strength than 'has been shown to improve efficiency.'"
:::

::: vocabulary word="phased implementation" pos="noun phrase" meaning="introducing a change gradually, in stages, rather than all at once (পর্যায়ক্রমিক বাস্তবায়ন)" example="A phased implementation began with low-risk processes and expanded as reliability improved."
:::

::: vocabulary word="subjunctive construction" pos="noun phrase" meaning="a formal grammatical pattern using the base verb form after phrases like 'it is recommended that,' rather than the usual present-tense form (সাবজাংটিভ গঠন)" example="'It is essential that the system remain available' uses a subjunctive construction: 'remain,' not 'remains.'"
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read this passage once, straight through. Then identify its rule, its
scope, its exception, its modal verbs' exact function, and whether its
final sentence is a recommendation or a qualified one, before checking
your reading against the notes that follow.

> All customs declarations must be submitted electronically at least
> 24 hours before a vessel's arrival. Declarations submitted after this
> deadline may still be accepted, provided that the delay does not
> affect berth scheduling. Agents who repeatedly submit declarations
> late should be referred to the compliance team for review. This
> requirement applies to all commercial vessels except those under a
> designated humanitarian or emergency exemption. Given the operational
> disruption late declarations can cause, agents are strongly advised
> to submit well before the deadline rather than relying on the grace
> period.

Rule: declarations must be submitted electronically at least 24 hours
before arrival. Scope: all commercial vessels. Exception: designated
humanitarian or emergency exemption. Conditional exception within the
rule: late declarations "may still be accepted" — permission, not
possibility — but only "provided that" berth scheduling is unaffected.
Escalation clause: repeat late submitters "should be referred" —
recommendation, addressed to whoever manages compliance referral, not
an instruction to the agents themselves. Final sentence: "strongly
advised... rather than" is itself a recommendation, contrasting the
preferred behaviour (submit early) against a rejected reliance (the
grace period) — not a restatement of the rule, but an added layer of
guidance on top of it.

## Golden Rule

::: golden
Don't read "should," "must," "may," and "could" as ordinary verbs — read them as signals of obligation, permission, possibility, recommendation, or expectation, and for any recommendation ask what is recommended, why, under what conditions, compared with what alternative, with what trade-off, and with how much certainty.
:::

## Lesson Summary

This lesson turned your attention to text that prescribes rather than
describes. You practised separating fact from recommendation from
obligation from permission, tracking the many jobs "should," "must,"
"may," and "could" can each do depending on context. You learned to
read policy text for its full structure — rule, scope, exception,
enforcement — and to treat exception markers like "unless," "provided
that," and "subject to" as carrying real operational weight, often more
than the main rule itself. You ranked recommendation and evidence
language on a certainty scale from "designed to" up through "proven
to," and worked through full decision-making passages — situation,
options, criteria, trade-off, decision — learning to treat a stated
"best" option as best only along one named dimension. And you practised
reading "however," "therefore," and conditional "if" clauses as tools
that qualify and limit a recommendation, not as simple connective
filler.

::: important title="Why modal verbs deserve this much attention"
A recommendation, a rule, and a fact can look almost identical on the
page — the entire difference often rests on a single modal verb.
Reading advanced professional and technical English accurately means
never letting "should," "must," and "may" blur together; each one is
making a distinct, specific claim about what is required, permitted,
possible, or merely expected.
:::
