## Introduction

Lesson 61 trained you to read the precise boundary an author draws
around a concept, and between two concepts that sound alike. Today's
lesson shifts from a single frozen moment to something that moves:
time, sequence, change, and historical development.

This skill matters everywhere serious nonfiction tells a story of
change — history, biography, technology evolution, business
development, scientific discovery, political history, company history,
product evolution, process documentation. And the central trap is
always the same one: mistaking "this happened after that" for "this
happened *because of* that." A text that merely lists events in order
is not the same as a text that explains how one event produced the
next — and a confident reader has to be able to tell, sentence by
sentence, which one they're looking at.

## Time Reading Is Not Just Tense Reading

::: concept title="'Past tense = past event' is too simple for advanced text"
Many learners assume past tense always means "this happened in the
past," full stop. But advanced writing routinely packs *several* time
points into a single sentence. Take: *By the time the company
introduced automation, employees had already developed several manual
procedures.* This sentence contains two past events, and the verb form
tells you their order:

```
Manual procedures developed
        ↓
   Already completed
        ↓
Automation introduced
        ↓
    Later point
```

*Had developed* — the past perfect — signals that one past event was
already finished *before* a second past event occurred. Reading past
perfect as "just another past tense" loses this ordering entirely.
:::

## Building a Mental Timeline

::: important title="For any long, event-dense sentence, sketch the order before moving on"
*After the company had expanded its operations, it introduced a
centralized billing system.* Don't just parse the grammar and move on
— build the timeline explicitly:

```
EXPANSION
   ↓
CENTRALIZED BILLING
```

This habit costs a few seconds and pays for itself constantly once
sentences start stacking three or four events together.
:::

## Before and After

::: concept title="Two of the simplest time connectors — and one common misreading"
*Before the system was introduced, billing was performed manually.*

```
MANUAL BILLING
      ↓
SYSTEM INTRODUCED
```

*After the system was introduced, processing time decreased.*

```
SYSTEM INTRODUCED
      ↓
PROCESSING TIME ↓
```

Both diagrams look tidy — which is exactly the trap. **"After" marks
order in time; it never, by itself, proves that the first event caused
the second.**
:::

## Sequence Is Not Causation

::: warning title="The single most important distinction in this lesson"
*The company introduced automation, and profits increased the
following year.* Automation happened first; the profit increase
happened later — but the sentence alone proves nothing about
automation *causing* that increase. Plenty of other explanations are
equally consistent with the same two facts: market growth, price
changes, staff reduction, new customers, or broader economic
conditions. Whenever a text simply places two events next to each
other in time, hold the causal claim as unproven until the author
gives you something stronger than sequence alone.
:::

## Sequence Markers: Then, Subsequently, Previously, Formerly, At the Time

::: note title="Five common markers that describe order or reference time — not cause"
*The company upgraded its servers. Then, it expanded its online
services.* "Then" signals plain temporal sequence, not a causal claim.
*The policy was introduced in 2018. Subsequently, several organizations
adopted similar measures.* "Subsequently" is a more formal way of
saying "afterward." *The company previously relied on manual records*
places an event before some reference point that you need to identify
from context. *The facility was formerly used for storage* implies a
clear present contrast: it was storage before, and — the sentence
implies — it is something else now. *At the time, most cargo records
were maintained manually* leaves "the time" undefined until you track
down which period the surrounding context is actually pointing to.
:::

## Overlapping Time: Meanwhile and In the Meantime

::: concept title="Two processes running during the same stretch of time"
*The billing team was updating the tariff database. Meanwhile, the
operations team was testing the new tracking system.*

```
Billing team    ────────────────
Operations team ────────────
                    ↑
               simultaneously
```

*The system was being repaired. In the meantime, operators used a
manual procedure* describes the same kind of overlap, but specifically
as a **temporary parallel arrangement** that holds only until the
primary process finishes.
:::

## Stage Markers: At First, Eventually, Gradually, Suddenly, Initially

::: important title="Five words that locate a moment inside a larger process of change"
*At first, employees were reluctant to use the new system* signals an
initial state, and usually implies a later change is coming: *At
first, employees resisted the system, but they gradually became
comfortable with it.*

```
Resistance
   ↓
Adaptation
   ↓
Acceptance
```

*Eventually, the organization adopted the new workflow* means a final
state was reached after some period of development — but tells you
nothing about exactly when. *The system gradually became more
reliable* describes slow, continuous change, in direct contrast to
*demand suddenly increased*, which describes a rapid, unexpected shift.
*Initially, the project focused on billing automation* names an early
stage, almost always setting up a later shift in scope.
:::

## Development Across a Period: Over Time

::: example title="A single phrase that compresses an entire multi-stage transition"
*Over time, the organization shifted from manual reporting to
automated analytics* compresses a whole development into one phrase:

```
Manual Reporting
       ↓
    Transition
       ↓
Automated Analytics
```

Whenever you see "over time," expect the author to be summarising a
process that, if unpacked, would itself contain several intermediate
stages.
:::

## "Eventually" Versus "Ultimately"

::: warning title="Similar-looking words, genuinely different jobs"
*Eventually, the company adopted the system* names the final stage of
a time-based process — nothing more. *Ultimately, the experience
changed how the company approached automation* does something extra:
"ultimately" often points to a claim's **final significance or
conclusion**, not just its final moment in time. Reading "ultimately"
as a plain synonym for "eventually" can cause you to miss the author
shifting from narrating events to stating their upshot.
:::

## "Initially... Later..." as a Structure

::: example title="A common two-stage development pattern"
*Initially, the system was designed for billing. Later, it was
expanded to support inventory management.*

```
Stage 1
Billing
   ↓
Stage 2
Billing + Inventory
```

This paired structure shows up constantly in product-evolution and
process-documentation writing — watch for it as a unit, not as two
unrelated sentences.
:::

## Describing the Change Process Itself: From X to Y, Shift, Transition, Transform, Evolve, Develop

::: concept title="Six verbs and phrases for change — each with a slightly different shape"
*The organization moved from manual reporting to automated reporting*
states a state transition directly:

```
FROM
old state
 ↓
TO
new state
```

*The company shifted from product-based planning to customer-centered
planning* — "shift" usually implies a meaningful change in approach or
priority, not just any difference. *The transition from manual
processing to automation took several years* — "transition" names the
**change process itself**, not the final state: old state →
intermediate change → new state. *Digitalization transformed the way
the organization managed information* — "transform" usually implies
substantial change, though its exact degree still depends on context.
*The system evolved from a simple billing tool into an integrated
operational platform* carries a strong sense of gradual development:

```
Simple billing tool
       ↓
Expanded functionality
       ↓
Integrated platform
```

*The technology developed rapidly during the following decade* — note
that "developed" can mean growth, change, or improvement, but it does
**not always** mean "improved." Judge each case from context, not from
the word alone.
:::

## State Change Versus Event

::: important title="An occurrence is not the same fact as a condition — connecting back to Lesson 58's state model"
*The company launched the system* names an **event** — a single
occurrence. *The system was operational* names a **state** — an
ongoing condition. The two often appear together: *the company
launched the system, after which the platform became operational*
links them directly:

```
Launch event
     ↓
Operational state
```

Keeping event and state distinct matters because a passage can report
an event without yet telling you whether the resulting state actually
held — and vice versa.
:::

## Persistence and Change: Remain, Continue, Still, No Longer, Yet

::: concept title="Five markers for whether a state kept going, ended, or defied expectation"
*The system remained stable throughout the period* signals no major
state change:

```
Stable ─────────────────── Stable
```

*The organization continued to use manual verification* means a
previous state or action simply persisted. *The system was automated,
but employees still reviewed unusual transactions* — "still" flags
**unexpected persistence**: automation might lead a reader to expect
less human involvement, yet review continued anyway. *The company no
longer relied on paper-based records* marks a previous state's clear
end:

```
Paper records
     ↓
   ENDED
     ↓
Digital records
```

*The system was highly automated, yet manual review remained
necessary* — "yet" here signals a contrast between expectation and
reality: automation would suggest less human involvement, yet manual
review remained necessary regardless.
:::

## Reference Points: By Then and By the Time

::: note title="Both anchor a claim to a moment established by context"
*By then, the organization had already adopted a centralized workflow*
— "then" points back to a reference moment that has to be recovered
from context; the sentence claims adoption was already complete by
that point. *By the time the new system was deployed, employees had
already received training* gives the same structure with the
reference event named explicitly:

```
Training
   ↓
System deployment
```
:::

## "Since": Starting Point or Reason?

::: warning title="Same word, two unrelated jobs — context alone decides which"
*The company has used the system since 2022* uses "since" as a
**starting point**: 2022 → now. *Since the data was incomplete, the
system required manual verification* uses "since" to mean
**"because"** — a reason, not a moment in time. Before translating
"since" in your head, check whether it's attached to a date or period
(time) or to a full clause giving a reason (cause).
:::

## "While": Time or Contrast?

::: warning title="Another two-job word — read the surrounding clause to tell them apart"
*While the system was running, operators monitored it* uses "while"
for **time**: during the period the system ran. *While the system is
fast, it is not always accurate* uses "while" for **contrast** — much
like "although." Advanced reading means identifying which logical
relationship "while" is doing in each specific sentence, rather than
defaulting to one reading automatically.
:::

## Duration and Boundary Prepositions: During, Throughout, Within, By, For, From...Until

::: important title="Six small words that carry precise time-boundary meaning"
*During the implementation period, several problems emerged* — "during"
usually pairs with a noun phrase (*during implementation*). *Throughout
the development process, the team maintained detailed records* means
across the **entire** period, start to end:

```
START ─────────────────── END
      ↑ continuous period
```

*The problem was resolved within three days* means inside a maximum
time boundary — **not** "after exactly three days." *The system was
operational by June* names a deadline or latest point: operational no
later than June. Compare directly: *within June* means somewhere
inside the period of June, while *by June* means at the latest by
June's arrival — a real and easily blurred difference. *The system
remained offline for three hours* states a duration; compare it with
*since 9 a.m.*, which states a starting point rather than a length of
time. *The system was unavailable from Monday until Wednesday* frames
a bounded span directly:

```
Monday ───────── Wednesday
        unavailable
```
:::

## Duration Versus Frequency

::: concept title="How long something lasted is a different fact from how often it happened"
*The system was unavailable for three hours* states **duration**. *The
system failed three times* states **frequency**. These are two
genuinely different quantitative relationships, and collapsing them
together — treating "three hours" and "three times" as the same kind
of fact — loses real information from the passage.
:::

## Change Vocabulary

::: framework title="High-value change verbs, grouped by direction"
- Increase: increase, rise, grow, expand, climb, escalate
- Decrease: decrease, fall, decline, drop, reduce, shrink
- Stability: remain stable, remain unchanged, level off, stabilize
- Variation: fluctuate, vary, oscillate
:::

## "Increase" Versus "Expand," "Decline" Versus "Collapse"

::: warning title="Near-synonyms with different scope and different intensity"
*Costs increased* describes a rise in quantity or value. *Operations
expanded* describes a rise in scope or scale — not the same claim.
Separately, *demand declined* suggests a moderate, unspecified
decrease, while *demand collapsed* strongly implies a very large,
sudden decrease. Track an author's lexical intensity carefully — the
choice between "declined" and "collapsed" is itself a claim about how
severe the change was.
:::

## Trend Vocabulary: Fluctuate, Level Off, Plateau, Peak, Trough

::: note title="Connecting back to Lesson 56's data-reading vocabulary, applied to a moving timeline"
*Prices fluctuated throughout the year* means repeatedly moving up and
down — **not** continuously increasing. *After rising rapidly, demand
leveled off* means growth stopped increasing significantly:

```
Rise ↑
  ↑
  ↑
  ─────────
   stable
```

*Productivity reached a plateau after several years of improvement*
means improvement slowed or stopped at a relatively stable level. A
**peak** is the highest point and a **trough** the lowest: *demand
peaked in July before declining*.

```
      PEAK
       /\
      /  \
_____/    \____
```
:::

## Turning Point

::: concept title="The moment after which a trajectory changes significantly"
*The introduction of automation marked a turning point in the
organization's development.* A turning point can be technological,
political, economic, personal, or organizational — but in every case
it names the specific moment where the story's direction genuinely
changes, not just any notable event along the way.
:::

## Before-and-After Structure

::: warning title="A clean comparison — but still not causal proof on its own"
Many passages are organised as *Before X → X happened → After X*: *Before
automation, employees manually entered billing data. After automation,
routine calculations were performed automatically.*

```
BEFORE
Manual entry
      ↓
INTERVENTION
Automation
      ↓
AFTER
Automatic calculation
```

This is a genuinely useful structure for organising a passage — but,
exactly as with plain "before/after" and "sequence," **before-and-after
is still not causal proof by itself.**
:::

## Three Standard Development Structures

::: framework title="Historical Development Structure — common across history writing"
- Initial condition
- Trigger
- Change
- Reaction
- Consequence
- Long-term development
:::

::: framework title="Technology Evolution Structure — common across technology writing"
- Problem
- Early solution
- Limitation
- Improvement
- New capability
- Adoption
- Unintended consequence
:::

::: framework title="Biography Structure — common across biographical writing"
- Background
- Early experience
- Decision
- Action
- Setback
- Adaptation
- Achievement
- Later impact
:::

::: note title="Why these three templates matter"
Recognising which of these standard shapes a paragraph or chapter is
following — history, technology, or biography — lets you predict what
kind of move the author is likely to make next, and notice immediately
when a passage deliberately breaks from the expected pattern.
:::

## Reading Historical Causation Carefully

::: warning title="A sequence of historical events is not automatically a causal chain"
*The economic crisis was followed by political instability.* This is a
sequence claim. Unless the author explicitly says the crisis *caused*
the instability, don't construct that causal claim yourself — the
sentence, on its own, has only established order in time.
:::

## A Causal-Strength Ladder: Led To, Contributed To, Triggered, Marked

::: important title="Four causal-sounding words with four different strengths — rank them before trusting them"
*The crisis led to political instability* is an explicitly stronger
causal claim:

```
Crisis
 ↓
Mechanism / Process
 ↓
Political instability
```

— though a careful reader still looks for the mechanism connecting the
two. *Economic pressures contributed to political instability* is a
**weaker, multi-causal** claim: one factor among several, not the sole
cause. *The event triggered a rapid political response* — "triggered"
suggests an immediate initiating event, which is not necessarily the
same thing as the **root cause**. *The decision marked the beginning of
a new phase* — "marked" usually signals a boundary or transition point,
not a claim that the decision caused everything that followed it.
Ranking these four by strength (contributed to < triggered ≈ marked <
led to) before accepting any of them prevents an author's weakest
causal hedge from being read as their strongest one.
:::

## More Reference-Period Phrases: By the End Of, Over the Course Of, At the Beginning/End

::: note title="Three more ways authors anchor a claim to a stretch or point of time"
*By the end of the decade, the industry had undergone major changes* —
the decade is the reference period, and "had undergone" marks the
change as completed by that point. *Over the course of several
decades, the system gradually evolved* signals long-duration
development. *At the beginning of the process… / At the end of the
process…* builds a direct state comparison:

```
INITIAL STATE
      ↓
    PROCESS
      ↓
FINAL STATE
```
:::

## Time and Causation Combined

::: important title="The most advanced pattern in this lesson — sequence and cause in one sentence"
*After the company introduced the system, processing times decreased
because several manual checks had been eliminated* packs four things
into one sentence: **time** ("after"), an **event** (system
introduced), an **outcome** (processing times decreased), and a
**cause** (manual checks eliminated).

```
SYSTEM INTRODUCED
       ↓
MANUAL CHECKS ELIMINATED
       ↓
PROCESSING TIME ↓
```

Here, sequence and causation are both genuinely present — but notice
that it's the explicit "because," not the "after," doing the causal
work.
:::

## Time as Condition: Once and Until

::: concept title="Two words that fuse a moment in time with a logical condition"
*Once the system receives valid data, it automatically generates the
invoice* — "once" means "immediately after / when this condition
becomes true":

```
Valid data received
       ↓
Condition satisfied
       ↓
Invoice generated
```

*The system remains pending until payment is verified* — "until"
describes a state that continues up to a triggering event:

```
PENDING
  │
  │ payment not verified
  │
  ↓
PAYMENT VERIFIED
  ↓
PAID
```

*Once the payment is verified, the invoice is finalized* shows "once"
doing both jobs at once — temporal sequence and condition, fused into
a single word.
:::

## "No Longer" and "Still" Together

::: example title="A common pairing that shows partial, not total, transition"
*The organization no longer uses paper records, but it still relies on
manual verification for exceptional cases.*

```
Paper records
   ↓
NO LONGER
   ↓
Digital records
```

— but —

```
Manual verification
   ↓
  STILL EXISTS
```

Read together, these two markers show the system has **not** become
fully automated — one process ended while another persisted alongside
the change.
:::

## A Full Integrated Example

::: example title="Timeline, state change, causation-hedging, and a turning point, all in one paragraph"
**Initially, the port relied on manual cargo records. As cargo volume
increased, however, the limitations of this approach became more
apparent. The organization subsequently introduced a digital tracking
system, which reduced the amount of routine data entry required from
operators. Over time, the system was expanded to include billing and
reporting functions. Although these changes improved operational
visibility, they did not eliminate manual work entirely. By the end of
the implementation period, employees were still required to review
unusual transactions and correct incomplete records.**

```
MANUAL RECORDS
      ↓
CARGO VOLUME ↑
      ↓
LIMITATIONS BECAME VISIBLE
      ↓
DIGITAL TRACKING
      ↓
ROUTINE DATA ENTRY ↓
      ↓
SYSTEM EXPANDED
      ↓
BILLING + REPORTING
      ↓
VISIBILITY ↑
      ↓
     BUT
      ↓
MANUAL EXCEPTIONS REMAINED
```
:::

::: golden title="The paragraph's real argument, underneath its timeline"
The author isn't just narrating history here: the underlying claim is
that technology evolved progressively, but automation did not
eliminate human work — it changed the *type* of human involvement, the
same mechanism-and-model thread built across Lesson 58 and Lesson 60,
now traced through time.
:::

## A Time-Reading Master Algorithm

::: framework title="The full sequence for reading any process, history, or development passage"
- Find the time frame
- Identify the reference point
- Mark before / after
- Identify the initial state
- Identify the events
- Identify the state changes
- Identify the duration
- Identify simultaneous events
- Identify turning points
- Identify the sequence
- Separate sequence from causation
- Identify causal language
- Identify conditions
- Identify the final state
- Identify long-term development
- Compress into a timeline
:::

## Timeline and Causal Map Are Two Different Maps

::: warning title="Chronological order and causal structure are not the same relationship"
For advanced text, a timeline alone isn't enough. Build two separate
maps. A **timeline** simply orders events: A → B → C → D. A **causal
map** shows what actually produced what, which can look quite
different:

```
A
↓
B
↓
D
```

Here C sits in the timeline but outside the causal chain — a detail a
timeline-only reading would quietly erase. Chronological order and
causal relationship are not one fact; keep both maps and compare them.
:::

## High-Value Chunks From This Lesson

::: important title="Sequence, duration, and causal-strength markers to keep active while reading"
**Stage and sequence:** at first, initially, subsequently, later,
eventually, ultimately, previously, formerly, at the time, meanwhile,
in the meantime, over time, throughout the period, during the process

**Reference points and boundaries:** by the time, by then, by the end
of, before, after, once, until, since, for, within, from…to…,
from…until…

**Pace of change:** gradually, suddenly, increasingly, no longer,
still, remain unchanged, remain stable, continue to

**Change process:** shift from…to…, transition from…to…, evolve into,
develop into

**Causal strength:** give rise to, lead to, contribute to, trigger,
mark the beginning of, mark a turning point

**Long span:** over the course of, in the following years, in
subsequent years
:::

## A Six-Question Deep Reading Drill

::: framework title="Run these questions on every historical, biographical, or development paragraph"
- What was the initial state?
- What changed?
- What happened next?
- What caused the change?
- What was the final state?
- Was the sequence actually causal, or merely chronological?
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read this passage once, straight through. Then build a timeline, a
separate causal map, and list every stage marker and causal-strength
word you can find, before checking your reading against the notes that
follow.

> The terminal initially processed all cargo manifests by hand.
> Subsequently, as shipping volumes grew, delays in manifest processing
> began to affect berth scheduling. Management therefore introduced a
> digital manifest system, which contributed to shorter processing
> times, although staff shortages during the same period also played a
> role. Over the following years, the system was gradually extended to
> cover customs documentation as well. By the end of the decade, most
> manifests were processed automatically, yet supervisors still
> reviewed any manifest flagged as incomplete or inconsistent.

Timeline: manual processing → volume growth → berth-scheduling delays →
digital system introduced → shorter processing times → gradual
extension to customs documentation → near-total automation, with
flagged-manifest review still in place. Causal map: the passage
explicitly hedges its strongest causal claim — "contributed to," not
"caused" or "led to" — and names a *second*, competing explanation
(staff shortages) for the same outcome, so a causal map here should
show two converging arrows into "shorter processing times," not one.
Stage markers: initially, subsequently, over the following years,
gradually, by the end of the decade. Persistence marker: "still
reviewed," confirming the change was not complete even by the
passage's final reference point.

## Golden Rule

::: golden
When reading a process, history, biography, or development story, don't read events as a simple list — build a timeline, identify state changes and turning points, separate chronological sequence from causation, and reconstruct how the initial state actually developed into the final state.
:::

## Lesson Summary

This lesson moved reading from a single frozen sentence to a passage in
motion. You learned that past tense alone doesn't reveal time
relationships — constructions like "had developed" order two past
events against each other — and you practised building explicit
timelines for dense, event-stacked sentences. You separated
chronological sequence from causation throughout, ranking causal
language on a strength ladder from "contributed to" up through
"triggered," "marked," and "led to." You worked through the precise
differences between remain/continue/still/no longer/yet, between
since/for/within/by/until, and between duration and frequency. And you
practised recognising the standard multi-stage shapes that historical,
technological, and biographical writing tend to follow, while building
two separate maps — a timeline and a causal map — for any passage
where order and cause might not be telling quite the same story.

::: important title="Why the two maps matter going forward"
A timeline tells you what happened, in what order. A causal map tells
you what actually produced what. Advanced nonfiction constantly invites
you to collapse these into one — and the single most valuable habit
this lesson builds is refusing to do that automatically, holding
"after" and "because" apart until the author has genuinely earned the
stronger claim.
:::

## Practice: Test What You've Learned

Work through every question yourself before checking anything.

::: important title="Before you start"
For every sentence below, state explicitly whether it makes a
chronological claim, a causal claim, or both — "these events are
connected" is not yet a complete answer.
:::

### Part A — Time Relationships and Tense

1. **By the time the audit began, the finance team had already reconciled the previous quarter's records.** Identify the two past events and state which one finished first.
2. **After the warehouse had relocated, delivery times improved.** Build a simple timeline for this sentence the way this lesson diagrammed "expansion → centralized billing."
3. **At the time, most invoices were still processed manually.** Explain what additional information you would need to know exactly which period "the time" refers to.

### Part B — Sequence Versus Causation

4. **The company hired additional staff, and customer complaints decreased the following month.** Explain why this sentence does not, by itself, prove that hiring caused the drop in complaints.
5. **Rising fuel costs contributed to the airline's decision to raise fares.** Explain why "contributed to" is a weaker causal claim than "caused," and name what else the sentence leaves open.
6. **The new regulation triggered a wave of compliance audits.** Explain why "triggered" does not necessarily mean the regulation was the audits' root cause.

### Part C — Persistence, Duration, and Boundaries

7. **The plant remained closed for six months, then gradually resumed full production.** Identify the duration, the state change, and the pace-of-change word in this sentence.
8. **The service was unavailable within the first two hours of the outage, but was fully restored by midnight.** Explain the difference in meaning between "within" and "by" as used here.
9. **The department no longer files paper reports, but staff still keep a manual backup for high-value transactions.** Explain what has changed and what has not, using this lesson's "no longer" + "still" pairing.

### Part D — Trends and Development Structures

10. **User engagement climbed steadily for two years before leveling off.** Identify which change-vocabulary category ("increase," "decrease," "stability," or "variation") applies to each half of this sentence.
11. **Sales fluctuated throughout the quarter before peaking in December.** Explain why "fluctuated" rules out reading this as one continuous increase.
12. **Classify the following short narrative by which of this lesson's three structures it best matches — historical development, technology evolution, or biography — and justify your choice: "A founder started the company after a personal setback, faced early failures, adapted the business model, and eventually achieved recognition in the industry."**

### Part E — Full Passage Analysis

Return to this lesson's guided reading passage about the terminal's
manifest-processing system.

13. Build the passage's timeline in your own words, without reusing the author's exact wording.
14. Build a separate causal map for the passage, and explain why it should show two arrows converging on "shorter processing times" rather than one.
15. List every persistence or change marker in the passage ("still," "gradually," and so on), and state what each one signals.
16. Explain why summarising this passage as "the digital system caused faster processing" would overstate what the author actually claimed.
