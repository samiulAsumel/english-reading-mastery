## Introduction

Today's topic makes a real difference to how precisely you read. An
author rarely just writes:

> The decision was good.

They might instead write:

> The decision was extremely effective.
> The decision was relatively effective.
> The decision was surprisingly effective.

These are not three ways of saying the same thing. Adjectives and
adverbs are not just grammar — they carry the author's judgment,
certainty, emphasis, and tone.

## What Is an Adjective?

An adjective gives information about a noun or pronoun — answering
*what kind? which one? how much? what quality?*

> a difficult problem · an effective strategy · an experienced manager ·
> a complex situation

*problem* → *difficult* · *strategy* → *effective* · *manager* →
*experienced* · *situation* → *complex*

## Where Does an Adjective Sit?

Most commonly, directly before its noun:

> an important decision · a successful company · a complex problem ·
> useful information

But an adjective can also come after the noun, connected through a
**linking verb**:

> The problem is difficult. · The strategy is effective. · The
> situation became complex.

## Adjectives After a Linking Verb

::: concept title="Some verbs connect a subject to its state, not to an action"
Verbs like *be, become, seem, appear, feel, look, remain, stay* don't
describe an action — they connect the subject to a description of its
current state.

**The problem is difficult.** · **The situation became complicated.** ·
**The idea seems reasonable.** · **The explanation appears
convincing.**

In each case, the adjective describes the *subject's* condition.
:::

## What Is an Adverb?

An adverb gives extra information about a verb, an adjective, another
adverb, or even an entire sentence — answering *how? to what degree?
when? where? how certain? how often?*

> **People carefully analyze information.** — how do they analyze?
> *carefully.*
> **She speaks slowly.** — how does she speak? *slowly.*

## Adjective vs. Adverb

::: example title="Adjective describes a noun; adverb modifies an action or description"
**He is a careful researcher.** — *careful* describes *researcher*
(adjective).

**He works carefully.** — *carefully* describes *how* the work happens
(adverb).
:::

## Many, Not All, Adverbs End in -ly

> careful → carefully · quick → quickly · effective → effectively ·
> clear → clearly · significant → significantly · gradual → gradually

::: note title="Don't rely on the ending alone"
*fast, hard, well, often, very* are all adverbs, none of them ending in
*-ly*. Check the word's *function* in the sentence — what it's
modifying — not its spelling.
:::

## Adverbs of Degree — "How Much?"

These matter enormously in non-fiction: *very, extremely, highly,
relatively, fairly, quite, somewhat, slightly, significantly,
particularly.*

::: example title="Degree words change the strength of a claim"
**The problem is very difficult.** — a high degree of difficulty.

**The problem is extremely difficult.** — even stronger.

**The problem is slightly difficult.** — a low degree.
:::

## A Rough Scale for Degree

```
slightly → somewhat → fairly → quite → very → extremely
```

This isn't a precise mathematical scale — context can shift each word's
real strength — but it's a useful rough ordering to keep in mind.

## VERY vs. EXTREMELY

> **The result is very important.** — strong importance.
> **The result is extremely important.** — even stronger emphasis.

Academic writing often reaches for *highly significant* or
*particularly important* to signal this same kind of emphasis.

## HIGHLY

A very common formal intensifier: *highly effective · highly important
· highly successful · highly influential.*

> **This is a highly effective method.** — *highly* raises the degree of
> *effective*.

## RELATIVELY

::: important title="relatively means 'compared to something else', not 'absolutely'"
**relatively = compared to something else, not in an absolute sense.**

**The method is relatively simple.** — not claiming the method is
simple on its own terms; claiming it's simple *compared to other
methods*.

**The book is relatively easy to read.** — easier than other, harder
books — not necessarily easy in any absolute sense.
:::

## PARTICULARLY

**particularly = especially, specifically for this case**

> **This is particularly important for beginners.** — not equally
> important for everyone; specifically emphasised for beginners.

## SIGNIFICANTLY — Two Meanings

::: important title="significantly can mean two different things"
**Meaning 1 — noticeably/considerably:** Performance improved
significantly. — a noticeable amount of improvement.

**Meaning 2 — a marker of importance:** Significantly, the researchers
found no major difference. — here the author is signalling: *this fact
deserves your particular attention.*
:::

## Adverbs of Certainty

These reveal how confident the author actually is: *certainly, clearly,
obviously, probably, possibly, perhaps, likely, unlikely, apparently.*

::: example title="Certainty words change the strength of a claim, not just its wording"
**This causes the problem.** — a direct, strong claim.

**This probably causes the problem.** — the author shows some
uncertainty.

**This may cause the problem.** — even more open to possibility.
:::

## A Rough Certainty Scale

```
certainly → probably → possibly
```

Certainty decreases as you move along this scale.

::: example title="The same claim at three levels of confidence"
**Certainly:** This is certainly important. — strong confidence.
**Probably:** This is probably important. — likely, not certain.
**Possibly:** This could possibly be important. — a real possibility,
nothing more.
:::

## Modal + Adverb

A combination you'll meet constantly in real books: *may possibly, will
probably, can often, may significantly, could potentially.*

::: example title="Breaking down a modal + adverb + verb chain"
**Social environment can significantly influence behavior.**

*can* → possibility/ability · *significantly* → degree · *influence* →
main verb · *behavior* → object.
:::

## Adverbs of Frequency — "How Often?"

> always → usually → often → sometimes → rarely → never

## Position of Frequency Adverbs

::: example title="Frequency adverbs usually sit before the main verb — but after 'be'"
**People often make mistakes.** · **Researchers usually examine the
evidence.** — before the main verb.

**People are often influenced by emotions.** — after *be*.
:::

## How an Adverb Reveals the Author's Tone

::: important title="The same claim, four different levels of confidence"
**The theory is wrong.** — flat, direct.
**The theory is probably wrong.** — lower certainty.
**The theory is clearly wrong.** — higher certainty.
**The theory is arguably wrong.** — the author is saying: *this is a
defensible interpretation, but not necessarily the only one.*

Each version makes a genuinely different claim about how confident the
author is — this is some of the most valuable information in academic
reading.
:::

## Hedging — A Cornerstone of Academic English

::: concept title="Academic writers often avoid absolute claims on purpose"
Instead of stating something as flatly certain, writers often reach for:
*may, might, could, tends to, appears to, seems to, is likely to,
possibly, probably.*

**Strong:** Stress causes poor decisions.

**Hedged:** Stress may contribute to poor decisions.

**More cautious still:** Stress may be associated with poorer
decision-making.

This softening of a claim's strength is called **hedging**.
:::

## Why Do Authors Hedge?

::: example title="Because evidence rarely proves absolute certainty"
**Exercise improves mental health.** — a broad, direct claim.

**Exercise may improve mental health.** — genuine uncertainty is
acknowledged.

**Exercise has been associated with better mental health.** — describes
a *relationship* the evidence shows, without claiming direct causation.

Recognising this pattern makes research papers and serious non-fiction
dramatically easier to read accurately.
:::

## Adjective + Preposition, Revisited

Connecting back to Lesson 7:

> interested in · responsible for · aware of · familiar with · similar
> to · different from · capable of · important for

> Researchers are interested in human behavior. · People should be
> aware of their biases. · The two methods are similar to each other.

## Stacking Adverb + Adjective + Noun

::: example title="Reading a stacked modifier chain from the outside in"
**a highly effective communication strategy**

*strategy* = head noun · *communication* = noun modifier · *effective*
= adjective · *highly* = adverb modifying *effective*.

```
highly → effective → communication → strategy
```
:::

## Another Stacked Noun Phrase

::: example title="Peeling back the layers"
**an extremely important psychological factor**

*factor* = head noun · *psychological* = what type of factor ·
*important* = the factor's quality · *extremely* = the degree of
*important*.

Core: *factor*.
:::

## Reading Strategy: Find the Head Noun First

Connecting back to Lesson 3: the technique is unchanged, but today's
modifiers are degree and certainty words rather than plain description,
so it's worth re-testing the habit here.

::: example title="However many modifiers stack up, the head noun anchors the phrase"
**The highly influential ideas of several experienced researchers…**

Head noun: **ideas**

Modifiers: *highly influential* (before) · *of several experienced
researchers* (after).

Find the head noun first, then add the details back around it.
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read the paragraph once, slowly, and underline every word that signals
degree, certainty, or frequency rather than plain description.

> People are often influenced by factors that they do not consciously
> recognize. These influences can be particularly important when
> individuals are making complex decisions. However, people may not
> always be aware of how strongly their emotions affect their judgment.
> Researchers have therefore increasingly examined the unconscious
> processes that may contribute to decision-making. Although these
> processes can be difficult to measure directly, they may significantly
> influence human behavior.

Notice the author's language choices:

**often** — frequency: how often?
**particularly** — emphasis: specifically important here.
**may not always** — uncertainty combined with frequency.
**increasingly** — a trend, growing over time.
**unconsciously** — the opposite condition of awareness.
**directly** — how something is measured.
**significantly** — degree/emphasis.

## The Paragraph's Hidden Meaning

::: important title="Literal content vs. the author's actual stance"
The literal content: people can be influenced by unconscious factors.

But the author's actual stance, carried entirely by the hedging words:
these factors **may** influence behavior — the author is not claiming
absolute certainty.

Missing this distinction means missing exactly how confident the writer
actually is — which is often the whole point of a careful academic
sentence.
:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="obvious" pos="adjective" meaning="easy to see or understand; clear (স্পষ্ট)" example="The connection between the two events was not obvious at first."
:::

::: vocabulary word="likely" pos="adjective" meaning="probable; expected to happen (সম্ভাব্য)" example="People with more experience are likely to make faster decisions."
:::

::: vocabulary word="potential" pos="adjective" meaning="possible, though not yet certain or realized (সম্ভাব্য/সম্ভাবনাময়)" example="Researchers identified several potential causes of the problem."
:::

::: vocabulary word="considerable" pos="adjective" meaning="large in amount or degree (যথেষ্ট/উল্লেখযোগ্য)" example="The study required a considerable amount of time."
:::

::: vocabulary word="demonstrate" pos="verb" meaning="to show clearly that something is true (প্রদর্শন করা/প্রমাণ করা)" example="The results clearly demonstrate a consistent pattern."
:::

::: vocabulary word="observe" pos="verb" meaning="to notice or watch something carefully (পর্যবেক্ষণ করা)" example="This behavior is rarely observed outside laboratory conditions."
:::

::: vocabulary word="significant" pos="adjective" meaning="large or important enough to be worth noting (উল্লেখযোগ্য)" example="The researchers found a significant improvement in performance."
:::

::: note title="Learn these as whole chunks"
*highly effective · highly influential · relatively simple ·
particularly important · significantly improve · strongly influence ·
potentially useful · probably correct · possibly related · clearly
demonstrate · carefully examine · generally accepted · commonly used ·
increasingly important · rarely observed*

Recognising these shapes instantly, as single units, is what fast,
accurate reading is built from.
:::

## Golden Rule

::: framework title="Three questions for any adjective or adverb"
- If it's an adjective — which noun does it describe?
- If it's an adverb — what does it modify?
- Either way — what does it tell me about the author's meaning or certainty?
:::

Never jump straight to a Bangla translation. Answer these three
questions first, and the word's real contribution to the sentence
becomes clear. Everything you met today collapses into one small map:

```
ADJECTIVE   → describes a noun/state
ADVERB      → modifies action / description / degree / certainty / frequency
DEGREE      → very / highly / extremely / relatively
CERTAINTY   → certainly / probably / possibly / apparently
FREQUENCY   → always / usually / often / rarely
HEDGING     → may / might / could / tends to / appears to
```

::: golden
An adjective or adverb is never just decoration — it's the author telling you how certain, how strong, or how frequent something is. Read it for that signal, not only for its dictionary meaning.
:::

## Lesson Summary

Today you learned that an adjective describes a noun while an adverb
modifies a verb, adjective, another adverb, or a whole sentence — and
that neither is decoration. Degree words (*very, highly, relatively,
extremely*) tell you how strong a claim is; certainty words (*certainly,
probably, possibly*) tell you how confident the author actually is; and
hedging language (*may, might, tends to, appears to*) is a deliberate
academic habit for avoiding overstated claims. You also practised
peeling back a stacked adverb + adjective + noun modifier chain to find
the head noun underneath it.
