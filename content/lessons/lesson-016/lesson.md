## Introduction

Today's goal is to separate, cleanly, who is making a claim from what
the claim actually is — and how confidently it's being made. This
matters across psychology, history, science, business, philosophy, and
leadership writing alike, because non-fiction is built almost entirely
out of claims, opinions, findings, beliefs, and explanations, each one
attached to a source.

## Direct Speech vs. Reported Speech

> **Direct:** The researcher said, "Stress affects decision-making." —
> the researcher's exact words sit inside quotation marks.

> **Reported:** The researcher said that stress affects
> decision-making. — the author folds the researcher's statement into
> their own sentence.

This second shape is **reported speech.**

## What Is a "That"-Clause?

::: concept title="that + a claim, acting as a verb's object"
**Researchers believe that stress affects decision-making.**

Main clause: *Researchers believe.* Then: *that stress affects
decision-making.* This whole stretch is the **that-clause** — it answers
the question *researchers believe what?*
:::

## The Most Important Pattern

Non-fiction is saturated with this shape:

**Subject + reporting verb + that + claim**

> Researchers argue that… · Studies suggest that… · Evidence indicates
> that… · The author claims that… · Experts believe that… · Scientists
> have found that… · The report shows that…

Recognising this shape instantly makes an entire paragraph's logical
structure far easier to follow.

## What Is a Reporting Verb?

A **reporting verb** names how something was said, thought, or claimed:
*say, argue, claim, suggest, believe, think, explain, report, indicate,
show, demonstrate, conclude, assume, propose, acknowledge, emphasize,
predict.*

::: important title="Not every reporting verb commits to the same strength of claim"
This distinction is the heart of today's lesson.
:::

## "Say" vs. "Argue" vs. "Suggest"

::: example title="Three verbs, three different commitments"
**The author says that social environments influence behavior.** —
simply reporting a statement.

**The author argues that social environments influence behavior.** —
implies the author is defending this position with reasons or evidence,
not just stating it.

**The author suggests that social environments influence behavior.** —
a comparatively tentative, less forceful claim.

**say → report. argue → position/argument. suggest →
possibility/interpretation.**
:::

## Reading a Claim's Strength

::: example title="The same underlying idea, three levels of confidence"
**The evidence shows that the method works.** — strong.
**The evidence suggests that the method works.** — comparatively less
strong.
**The evidence may indicate that the method works.** — even more
cautious.

Academic writers carefully control certainty this way, sentence by
sentence.
:::

## "That" Is Often Dropped

::: note title="A missing 'that' doesn't mean there's no noun clause"
**Researchers believe that the method is effective.**
**Researchers believe the method is effective.**

Both are grammatical, and both mean the same thing. When reading, expect
to recognise a noun clause even when *that* isn't visibly there.
:::

## "That" Is Not Always a Clause Introducer

::: important title="Two very different jobs for the same word — callback to Lesson 11"
**I know that the study is important.** — *that* is a conjunction
introducing a noun clause.

**The book that I bought is useful.** — *that* is a relative pronoun,
describing *book*.

**I know that the study is important.** → that-clause, answering *I
know what?*
**The study that I read was interesting.** → relative clause, answering
*which study?*
:::

## Noun Clause vs. Relative Clause

::: example title="Side by side"
**A:** I believe that the study is useful. — *that the study is useful*
is a noun clause, the object of *believe.* *I believe what?* → *that
the study is useful.*

**B:** The study that I read was useful. — *that I read* is a relative
clause, describing *study.* *Which study?* → *the study that I read.*
:::

## A Simple Test

::: framework title="Testing whether 'that' introduces a noun clause or a relative clause"
- Does everything after "that" form a complete idea on its own? → noun clause (I believe that [the study is useful] — complete on its own.)
- Does "that" itself fill a grammatical role inside the clause? → relative clause (The study that [I read ___] — "that" stands in for "study," the thing being read.)
:::

## Reporting Verb + "That"-Clause — Worth Knowing on Sight

> researchers argue that · researchers suggest that · studies show that
> · evidence indicates that · experts believe that · the author claims
> that · the report states that · scientists have found that · scholars
> conclude that · critics point out that

Learn each of these as one recognisable reading chunk.

## "Studies Show That…"

::: example title="Separating the source from the claim"
**Studies show that regular exercise improves cognitive performance.**

*Studies* = subject/source. *show* = reporting verb. *that regular
exercise improves cognitive performance* = the that-clause. Main claim:
*regular exercise improves cognitive performance.* Source: *studies* —
worth keeping distinct.
:::

## "Evidence Indicates That…"

::: example title="A claim attributed to evidence, not stated outright"
**Evidence indicates that the problem is more complex than
expected.**

The author doesn't flatly assert *the problem is definitely...* — they
route the claim through its source:

```
Source: evidence
   ↓
Reporting verb: indicates
   ↓
Claim: problem is more complex
```
:::

## "The Author Argues That…"

::: example title="Tracking one source's position against another's"
**The author argues that technology has changed the way people
communicate.**

Source: *author.* Reporting verb: *argues.* Claim: *technology has
changed communication.*

If the next sentence reads: **However, other researchers have suggested
that the effect is more limited.** — you're now tracking two distinct
positions: the author's strong argument, versus other researchers'
alternative interpretation.
:::

## Reported Speech and Tense

::: concept title="Tense can shift when speech is reported"
**Direct:** He said, "I am tired." **Reported:** He said that he was
tired.

**Direct:** She said, "I finished the work." **Reported:** She said
that she had finished the work.

This shift is called **backshift** — but reading it well needs more
than mechanical memorisation; context still matters.
:::

## Backshift — The Basic Pattern

> "I am working." → He said that he was working.
> "I know the answer." → He said that he knew the answer.
> "I have finished the study." → He said that he had finished the
> study.
> "I will return." → He said that he would return.

## But Backshift Doesn't Always Happen

::: important title="A general truth can keep its present tense even when reported"
**The researcher said that water boils at 100°C.**

Because this is a general truth, the present tense stays. Don't assume
"reported speech = always past tense" as a rigid rule — check whether
the reported content is a general truth first.
:::

## Reporting Verb + "To" + Verb

Another common shape:

> He told me to wait. · The researcher asked participants to complete
> the task. · The manager encouraged employees to communicate openly.

**Pattern:** reporting verb + object + to + verb

> tell someone to do · ask someone to do · encourage someone to do ·
> advise someone to do · remind someone to do · warn someone to do

## "Say" vs. "Tell"

::: warning title="A common mistake worth recognising, even if you never make it yourself"
❌ *He said me…* — not correct.

✅ *He said that…* or *He told me that…*

**say** → say + something, or say that… **tell** → tell + someone +
something, or tell + someone + that…
:::

## A Reporting-Verb Strength Map

| Strength | Verbs |
|---|---|
| Stronger / more direct | demonstrate · establish · show · confirm |
| Moderate | indicate · argue · maintain · report |
| More cautious | suggest · imply · appear to indicate · may indicate |
| Very cautious | speculate · hypothesize · propose |

Context can shift any of these — this is a rough map, not an absolute
scale.

## Author's Position vs. Someone Else's Position

::: example title="A single sentence can carry two competing claims"
**Some researchers argue that social media has improved communication.
However, others suggest that it has created new forms of isolation.**

Group 1's claim: social media has improved communication. Group 2's
claim: social media has created new forms of isolation. *However* signals
the contrast — the author is deliberately placing two positions side by
side.
:::

## Attribution

**Attribution** means showing whose idea or claim something is.

::: concept title="Don't blur the author's own claim with someone else's"
**According to Kahneman, people often rely on mental shortcuts when
making decisions.**

*According to Kahneman* = the source. Claim: *people often rely on
mental shortcuts.* Never assume the author's own view and a cited
source's view are automatically the same thing.
:::

## "According To…"

> According to researchers,… · According to the report,… · According to
> the author,…

Meaning: *in X's view* / *according to X's information.* The moment you
see this shape, expect the information that follows to be attributed
specifically to X.

## "It Is Argued That…" — Passive Reporting

::: example title="A passive structure that can leave the source deliberately vague"
**Active:** Some researchers argue that the policy is ineffective.
**Passive:** It is argued that the policy is ineffective.

Here, *who argues?* has been left out — the focus stays on the claim
itself, not on a specific researcher.
:::

## "It Has Been Suggested That…"

::: example title="Passive + Present Perfect + a that-clause"
**It has been suggested that stress affects memory.**

*has been suggested* = passive + present perfect. Main claim: *stress
affects memory.* Who suggested it? Not specified — very common in
academic writing.
:::

## "It Is Widely Believed That…"

::: important title="Widely believed is not the same thing as proven"
**It is widely believed that early experiences influence adult
behavior.**

Structure: *It is widely believed* (passive + adverb *widely*) + *that
early experiences influence adult behavior* (that-clause).

Meaning: this is a commonly held belief — but "widely believed" is not
itself evidence. This distinction is essential for reading critically.
:::

## Reporting Verb + Embedded Question

Not every clause after a reporting verb starts with *that.*

::: concept title="whether introduces an embedded question, not a settled claim"
**Researchers examined whether stress affects memory.**

*whether stress affects memory* = an embedded question — the underlying
question is *does stress affect memory?* — not yet answered by this
sentence alone.
:::

## "Whether" vs. "That"

::: important title="A settled claim vs. an open question — callback to Lesson 12"
**Researchers found that stress affects memory.** — a claim: *stress
affects memory.*

**Researchers investigated whether stress affects memory.** — the
answer isn't settled yet; this sentence only tells you that the
question was investigated.
:::

## "What"-Clause

::: example title="Another kind of embedded question, functioning as a noun clause"
**Researchers examined what participants remembered.**

*what participants remembered* = the object of *examined.* Compare:
**Researchers examined the participants' memories.** — a similar idea,
expressed differently.
:::

## "Why / How / When"-Clause

> Researchers investigated why people make certain decisions. · The
> study examined how people respond to stress. · The researchers
> recorded when participants changed their decisions.

These are all embedded questions — no question mark, but a question's
structure still sits inside the sentence.

## Today's Mega Sentence

::: example title="Two sources, two claims, one contrast"
**Several researchers have argued that people tend to rely on mental
shortcuts when making decisions, while other scholars have suggested
that the effects of these shortcuts depend on the context in which
decisions are made.**

**Reporting clause 1:** Several researchers have argued. Claim: *people
tend to rely on mental shortcuts.* *when making decisions* → reduced
time clause; full form: *when they are making decisions.*

**Contrast:** *while* signals a contrast between the two positions.

**Reporting clause 2:** other scholars have suggested. Claim: *effects
of shortcuts depend on context.*

**Relative clause:** *context in which decisions are made* — the
context in which decisions happen; *are made* is passive.
:::

## How an Author Builds an Argument

A very common non-fiction paragraph shape:

::: framework title="Claim/source → explanation → evidence → contrast → conclusion"
- Who is making the claim?
- What are they claiming?
- How confidently are they claiming it?
- What evidence do they offer?
- Is there another position?
- What conclusion does the author draw at the end?
:::

**Researchers argue that X. They support this claim with evidence from
several studies. However, other researchers suggest that Y. Therefore,
the available evidence should be interpreted carefully.**

Read this way, every paragraph becomes something to interrogate, not
just something to translate.

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="establish" pos="verb" meaning="to show something to be true through solid evidence (প্রতিষ্ঠিত করা)" example="The study established a clear link between stress and memory loss."
:::

::: vocabulary word="confirm" pos="verb" meaning="to state or show that something is definitely true (নিশ্চিত করা)" example="A follow-up study confirmed the original finding."
:::

::: vocabulary word="maintain" pos="verb" meaning="to state firmly that something is true, often despite disagreement (দাবি করা/বজায় রাখা)" example="The author maintains that the policy is effective, even though critics disagree."
:::

::: vocabulary word="acknowledge" pos="verb" meaning="to accept or admit that something is true (স্বীকার করা)" example="The researchers acknowledge that their sample size was small."
:::

::: vocabulary word="emphasize" pos="verb" meaning="to give special importance to something (জোর দেওয়া)" example="The report emphasizes that further research is needed."
:::

::: vocabulary word="speculate" pos="verb" meaning="to form a theory without firm evidence (অনুমান করা)" example="Some scientists speculate that the pattern may be linked to diet."
:::

::: note title="Chunks worth learning as whole units"
*argue that · claim that · suggest that · believe that · assume that ·
indicate that · show that · demonstrate that · report that · explain
that · conclude that · acknowledge that · emphasize that · point out
that · according to · it is argued that · it has been suggested that ·
it is widely believed that · investigate whether · examine how ·
determine why · consider whether*
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read the paragraph once, slowly, tracking every source and claim.

> Researchers have long debated whether technology improves the quality
> of human communication. Some scholars argue that digital tools make it
> easier for people to communicate across geographical distances. They
> point out that individuals can maintain relationships even when they
> live far apart. Other researchers, however, suggest that frequent
> digital communication does not necessarily lead to deeper
> relationships. They argue that the quality of communication depends on
> how people use these tools rather than on the tools themselves. Recent
> studies have indicated that both positions may contain some truth,
> although the effects appear to vary depending on the social context.

**1** — *whether technology improves…* → an open question, not yet
settled.

**2** — Source: *Some scholars.* Claim: *digital tools make
communication easier.*

**3** — *They* → some scholars. Reporting verb: *point out.* Claim:
*individuals can maintain relationships.*

**4** — Source: *Other researchers.* Contrast: *however.* Claim:
*frequent digital communication does not necessarily lead to deeper
relationships.*

**5** — *how people use these tools* → embedded question/noun clause,
inside a further claim from the same source.

**6** — Source: *Recent studies.* Reporting verb: *have indicated.*
Claim: *both positions may contain some truth.* *may* → hedging,
uncertainty.

## Today's Reading Skill

::: golden
Before absorbing any claim in non-fiction, split it into five parts: source, reporting verb, claim, certainty, and claim owner. That habit alone separates a careful reader from someone just decoding words.
:::

::: example title="Annotating a sentence this way"
**Researchers argue that people behave differently under stress.**

**SOURCE:** Researchers **REPORTING VERB:** argue **CLAIM:** people
behave differently under stress **CERTAINTY:** relatively strong
**CLAIM OWNER:** Researchers
:::

## Lesson Summary

Today you learned to separate a claim from its source and to read the
reporting verb connecting them as a signal of strength — from
*demonstrate/show* at the strong end to *suggest/speculate* at the
cautious end. You practised telling a that-clause (a noun clause) apart
from a relative clause that also uses *that*, met passive reporting
structures that can deliberately omit a source (*it is argued that*),
and learned to recognise embedded questions introduced by *whether,
what, why, how,* and *when* as a different kind of noun clause
altogether.
