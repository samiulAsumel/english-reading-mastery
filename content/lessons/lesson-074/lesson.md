## Introduction

Lesson 73 gave you a named taxonomy of argument gaps and a test for
which hidden assumptions actually matter. This lesson closes out the
argument-analysis arc that ran through Lessons 72–74 with the question
underneath all of it: **how strong is this argument, really?** Because
evidence being true is not the same as evidence being sufficient — an
argument's evidence can be entirely accurate and still fall short of
supporting the conclusion built on top of it.

## Evidence Existing Is Not the Same as an Argument Being Strong

::: important title="The question isn't 'is there evidence' — it's 'how much, and of what kind'"
*A six-month study found that automated billing reduced processing
time by 28%. Therefore, the company should replace its entire billing
system.* Evidence exists. But ask: how big was the study? How many
teams? Was there a comparison group? Was only processing time measured?
What happened to accuracy? What's the cost? Is the long-term effect
known? Is there another explanation? **Evidence exists ≠ evidence is
sufficient.**
:::

## What Is Evidence Sufficiency?

::: concept title="Whether the evidence is enough to support the specific conclusion drawn"
*One employee reported that the new system was easier to use.* That's
an observation. *Therefore, all employees will prefer the new system*
is not adequately supported by it — one employee's report scaling up to
"all employees" is a scope mismatch, exactly the kind of leap this
course has repeatedly flagged.
:::

## Burden of Proof

::: important title="The claim-maker owes the support — and stronger claims owe more of it"
Burden of proof means: whoever makes a claim carries responsibility
for supplying adequate support for it. *"This software will reduce
operational costs by 40%"* is a specific claim — it demands specific
evidence for that 40% figure. *"The software is efficient"* alone does
not meet that burden. As a general rule, the more specific or forceful
the claim, the heavier the evidence requirement behind it.
:::

## Claim Strength Determines the Evidence Burden

::: concept title="A ladder from weak claim to very strong claim, each with a different evidence requirement"
**Weak claim**: *the system may improve efficiency* — limited evidence
can be enough. **Moderate claim**: *the system can improve efficiency
under certain conditions* — needs a mechanism plus supporting evidence.
**Strong claim**: *the system will significantly improve efficiency
across all terminals* — needs considerably more evidence. **Very
strong claim**: *the system always improves efficiency* — a universal
claim, and one of the hardest kinds of claim to actually establish.
:::

## Universal Claims Deserve Extra Scrutiny

::: warning title="One counterexample can break a universal claim entirely"
Watch for: always, never, all, none, every, completely, inevitably,
guaranteed, proves, must. *Automation always reduces costs* — a single
counterexample can challenge this. If one automated system's
maintenance cost is high enough to raise total cost, "automation always
lowers cost" is no longer valid, full stop. Universal claims are
uniquely fragile in exactly this way.
:::

## Quantifier Strength, Revisited

::: note title="Connecting back to Lessons 65, 66, and 69"
Keep this ordering active while reading: all > most > many > some, and
always > usually > often > sometimes > rarely. *All ports use
automation* is the strongest claim in that family; *some ports use
automation* is considerably weaker. Preserve the author's exact
quantifier rather than rounding it up or down in your own summary.
:::

## Five Dimensions of Evidence Quality

::: framework title="Ask all five questions of any piece of evidence"
- Relevance — is the evidence directly related to the claim?
- Reliability — is the evidence trustworthy?
- Sufficiency — is there enough of it?
- Representativeness — does the sample represent the broader population?
- Consistency — does other evidence show the same pattern?
:::

## Relevance Is Not Sufficiency

::: important title="Evidence can be genuinely on-topic and still not be enough"
*Three experienced billing officers tested a new system and liked it.*
That's relevant. Is it sufficient? Probably not — management, new
employees, IT staff, and finance staff may all have quite different
experiences with the same system. **Relevant evidence can still be
insufficient evidence.**
:::

## Reliability

::: concept title="Objective-looking data still needs evaluation"
Compare *"employees said the system was faster"* with *"system logs
showed that average processing time decreased by 28%."* The second is
closer to an objective measurement — but it isn't automatically perfect
either. Ask about the measurement method, the sample period, missing
data, system changes during the period, and workload differences.
Objective-looking data still requires evaluation, not automatic trust.
:::

## Representativeness

::: concept title="Connecting back to Lesson 73's generalization gap"
*A new system was tested in three large terminals. Therefore, it will
work equally well in every small terminal.* Large and small terminals
can differ in workload, staffing, infrastructure, procedures, and cargo
patterns. Sample ≠ population, unless the evidence actually supports
that generalization.
:::

## Sample Size Is Not the Same as Representativeness

::: warning title="A big number is not automatically a good sample"
*10,000 randomly selected users reported similar results* can be a
genuinely strong sample. *100,000 users from a highly biased sample
reported similar results* can be misleading despite being ten times
larger. Large sample ≠ automatically representative sample — size and
representativeness are two separate qualities, and a huge biased sample
is still a biased sample.
:::

## Corroboration and Triangulation

::: important title="Independent sources pointing the same direction genuinely strengthen a claim"
If system logs show fewer errors, audit records show fewer billing
corrections, user reports show fewer complaints, and an independent
review finds a similar improvement, these different, independent
sources **corroborate** each other. This is broadly called
**triangulation** — examining the same phenomenon through several
different kinds of evidence at once. Triangulated evidence is
genuinely stronger than any single source alone, precisely because
each source's individual weaknesses are unlikely to all point the same
false direction by coincidence.
:::

## Conflicting Evidence

::: important title="Advanced reading means actively looking for evidence against, not just evidence for"
*Automated billing reduced processing time by 28%. But maintenance
costs increased by 35%.* One metric improved; another worsened. Overall
evaluation is no longer simple — a genuine trade-off has appeared. A
careful reader doesn't stop at the first supporting fact; they actively
check whether the passage (or their own knowledge) contains evidence
pulling in the opposite direction.
:::

## Evidence Weight: A Rough Hierarchy

::: framework title="From weakest to strongest — a useful default, not an absolute law"
- Anecdote
- Personal opinion
- Single case
- Small, uncontrolled observation
- Observational study
- Comparative study
- Controlled experiment
- Replicated findings
- Converging independent evidence
:::

::: note title="Context can still shift this ordering"
This hierarchy is a strong default, not an absolute rule — the actual
quality of evidence within any one category can vary enormously
depending on how it was gathered.
:::

## "Proves" Versus "Supports": An Evidentiary Verb Ladder

::: important title="Connecting back to Lessons 66, 69, and 71 — now arranged as one explicit scale"
**Supports** — the evidence supports the claim; it points in the
claim's favour. **Strongly supports** — more convincing than plain
support. **Consistent with** — compatible with the claim, though other
explanations may fit equally well. **Suggests** — points toward a
possible interpretation, with low asserted certainty. **Demonstrates**
— stronger language, closer to established. **Proves** — an extremely
strong claim, especially in empirical or real-world contexts, and one
that deserves real scrutiny rather than automatic acceptance.
:::

## "Consistent With"

::: note title="Compatible with a claim is not the same as proof of it"
*The findings are consistent with the hypothesis that automation
improves efficiency* means the findings fit the hypothesis — it does
**not** mean the findings prove the hypothesis. The same findings could
just as easily be consistent with a different explanation entirely.
:::

## "No Evidence" Versus "Evidence Against"

::: important title="Two genuinely different epistemic positions, easy to conflate"
*There is no evidence that the system improves accuracy* means no
supporting evidence has been found so far — an absence. *There is
evidence that the system reduces accuracy* means evidence actively
points against the claim — a presence, pulling the other way. No
evidence ≠ evidence against; collapsing the two turns "we haven't found
support yet" into a much stronger, unearned negative claim.
:::

## "Insufficient Evidence," Revisited

::: note title="Connecting back to Lesson 73"
*The evidence is insufficient to establish the claim* means the claim
hasn't been shown false — only that current evidence doesn't yet
establish it. *A three-month pilot suggests that the system may reduce
costs, but the evidence is insufficient to establish its long-term
cost-effectiveness* is precise, careful academic English, worth
imitating in your own reading notes.
:::

## Conclusion Strength Should Not Exceed Evidence Strength

::: important title="A rule worth applying to every argument you read from now on"
If the evidence says *"a pilot suggests…,"* the conclusion should read
*"the system may be beneficial"* — not *"the system will definitely be
beneficial everywhere."* Whenever a conclusion's certainty outruns its
evidence's actual strength, that gap is exactly what a critical reader
should flag.
:::

## Argument Calibration

::: important title="Matching claim strength to evidence strength, in either direction"
**Calibration** asks whether an author's claim is properly matched to
their evidence's actual strength. **Good calibration**: limited
evidence → a cautious conclusion. **Poor calibration**: limited
evidence → an extremely strong conclusion. *Two employees preferred the
new interface. Therefore, the interface is universally superior* is
badly calibrated — a tiny, non-representative sample supporting a
sweeping universal claim.
:::

## A Full Worked Example

::: example title="Evidence, supporting factors, limitations, and a carefully calibrated final claim"
**A six-month pilot of an automated billing system across four
terminals reduced average processing time by 28%. System logs also
showed a moderate decline in billing corrections. However, the pilot
did not measure long-term maintenance costs, and the participating
terminals had relatively standardized workflows. These findings
therefore provide encouraging evidence that automation can improve
billing efficiency, but they do not establish that the system will be
cost-effective or equally effective across all terminals.**

Evidence: six-month pilot, four terminals, processing time down 28%,
billing corrections down. Supporting factors: system logs, multiple
terminals. Limitations: long-term maintenance unmeasured, standardized
workflows. What the evidence supports: automation can improve billing
efficiency. What it does **not** establish: cost-effectiveness, or
equal effectiveness everywhere. Author stance: cautiously positive.
Calibration: excellent — the conclusion is deliberately kept inside the
evidence's actual boundary.
:::

## "What Does the Evidence NOT Establish?"

::: important title="A new, powerful question to hold alongside 'what does it establish?'"
Evidence: *processing time decreased.* It may establish: faster
processing occurred during the pilot. It may **not** establish: lower
total cost, higher accuracy, long-term reliability, employee
satisfaction, universal effectiveness, or causation without a properly
controlled design. Asking this second question explicitly is what
turns a competent reading into a genuinely sophisticated one.
:::

## The Evidence Boundary

::: concept title="Every piece of evidence has an edge — name it before generalising past it"
Given: 4 teams, 6 months, automated billing, processing time. Evidence
boundary: 4 teams + 6 months + processing time. Anything beyond that
boundary — all teams, 5 years, total cost — requires additional
support before a reader should accept it. Naming the boundary
explicitly is a concrete way of catching the generalization and time
gaps from Lesson 73 the moment they appear.
:::

## A Master Test for Argument Strength

::: framework title="Ten steps for rating how strong any argument actually is"
- What is the conclusion?
- What evidence supports it?
- How strong is the evidence?
- Is the evidence relevant?
- Is it sufficient?
- Is the sample representative?
- Are there alternative explanations?
- Is there conflicting evidence?
- What assumptions are required?
- Does the conclusion exceed the evidence?
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read this passage once, straight through. Then rate its evidence
against this lesson's five quality dimensions, identify any conflicting
evidence, and state its evidence boundary, before checking your reading
against the notes that follow.

> A logistics company tested a new route-optimization algorithm on its
> 12 busiest delivery routes over four months. Fuel costs on those
> routes fell by 14%, and drivers reported the new routes felt more
> efficient. However, average delivery times on three of the twelve
> routes increased slightly, which the company attributed to
> unrelated road construction. Independent GPS tracking data confirmed
> the fuel savings figure, closely matching the company's internal
> reports. Based on these results, the company announced plans to apply
> the algorithm to its entire national delivery network of over 900
> routes.

Relevance: fuel-cost and delivery-time data are directly relevant to a
route-optimization claim. Reliability: independent GPS tracking
corroborating the company's own reported figures is a genuine strength
— a small triangulation. Representativeness: the 12 busiest routes are
very unlikely to represent the full diversity of a 900-route national
network — a clear representativeness gap. Sufficiency: four months on
12 routes is a real but narrow evidence base for a claim covering
900 routes nationwide. Conflicting evidence: delivery times worsened on
three routes — attributed to construction, but not independently
verified as unrelated, which is itself worth flagging. Evidence
boundary: 12 busiest routes, 4 months, fuel cost and (mostly) delivery
time. The rollout decision to all 900 routes reaches well past that
boundary — a calibration problem the passage's own evidence doesn't yet
support.

## Golden Rule

::: golden
The strength of a conclusion must be proportional to the strength, relevance, reliability, and scope of the evidence supporting it — so never ask only "is there evidence?"; ask "is this evidence enough for the conclusion the author wants me to accept?"
:::

## Lesson Summary

This lesson closed out the three-lesson argument-analysis arc by moving
from finding gaps and assumptions to judging overall argument strength.
You learned that evidence existing is not the same as evidence being
sufficient, and that burden of proof scales with how strong or
universal a claim actually is. You applied five evidence-quality
dimensions — relevance, reliability, representativeness, sufficiency,
consistency — and learned that relevant evidence can still be
insufficient, that large samples aren't automatically representative,
and that triangulated, independently converging evidence is genuinely
stronger than any single source. You practised actively hunting for
conflicting evidence rather than stopping at the first supporting fact,
ranked evidentiary language from "consistent with" to "proves," and
separated "no evidence for" from "evidence against." You closed on
calibration — checking whether a conclusion's certainty actually
matches its evidence's real strength and boundary — and on the
question that ties the whole arc together: what does this evidence NOT
establish?

::: important title="What Lessons 72 through 74 have built together"
Lesson 72 gave you the anatomy of a single argument. Lesson 73 gave you
a taxonomy for naming exactly where an argument's bridge is missing.
This lesson gave you a way to weigh how much that missing bridge — or
present, well-supported one — actually matters. Together, they replace
a vague sense that "this argument seems okay" or "something feels off"
with a precise, defensible verdict: what the evidence establishes, what
it does not, and whether the author's conclusion has stayed properly
inside that boundary.
:::

## Practice: Test What You've Learned

Work through every question yourself before checking anything.

For every argument below, name which of the five evidence-quality
dimensions is most in question, and state explicitly what the evidence
does and does not establish.

### Part A — Sufficiency and Burden of Proof

1. **A single customer review states a product "changed my life." A retailer features it as proof the product works for everyone.** Explain why this evidence fails the sufficiency test.
2. **A startup claims its app "will increase user engagement by 300%."** Explain why this specific claim carries a heavier burden of proof than a vaguer claim like "the app may help engagement."
3. **Explain, in your own words, why a universal claim (always, never, all) is especially vulnerable to a single counterexample.**

### Part B — The Five Evidence-Quality Dimensions

4. **A company surveys only its most loyal customers and concludes overall customer satisfaction is high.** Identify which evidence-quality dimension is weakest here.
5. **A lab result is confirmed by three independent research teams using different methods.** Identify what this lesson calls this pattern, and explain why it strengthens the claim.
6. **A dataset has 50,000 entries, but all of them come from a single region during one unusual month.** Explain why sample size alone does not resolve the problem here.

### Part C — Evidentiary Language

7. **Rank these three claims from weakest to strongest: "the data are consistent with the theory," "the data support the theory," "the data prove the theory."**
8. **A report states there is "no evidence that the additive is harmful."** Explain why this is a different claim from "there is evidence that the additive is safe."
9. **Explain why "the findings are consistent with X" does not rule out other explanations for the same findings.**

### Part D — Conflicting Evidence and Calibration

10. **A new hiring process reduced time-to-hire by 40%, but exit interviews from new hires show declining satisfaction with the process's transparency.** Identify the conflicting evidence, and explain why it matters for an overall evaluation.
11. **A pilot study of 30 patients shows promising results. A press release states the treatment "is proven effective."** Explain the calibration problem in this scenario.
12. **Explain why "the evidence is insufficient to establish the claim" is a more defensible statement than "the claim is false," using this lesson's terms.**
