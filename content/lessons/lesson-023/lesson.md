## Introduction

Lesson 20 established that a paragraph is one argument, not a pile of
sentences. Today's question follows naturally: *how does the author
keep that argument feeling like one continuous thread, sentence after
sentence?* A skilled writer almost never repeats the same noun five
times in a row. Instead, they reach for pronouns, *this/that/these/
those*, *such*, synonyms, nominalizations, and a whole network of
related vocabulary — constantly renaming the same handful of ideas so
the paragraph never feels repetitive, yet never loses the thread either.
This whole system of connection is called **cohesion**, and learning to
trace it is one of the fastest ways to read a dense paragraph well.

## What Is Cohesion?

::: concept title="Linguistic links holding a text's parts together"
**Cohesion** = the way a text's different parts stay connected through
grammar and vocabulary, rather than through logic alone.

**The company introduced a new software system. The system reduced
processing time. It also improved data management. This improvement
allowed employees to complete tasks more quickly.**

Follow one thread through all four sentences: *software system → the
system → it → this improvement.* Every link is related to the one
before it, which is exactly why the paragraph doesn't feel broken into
four disconnected statements.
:::

## Cohesion ≠ Coherence

::: important title="Grammatical connection and logical connection are two different things"
**Cohesion** is grammar and vocabulary connecting sentences on the
surface. **Coherence** is the ideas themselves making logical sense
together.

**John bought a computer. The computer is expensive. Expensive products
require careful consideration.**

This has real cohesion — *computer → computer → expensive products* —
but the paragraph's overall logical purpose isn't very clear. A text
can be cohesive on the surface without being especially coherent
underneath, which is exactly why both checks matter separately.
:::

## Reference Chains

::: concept title="One idea, renamed across several sentences"
A **reference chain** is a single noun or idea, tracked as it gets
renamed by different expressions across a paragraph.

**The company introduced a new system. The system was expensive. It
required additional training. This investment created short-term
difficulties.**

Chain: *new system → the system → it → this investment.* Four different
expressions, one continuous referent.
:::

## Pronoun Reference at Paragraph Level

::: example title="Lesson 10's skill, now working across sentences"
**The company introduced a new platform. It was expensive.** — *it* =
*platform*, unambiguous.

**The company introduced a new platform, and it required extensive
training.** — the surrounding context still points *it* back to
*platform*, even inside a longer, busier sentence. The method from
Lesson 10 hasn't changed; you're simply applying it across a whole
paragraph instead of a single sentence now.
:::

## "This" Refers to a Whole Idea, Not Just a Noun

::: important title="This can package an entire previous event, not one word"
**The company reduced its operating costs by 20%. This allowed it to
invest in new technology.**

*This* doesn't refer only to *"20%."* It refers to the **entire previous
event**: *the company reduced operating costs by 20%.* Treating *this*
as if it only pointed to the nearest noun is one of the most common
reference mistakes a reader can make — always check whether it's
packaging a whole idea instead.
:::

::: example title="That works the same way"
**The system failed repeatedly. That created serious problems for
users.**

*That* → the previous event/situation (*the system's repeated
failure*), not a single noun.
:::

## "These" and "Those" for Plural Reference

::: example title="Packaging a whole list, not just one item"
**Several problems were identified. These were addressed in a later
update.**

*These* → *several problems* — the entire list mentioned just before it.
:::

## "Such" Points Back to a Category

::: concept title="Such + noun = 'this type of' or 'the kind just mentioned'"
**The company faced several financial difficulties. Such problems
affected its expansion plans.**

*Such problems* = *this type of problem*, or *the previously mentioned
problems.*

**Poor communication can create misunderstandings. Such problems are
common in large organizations.**

Here *such problems* = *this kind of communication-related problem* —
*such* is naming a whole category, not one specific instance.
:::

## "This" + Noun: A Common Academic Pattern

::: example title="A brand-new noun, standing in for the whole previous sentence"
**The study found a significant relationship between stress and sleep.
This finding suggests that…**

*This finding* = the previous study result. This pattern is extremely
productive in academic writing — *this result, this evidence, this
observation, this pattern, this approach* can all put a fresh label on
whatever information came just before, without repeating a single word
of it.
:::

## Nominalization Creates Cohesion

::: concept title="A verb becomes a noun, then that noun becomes the next sentence's subject"
**Researchers analyzed the data carefully. This analysis revealed
several patterns.**

First sentence's verb: *analyzed.* Second sentence's subject:
*analysis.* Lesson 18's nominalization is doing double duty here — the
author turns an earlier action into a noun, then uses that noun as the
hook for the next sentence.

**The company implemented a new policy. The implementation initially
caused confusion.**

Chain: *implemented → implementation* — one of the most common forms
lexical cohesion takes in reports and news writing.
:::

::: example title="Verb, then noun, then pronoun — three links in one chain"
**Researchers examined the relationship between stress and sleep. This
examination revealed several limitations.**

Chain: *examined → examination → this examination.* The author converts
an action into a noun, then refers back to that noun with a
determiner — two cohesion techniques stacked in a row.
:::

## Synonyms Carry the Same Referent

::: example title="Different words, same entity — read contextually, not word by word"
**The company introduced a new system. The organization expects the
system to improve efficiency. The business plans to expand its use.**

*Company → organization → business* — all pointing at the same
referent, even though they aren't identical dictionary synonyms.
:::

::: warning title="A near-synonym is not always interchangeable"
*Company, organization,* and *business* are close enough to track as
the same referent inside one paragraph, but they are not exact synonyms
in every context — a legal document, for instance, might treat them
quite differently. Track the reference relationship the paragraph is
actually building, not just a dictionary definition.
:::

## Lexical Chains

::: concept title="A family of related words, not one word repeated"
A **lexical chain** is a sequence of words related to the same topic,
running through a paragraph without ever needing to reuse a single word.

**Education requires effective teaching. Good teachers help students
develop important skills. These learners may improve their knowledge
through regular practice.**

Chain: *education → teaching → teachers → students → learners → skills
→ knowledge → practice* — every word belongs to the same conceptual
field, even though none of them repeat.
:::

::: important title="Read the field, not just the individual words"
If you look at *education, teaching, students, learning* as separate
vocabulary items, they're just four unrelated words to memorize. An
advanced reader instead recognizes them as one lexical network —
and once you see the network, the paragraph's topic jumps out almost
immediately.
:::

::: example title="Two more lexical chains, spotted in one pass"
**Technology:** *Digital technology has changed modern communication.
Online platforms allow users to exchange messages, documents, and data
rapidly. These systems have also changed workplace collaboration.*
Chain: *technology → communication → platforms → messages → documents →
data → systems → collaboration.* Central topic: *digital communication
technology.*

**Business:** *The company faced declining sales. Lower revenue reduced
its ability to make new investments. As a result, management postponed
several projects and reduced operating costs.* Chain: *company → sales
→ revenue → investments → management → projects → operating costs.*
Central topic: *the company's financial difficulties.*
:::

## Reference Chain and Lexical Chain Working Together

::: example title="Two chains running through the same short paragraph"
**The company introduced a new billing system. The platform automated
several routine processes. This change reduced manual work and improved
processing speed. These improvements allowed employees to focus on more
complex tasks.**

Reference chain: *billing system → platform → this change → these
improvements* — one entity, renamed four times.

Lexical chain: *billing → automated → processes → manual work →
processing speed → employees → tasks* — the surrounding topic
vocabulary.

Once you can see both chains at once, the paragraph's information flow
becomes visible almost instantly.
:::

## "It" vs. "This"

::: important title="A specific entity, or the whole surrounding event"
**It** usually points to one specific noun or entity: *The system was
updated. It now works faster.* → *it = the system.*

**This** more often points to a whole previous event or idea: *The
system was updated. This improved performance.* → *this = the update
itself*, not just *"the system."*

Mixing these up — reading *this* as if it only meant the nearest noun —
is a small habit worth correcting early, because academic and business
writing lean on *this* constantly to summarize a whole preceding clause.
:::

## "This" + Abstract Noun in Academic Writing

::: concept title="A small, recurring family of academic labels"
**This discovery** — *The researchers found several unexpected
patterns. This discovery changed their interpretation of the results.*

**These findings** — *The researchers found that stress affected
memory. These findings suggest that…* — perhaps the single most common
phrase in academic reporting.

**These results** — *The experiment produced unexpected results. These
results require further investigation.*

**This evidence** — *The study found no significant relationship. This
evidence challenges the earlier assumption.*

**This pattern** — *Similar behavior was observed across several
groups. This pattern suggests a common underlying factor.*

**This approach** — *The researchers focused on qualitative interviews
rather than numerical data. This approach provided detailed information
about participants' experiences.*

All six phrases do the same job: take a whole previous sentence's
content and give it a fresh, compact label to build the next sentence
around.
:::

## The Former and the Latter

::: example title="Naming the first-mentioned and second-mentioned of two items"
**Traditional systems and digital platforms have different strengths.
The former are often easier to understand, while the latter can process
information more efficiently.**

*The former* → *traditional systems* (mentioned first). *The latter* →
*digital platforms* (mentioned second).
:::

::: warning title="Former/latter only works cleanly for two nearby items"
*Former* and *latter* refer back to exactly **two** previously
mentioned entities, and they work best when those two entities were
just mentioned. Reach for them across a longer list, or several
sentences later, and a reader has to scroll back to figure out which is
which — check for exactly two candidates in the immediately preceding
sentence before trusting either word.
:::

::: note title="Respectively pairs up a list, item by item"
**The first and second systems require 4 GB and 8 GB of memory,
respectively.**

Mapping: *first → 4 GB*, *second → 8 GB.* *Respectively* signals a
one-to-one pairing between two parallel lists — reading it correctly
means matching the items up in order, not at random.
:::

## Repetition Is Not Always Bad

::: important title="Key technical terms are often repeated on purpose"
**Machine learning models require training data. The quality of the
training data affects model performance. Poor training data can produce
unreliable predictions.**

*Training data* repeats three times here — deliberately, because it's
the paragraph's key technical concept, and swapping in a synonym each
time would only blur precision that the writer actually wants. *"Good
writing never repeats a word"* is not a reliable rule; precise technical
terms are a clear exception.
:::

::: example title="Repetition and variation, side by side in one paragraph"
**The new system was introduced in January. The platform replaced
several older tools. This technology improved data processing.
However, the system still required additional configuration.**

*System → platform → technology → system* — three different labels and
one repeated one, all referring to the same central entity.
:::

## "One/Ones", "Another", "The Other", "Others"

::: concept title="Small substitute words that stand in for a whole noun"
**One / ones** — *Several methods were considered. The researchers
selected the most effective one.* (*one* = *method*.) Plural: *The
older systems were replaced with newer ones.* (*ones* = *systems*.)

**Another** — *The first method was relatively simple. Another required
more resources.* (*another* = *another method* — an additional or
different one.)

**The other** — with exactly two items: *Two methods were considered.
One was expensive; the other was difficult to implement.* (*one* = the
first; *the other* = the second.)

**Others** — pointing at the rest of a group: *Some employees preferred
the new system, while others preferred the old one.* (*others* = *other
employees*.)
:::

## Reference Can Point Forward, Too

::: important title="Cataphoric reference: the explanation arrives after the pointer"
Reference is usually **backward** — *The company introduced a system.
It was expensive* points back to something already mentioned. But it
can occasionally point **forward**:

**This problem became clear later: the system could not handle large
datasets.**

Here *this problem* arrives before its own explanation. When a reader
meets a *this/these* phrase with nothing obvious behind it to explain,
the right move is to keep reading forward — the explanation is often
sitting just past a colon or in the very next clause.

**This was the main problem: employees had not received adequate
training.** Same pattern — *this* comes first, and the explanation
follows the colon. Reference doesn't always live in the previous
sentence; sometimes it's still ahead of you.
:::

## Cohesion Through Contrast, Cause, Time, and Addition

Cohesion isn't only about tracking the *same* idea — a paragraph also
stays connected through the *relationships* between its ideas.

::: example title="Four relationship types that hold a paragraph together"
**Contrast:** *Traditional systems are relatively slow. However, modern
platforms can process data rapidly.* — *traditional systems* vs.
*modern platforms* creates a lexical contrast.

**Cause:** *The company reduced staff. As a result, processing times
increased.* — *reduced staff → as a result → processing increased.*

**Time:** *The company introduced the system in January. Initially,
users experienced difficulties. Later, several updates were released.
Eventually, performance improved.* — *January → initially → later →
eventually* builds the paragraph's timeline.

**Addition:** *The system reduced costs. In addition, it improved
processing speed. Moreover, it provided better reporting capabilities.*
— benefit, then a second benefit, then a stronger, reinforcing third
benefit.
:::

## Cohesion Through Restatement

::: example title="Saying the same thing again, more plainly"
**The system is highly efficient. In other words, it can process large
volumes of data with relatively few resources.**

*Efficient* gets restated, in plainer terms, as *processing large
volumes of data with relatively few resources* — the same concept,
given a second, more concrete form.
:::

## A Six-Step Method for Finding a Lexical Chain

::: framework title="Reading a paragraph for its lexical chain"
- Mark the repeated important nouns
- Look for synonyms and related words
- Identify the pronouns
- Identify every this/that/these/those + noun
- Look for nominalizations (verb → noun)
- Group everything you found by topic
:::

## Worked Example: The Remote Work Paragraph

::: example title="Finding the chain in a shorter passage"
**Remote work has become increasingly common. Many employees now
perform their jobs from home. This arrangement can provide greater
flexibility, but it may also reduce face-to-face interaction. Some
workers report higher satisfaction, whereas others experience
difficulties maintaining communication with colleagues.**

Lexical chain: *remote work → employees → jobs → arrangement →
interaction → workers → communication → colleagues.* Central topic:
*remote work and its effects.*

Notice *this arrangement* — not an exact noun repetition, but a fresh
label for *remote work from home.* And notice *others* near the end —
it means *other workers*, pulling a contrast out of the group
introduced by *some workers* just before it.
:::

## Full Paragraph Analysis: AI in Logistics

::: example title="Reference chain and lexical chain, mapped side by side"
**Artificial intelligence is increasingly being used in logistics
operations. AI systems can analyze large amounts of data, predict
demand, and identify potential disruptions. These capabilities may help
companies improve planning and reduce operational delays. However, the
use of such systems also creates new challenges. Employees may need
additional training, and organizations must establish procedures for
evaluating AI-generated recommendations. These requirements mean that
successful AI adoption involves more than simply purchasing new
software. Instead, companies must also develop the skills and processes
necessary to use the technology effectively.**
:::

**Reference chain:** *artificial intelligence → AI systems → these
capabilities → such systems → these requirements → AI adoption → new
software → the technology* — one broad topic, renamed across the whole
paragraph.

**Lexical chain, grouped by subtopic:**

- *Technology:* AI, systems, software, technology
- *Logistics:* logistics, demand, disruptions, planning, operational
  delays
- *Organization:* companies, employees, organizations
- *Implementation:* training, procedures, recommendations, adoption,
  skills, processes

**Reading the pointers precisely:** *these capabilities* packages the
whole previous list — *analyze data, predict demand, identify
disruptions* — into one phrase. *Such systems* avoids repeating *AI
systems* outright. *These requirements* points back to the two items
just named: *training* and *procedures.* And *instead*, in the final
sentence, corrects an idea the paragraph is actively rejecting —
*simply purchasing software is not enough* — and replaces it with what
actually is needed: *skills and processes.*

**Central argument:** *AI can benefit logistics operations, but
successful adoption requires more than software; organizations also
need trained employees, appropriate procedures, skills, and processes.*
Read only the vocabulary — *AI, logistics, data, demand, training* —
and you'd get a topic list. Read the cohesion, and the whole argument
comes with it.

## Three Reading Rules Worth Automating

::: important title="This/these — ask exactly which earlier idea is being labelled"
**The system failed repeatedly. This problem…** → *this problem* = the
repeated failure, not just *"the system."*

**Employees lacked training and resources. These limitations…** →
*these limitations* = the lack of training **and** the lack of
resources — both items in the previous sentence, packaged together.
:::

::: note title="Such — expect a category, not a single item"
*Such + noun* almost always means *"this type of"* or *"the kind just
described."* Look one sentence back for the category being named, not
for one exact noun to match.
:::

::: note title="Nominalization — expect the next sentence to pick the noun back up"
*Implemented → implementation. Evaluated → evaluation. Improved →
improvement.* Whenever a sentence turns a verb into a noun, expect the
following sentence to reuse that noun — often behind *this* — to keep
building on the same action.
:::

## Cohesion's Master Map

| Relationship | Example |
|---|---|
| Reference | it, this, these, such |
| Repetition | system → system |
| Synonym | company → organization |
| Nominalization | analyze → analysis |
| Lexical association | teacher → student → classroom |
| Contrast | traditional → modern |
| Cause | problem → consequence |
| Time | initially → later → eventually |
| Addition | furthermore → moreover |

Any one of these relationships, on its own, is a small grammatical
detail. All of them working together are what make a paragraph feel
like one continuous argument instead of a list of unrelated facts.

## Three Layers, Read at Once

::: concept title="Grammar, logic, and cohesion, all in the same pass"
From now on, read every paragraph on three layers simultaneously.

**Layer 1 — Grammar:** how is each sentence built?

**Layer 2 — Logic:** how do the ideas relate — claim, reason, contrast,
cause?

**Layer 3 — Cohesion:** how does one idea carry forward into the next
sentence?

Reading all three layers together is what turns comprehension from
shallow to genuinely deep.
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read this passage once for its overall meaning, then go back and trace
two separate chains through it: one tracking a single entity as it gets
renamed sentence by sentence, and one tracking the surrounding topic
vocabulary.

> The port authority introduced a new container-tracking platform last
> year. The system allowed shippers and customs officials to monitor
> cargo location in real time. This visibility reduced document-
> processing delays and improved coordination between terminal
> operators and inland transport providers. Such improvements were
> especially valuable during peak shipping seasons, when congestion at
> the gates had previously caused lengthy vessel queues. However,
> smaller freight forwarders reported difficulty adapting to the new
> platform, and these difficulties required additional staff training
> before the benefits became fully apparent.

**Reference chain:** *container-tracking platform → the system → this
visibility → such improvements → the new platform → these
difficulties* — one central entity, renamed six times without a single
exact repetition.

**Lexical chain:** *port authority, container, shippers, customs
officials, cargo, terminal operators, inland transport providers,
congestion, gates, vessel queues, freight forwarders* — the surrounding
port-operations vocabulary that keeps the topic visible throughout.

Before moving on, work out for yourself exactly what *this visibility*,
*such improvements*, and *these difficulties* each package — one is a
single previous noun, and two are packaging a whole preceding idea.
That distinction is the core reading skill this lesson has been
building.

## Golden Rule

::: golden
A word never stands alone in a paragraph — always ask not just "what does this mean?" but "which earlier idea is this connected to?"
:::

## Lesson Summary

Today's lesson moved past reading one sentence at a time and into
tracking how an author keeps an entire paragraph feeling like one
continuous thought. You practised telling cohesion (the grammar and
vocabulary links) apart from coherence (the logic underneath them);
following reference chains as a single entity gets renamed by pronouns,
*this/these* phrases, synonyms, and nominalizations; recognizing lexical
chains as a whole family of related vocabulary rather than one repeated
word; and reading *this, these,* and *such* by asking exactly which
earlier idea each one is packaging. Put reference chains and lexical
chains together, and a paragraph's central argument becomes visible
almost immediately — often faster than reading every sentence in
isolation ever could.

::: important title="The question that replaces 'what does this word mean?'"
From today onward, every time you meet a pronoun, a *this/these* phrase,
or a *such* phrase, ask: *which earlier idea is this standing in for?*
That single habit is what separates reading vocabulary from reading a
paragraph's actual argument.
:::
