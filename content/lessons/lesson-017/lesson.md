## Introduction

Today's goal is to read past a sentence's plain factual content and
notice how certain, uncertain, cautious, or strong the author actually
is. This matters enormously in academic books, psychology, science,
history, business, research papers, and non-fiction generally.

## The Same Idea, Different Certainty

::: example title="One small word can completely change a claim's strength"
**Stress causes poor decisions.** — a strong claim: stress *definitely*
causes poor decisions.

**Stress may cause poor decisions.** — a possibility: stress *can*
cause poor decisions.

**Stress may contribute to poor decisions.** — even more cautious:
stress *may play a role* in poor decisions.

One small word — *may* — has completely changed the claim's strength.
:::

## What Is a Modal Verb?

Common modals: *can, could, may, might, must, should, will, would.*
They sit directly before a main verb.

> People can change. · Stress may affect memory. · Researchers could be
> wrong. · The evidence might support the theory. · This result must be
> interpreted carefully.

## "May" — Possibility

::: example title="The most common academic modal"
**Stress may affect decision-making.**

The author is not claiming *stress definitely affects
decision-making* — only that it's possible.
:::

## "Might" — A More Uncertain Possibility

::: example title="A slightly more tentative possibility"
**Stress might affect decision-making.**

Roughly: *may* → possibility. *might* → a somewhat more tentative
possibility. In real English, though, the difference between *may* and
*might* isn't always sharp — context matters more than the word choice
alone.
:::

## "Could" — Possibility / Potential

::: example title="Possible, or potentially able to"
**Stress could affect decision-making.** — *could* = possible/potential.

**This method could improve reading comprehension.** — a future
potential.
:::

## "Can" — General Possibility / Ability

::: example title="A general capability, not a specific instance"
**Stress can affect decision-making.** — generally speaking, this can
happen.

Compare: **Stress may affect decision-making.** → possibility in this
particular situation. **Stress can affect decision-making.** →
generally, this kind of thing can happen.
:::

## May / Might / Could — A Rough Reading Scale

```
definitely
   ↓
probably
   ↓
likely
   ↓
may / could
   ↓
might / possibly
```

Treat this as a ladder, not a ruler: it tells you which direction a word
moves the certainty, not an exact, measurable amount.

## "Must" — Strong Necessity

::: example title="A passive + modal combination"
**The evidence must be examined carefully.**

*must + be + V3* = passive + modal. Meaning: examining the evidence
carefully is necessary.

But *must* has a second, very different job:

**He must be tired.** — here *must* signals a strong logical inference:
*he is surely tired.*
:::

## Must's Two Functions

::: important title="Obligation vs. deduction — check the context"
**A. Obligation:** Researchers must follow the procedure. — following
the procedure is required.

**B. Deduction:** The researchers must have noticed the problem. — the
author is confidently inferring that they surely noticed it.

Context tells you which function is intended.
:::

## "Should" — Advice / Expectation

::: example title="Two different jobs for the same modal"
**Advice:** Researchers should consider alternative explanations. —
researchers ought to consider them.

**Expectation:** The results should be available tomorrow. — not
advice; this means the results are expected, probably, to be available
tomorrow.
:::

## "Will" — Prediction / Strong Expectation

::: example title="A confident prediction"
**The situation will change.** — a prediction.

**These changes will affect future research.** — a relatively strong
academic prediction.
:::

## "Would" — Hypothetical / Reported Future / Expected Result

::: example title="Callback to Lesson 12's conditionals"
**If people had more information, they would make better decisions.** —
*would* = a hypothetical result.

**He said that the situation would change.** — reported speech, viewing
a future event from a past perspective.
:::

## "Likely"

::: concept title="be likely to + verb"
**The results are likely to vary.**

Meaning: there's a good chance the results will vary.

**Pattern:** be likely to + verb — *is likely to affect · are likely to
change · is likely to increase · are likely to influence.*
:::

## "Unlikely"

::: important title="unlikely does not mean impossible"
**The results are unlikely to change.** — a low probability of change.

**unlikely ≠ impossible.** This distinction matters — don't read
*unlikely* as a flat denial.
:::

## "Probably"

::: example title="A relatively high probability, but not certainty"
**The results will probably change.**

Compare: **The results will change.** (a flat prediction) vs. **The
results will probably change.** (a more cautious version of the same
prediction).
:::

## "Possibly"

::: example title="Raising uncertainty further"
**The results could possibly change.**

*possibly* adds an extra layer of uncertainty on top of *could.*
:::

## "Certainly" and "Clearly"

::: important title="These signal the author's stance, not objective proof"
**Certainly:** The evidence certainly supports the conclusion. — the
author is very confident.

**Clearly:** The results clearly demonstrate the effect. — the author
is asserting that the evidence is obvious or strong.

But in critical reading, don't assume *clearly* being written means the
evidence is objectively, undeniably clear — it's the author's stance,
not an independent guarantee.
:::

## "Apparently"

::: example title="Based on available information, but not fully confirmed"
**Apparently, the researchers were unaware of the problem.**

Meaning: based on the information available, this seems to be true. The
author is making a claim without taking on full, direct certainty.
:::

## "Arguably"

::: example title="A defensible interpretation, not the only possible one"
**The most important factor is arguably experience.**

Meaning: a reasonable case can be made that experience is the most
important factor — but the author isn't claiming this is the only
possible interpretation.
:::

## "Tends To"

::: important title="A tendency, not a universal rule"
**People tend to rely on familiar information.**

The author is not saying *people always rely on familiar information* —
only that there's a general tendency.

Compare: **People rely on familiar information.** (a general statement)
vs. **People tend to rely on familiar information.** (a tendency, not a
universal rule).
:::

## "Appears To"

::: example title="Cautious academic language"
**The method appears to be effective.**

Meaning: based on available evidence, the method seems to be effective
— cautious, not a flat assertion.
:::

## "Seems To"

::: example title="Reducing certainty explicitly"
**The results seem to indicate a relationship.**

Meaning: the results appear to indicate a relationship — the author has
deliberately lowered the certainty here.
:::

## "Is Associated With"

::: important title="A crucial phrase, especially in psychology and science — association is not causation"
**Stress is associated with poor sleep.**

Meaning: stress and poor sleep are found together, statistically. This
does **not** automatically claim: *stress causes poor sleep.*

**Association ≠ causation.** *X and Y occur together* is a much weaker
claim than *X definitely causes Y.*
:::

## "May Be Associated With"

::: example title="Two layers of caution, stacked"
**Stress may be associated with poor sleep.**

*may* → uncertainty. *be associated with* → a relationship, not
necessarily causation. Together, these make an already-cautious claim
even more cautious.
:::

## "Has Been Shown To"

::: example title="Passive reporting, source often left implicit"
**The method has been shown to improve performance.**

*has been shown* (passive) + *to improve* (infinitive). Meaning:
studies/evidence have demonstrated this — but exactly *who* showed it
often has to be worked out from context.
:::

## "Is Believed To" / "Is Thought To" / "Is Considered To Be"

::: concept title="Three interchangeable shapes for an unnamed source's view"
All three follow the same pattern — **be + reporting verb (passive) +
to + verb** — and all three let a writer report a widely-held view
without naming exactly who holds it.

**The treatment is believed to improve memory.** — roughly equivalent
to *Researchers believe that the treatment improves memory*, but as a
passive, the focus stays on *treatment*, and the believer stays
unnamed.

**The behavior is thought to reflect social learning.** — same shape:
*someone* thinks this, but the sentence never says who.

**The method is considered to be effective.** — *is considered*
(passive reporting) + *to be effective* (infinitive); again, no named
source.

Treat all three as one recognisable pattern rather than three separate
verbs to memorise.
:::

## A Master Map of Certainty Expressions

| Strength | Examples |
|---|---|
| Very strong | definitely · certainly · clearly · must |
| Strong/moderate | likely · probably · will · is expected to |
| Cautious | may · could · might · possibly · appears to · seems to · tends to |
| Very cautious / qualified | may contribute to · may be associated with · is sometimes interpreted as · could potentially · arguably |

Once again, this is a rough reading guide, not an absolute scale.

## What Is Hedging?

Connecting back to Lesson 9: you met hedging there through degree and
certainty words; here the focus shifts to the specific modal and
passive-reporting shapes that carry it.

::: concept title="Formulating a claim so it doesn't overstate certainty"
**Strong:** Exercise improves memory.
**Hedged:** Exercise may improve memory.
**More hedged:** Exercise may contribute to improvements in memory.
**Even more cautious:** Exercise may be associated with better memory
performance.
:::

## Why Academic Writers Hedge

::: example title="Because evidence is rarely simple or complete"
Scientific evidence is often limited, incomplete, context-dependent,
probabilistic, based on one sample, and open to alternative
explanations.

So writing *"X may contribute to Y"* instead of *"X always causes Y"*
is often the more scientifically responsible choice.
:::

## "Not Necessarily"

::: important title="Callback to Lesson 12 — a very subtle, very important phrase"
**More information does not necessarily lead to better decisions.**

Meaning: having more information doesn't *guarantee* better decisions.

Compare: **More information does not lead to better decisions.**
(a stronger, flat negative claim) vs. **More information does not
necessarily lead to better decisions.** (leaves room for exceptions).
:::

## "Generally"

::: example title="Weaker than 'always'"
**People generally prefer familiar options.**

Meaning: as a general pattern, people prefer familiar options — weaker
than *always.*
:::

## "Often"

::: example title="Frequent, not universal"
**People often rely on shortcuts.** — frequently.

**People always rely on shortcuts.** — universally.

These are not the same claim.
:::

## Frequency and Certainty, Stacked Together

::: example title="Multiple layers of caution in one short sentence"
**People often tend to rely on familiar information.**

*often* → frequency. *tend to* → tendency. Together, this is far from a
strong, universal claim.
:::

## Today's Mega Sentence

::: example title="Multiple levels of caution in a single academic sentence"
**The available evidence suggests that stress may contribute to poorer
decision-making, although the relationship is unlikely to be simple and
may depend on the type of decision being made.**

**Source:** the available evidence. **Reporting verb:** suggests
(cautious). **Main claim:** stress may contribute to poorer
decision-making. *may* → possibility.

**Contrast:** although. **Certainty:** is unlikely to be simple —
low probability of simplicity. **Condition/context:** may depend on… —
the relationship might be context-dependent.

The author has layered several levels of caution into one sentence.
:::

## Extracting the "Certainty Layer"

::: example title="Separating what is claimed from how confidently"
**Researchers strongly argue that the results probably reflect
differences in experience.**

*strongly* → the strength of the argument itself. *argue* → the
reporting stance. *probably* → the claim's probability. *reflect* → the
actual proposition being made.

Reading only *"results reflect differences"* would miss most of the
sentence's real information.
:::

## Separating the Author's Confidence

::: example title="Two sentences, two very different confidence levels"
**The results clearly demonstrate that the method is effective.** —
author confidence: very high.

**The results appear to suggest that the method may be effective.** —
author confidence: much lower. Two hedges stacked here: *appear to
suggest* and *may be effective.*
:::

## "Could" Is Not Always Weak Certainty

::: important title="Check which of could's several jobs is in play"
**The new system could reduce costs.** — potential.

**He could have forgotten the meeting.** — past possibility.

**When he was younger, he could run 10 kilometers.** — past ability.

A modal's function depends on context — never assume a fixed meaning
from the word alone.
:::

## Today's Reading Method

::: framework title="Label every modal or hedge you meet"
- FACT — a plain, unqualified statement
- CLAIM — an assertion, possibly attributed to a source
- POSSIBILITY — may, might, could
- UNCERTAINTY — appears to, seems to, apparently
- EXPECTATION — should, will, is expected to
- OBLIGATION — must, should (advice sense)
:::

::: example title="Applying the labels"
**The method may improve performance.** → POSSIBILITY.
**Researchers must consider alternative explanations.** → OBLIGATION.
**The results are likely to vary.** → HIGHER PROBABILITY.
**The findings appear to suggest a relationship.** → CAUTIOUS CLAIM.
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read the paragraph once, slowly, tracking every hedge and modal.

> Research suggests that people may not always make decisions in a
> fully rational manner. Individuals often rely on mental shortcuts,
> which can sometimes help them make decisions quickly but may also lead
> to errors. These shortcuts are likely to be particularly useful when
> people have limited time or information. However, they may be less
> effective in situations that require careful analysis. Some
> researchers argue that training could help people recognize situations
> in which these shortcuts are likely to produce poor outcomes, although
> it is unlikely that such training would eliminate biased
> decision-making completely.

**1** — Source: *Research.* Reporting verb: *suggests.* Claim: *people
may not always make decisions rationally.* Certainty: *suggests* +
*may* → cautious.

**2** — *often* → frequency. *can sometimes help* → possibility +
frequency. *may also lead to errors* → possibility.

**3** — *are likely to be* → relatively high probability.

**4** — *may be less effective* → possibility.

**5** — Source: *Some researchers.* Reporting verb: *argue.* Claim:
*training could help…* — *could* → potential.

**6** — *unlikely* → low probability. *would eliminate* → hypothetical
result. The author has deliberately limited the claim: training *could
help*, but is *unlikely* to *eliminate completely* — improving is not
the same as completely solving.

## Golden Rule

::: golden
Never stop at "what does this sentence say?" Always ask a second question: "how certain is the author that it's true?" Content and certainty are two separate pieces of information — read them both.
:::

**Content:** Stress may affect memory. **Certainty:** *may* → possibility,
not certainty. Both matter equally.

## Lesson Summary

Today you learned to read *may, might, could, can* as a rough
possibility scale, and *must, should, will, would* by their specific
function — obligation, deduction, advice, expectation, prediction, or
hypothetical result. You met the crucial distinction between
association and causation, practised recognising hedging phrases like
*appears to, tends to,* and *may be associated with*, and understood why
academic writers reach for caution rather than absolute claims. Most
importantly, you practised separating a sentence's content from its
certainty — two different pieces of information carried by the same
sentence.
