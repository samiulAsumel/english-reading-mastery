## Introduction

Today's tense lesson is not about speaking English fluently — it's about
reading it accurately. Books, articles, documentation, and research
papers lean on tense constantly to signal something you need to catch:
when something happened, whether it's still going on, whether it's
finished, and why the author chose that particular form.

Your goal today: look at a verb's form and immediately know when the
action happens, how long it lasts, whether it's over, and why the writer
reached for that tense.

## What Is Tense, Really?

::: concept title="Tense signals time and state"
**Tense tells you about the time and state of an action or
situation.**

| Sentence | Basic time |
|---|---|
| People learn. | present / general |
| People learned. | past |
| People will learn. | future |

All three sentences describe the same idea — *learning* — but each one
places it at a different point in time.
:::

## The First Distinction That Matters for Reading

::: important title="Present tense does not always mean 'right now'"
**Water boils at 100°C.**

*Boils* here does not mean water is boiling at this exact moment. It
states a **general fact** — something that is always true. Present
Simple is very often used for exactly this: a general truth, not a
live, in-progress action.
:::

## Present Simple

**Structure:** Subject + base verb

> People learn. · People make decisions. · Experience matters. ·
> Research shows that behavior can change.

Present Simple typically covers four jobs:

1. **General truth** — People need social interaction.
2. **Habit** — People often read books.
3. **Repeated action** — She studies every day.
4. **General statement** — Human behavior is complex.

## Present Simple in Books

::: example title="A writer's default tense for theory and general observation"
Non-fiction authors reach for Present Simple whenever they're stating a
theory, a principle, or a general observation.

**Human behavior is influenced by many factors.**
*is* is Present Simple, because this is a general statement.

**People make decisions based on their experiences.**
Also a general observation — not something happening at this exact
second.
:::

## Present Continuous

**Structure:** am/is/are + verb-*ing*

> People are learning.

This usually signals an action in progress right now, or a temporary
process.

::: example title="Habit vs. ongoing process"
**People learn English.** → a general fact or habit.

**People are learning English.** → learning is actively happening during
this current period.
:::

## Context Still Matters When Reading a Book

::: example title="Present Continuous for a large-scale, ongoing change"
**Society is changing rapidly.**

*is changing* is Present Continuous. It describes society as being in
the middle of an ongoing process — a large-scale change actively taking
place, not a single instant.
:::

## Past Simple

**Structure:** Subject + past-tense verb

> People learned. · He studied psychology. · Researchers examined the
> data.

Past Simple describes a **completed** past event.

::: example title="A finished event in the past"
**Researchers studied the behavior of participants.**

The study happened in the past, and it's finished.
:::

## Historical Writing

::: example title="Past Simple for dated, historical facts"
History and background sections lean heavily on Past Simple.

**The researchers conducted the experiment in 1975.**
The experiment is a completed historical event.

**The company introduced the product in 2005.**
Also a completed historical event.
:::

## Present Perfect

**Structure:** have/has + past participle

> People have learned from experience. · Researchers have discovered
> several important patterns. · Society has changed significantly.

## The Core Idea of Present Perfect

::: important title="Present Perfect connects a past event to right now"
Compare directly:

**Past Simple:** Researchers studied the problem. → The study happened
in the past, and the sentence stops there.

**Present Perfect:** Researchers have studied the problem. → The study
happened in the past, *and its relevance still exists right now.*

That connection — past action, present relevance — is the entire point
of Present Perfect.
:::

## Present Perfect in Non-Fiction

::: example title="'Past research → current knowledge' is Present Perfect's signature move"
**Researchers have found that people often rely on mental shortcuts.**

The writer is saying: past research is now part of current knowledge —
hence Present Perfect.

**Scientists have discovered several factors that affect human
behavior.**

Same pattern: past research feeding directly into present-day
understanding.
:::

## Past Perfect

**Structure:** had + past participle

::: example title="The earlier of two past events"
**The researchers had completed the study before they published the
results.**

Two past events here: *the study was completed* and *the results were
published.* Whichever happened first gets Past Perfect: *had completed.*

```
Past
├── Study completed   (had completed — earlier)
└── Results published (published — later)
```

Past Perfect marks "further back" inside the past.
:::

## Future

**Basic form:** will + base verb

> People will continue to learn. · Technology will change society.

This expresses a prediction or expectation.

::: example title="Future prediction paired with a present reporting verb"
**Artificial intelligence will change many aspects of work.** — a
prediction on its own.

**Research suggests that AI will change many aspects of work.** — here,
*suggests* is a present-tense reporting verb, and *will change* is the
future prediction it introduces. This combination — present reporting
verb + future prediction — is extremely common in non-fiction.
:::

## A Reading Pattern Worth Memorising

::: note title="Reporting-verb phrases signal a noun clause is coming"
You'll meet these shapes constantly in non-fiction:

*Research shows that… · Studies suggest that… · Evidence indicates
that… · Researchers have found that…*

**Research shows that regular practice improves performance.**

Core: *Research shows…* Noun clause (the object of *shows*): *that
regular practice improves performance.*
:::

## Beyond "When": Aspect

English tense isn't only about *when* something happens — it also
signals the action's state, progress, or completion. That extra layer is
called **aspect**, and it comes in four basic patterns.

| Aspect | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Simple | People learn. | general fact or single event |
| Continuous | People are learning. | ongoing |
| Perfect | People have learned. | completed, connected to the present |
| Perfect Continuous | People have been learning. | started in the past, still ongoing |

## Perfect Continuous

**Structure:** have/has been + verb-*ing*

::: example title="Started in the past, still running now"
**She has been studying for three hours.**

She started three hours ago, and she's still studying now.

**Researchers have been studying human behavior for decades.**

The research began in the past and has continued, without stopping,
across a long stretch of time.
:::

## Comparing Four Forms of the Same Verb

::: example title="One verb, four timelines"
1. **People study.** — general fact or habit.
2. **People are studying.** — currently in progress.
3. **People have studied.** — past experience, still relevant now.
4. **People have been studying.** — an ongoing activity spanning a
   period of time.

Seeing these four side by side is one of the fastest ways to make this
distinction click.
:::

## Modals Add a Tense-Like Meaning of Their Own

> People can learn. — ability or possibility
> People could learn. — past ability, or a weaker, context-dependent possibility
> People may learn. — possibility
> People might learn. — weaker possibility
> People should learn. — recommendation or expectation
> People must learn. — necessity

## Passive Voice + Tense

Combine what you learned about passive voice in Lesson 3 with today's
tenses, and the verb phrase simply grows by one more piece — always read
the whole thing as one unit.

> **Present:** Behavior is influenced by emotions.
> **Past:** Behavior was influenced by emotions.
> **Present Perfect:** Behavior has been influenced by social factors.
> **Future:** Behavior will be influenced by the environment.

Whether it's *is influenced*, *was influenced*, *has been influenced*, or
*will be influenced* — treat the whole phrase as one verb carrying one
piece of timeline information.

## A Rule Worth Keeping for Every Book You Read

::: framework title="Five questions to ask about any verb form"
- When does this happen?
- Is it general, or is it specific to one moment?
- Is it ongoing?
- Is it completed?
- Does it connect past information to the present?
:::

Don't try to recall a Bangla grammar-term for the tense. Answer these
five questions instead — they get you straight to the meaning.

## Real Example 1

::: example title="Present Perfect opening, then a relative clause, then a general-truth verb inside a noun clause"
**Researchers have found that people who regularly practice a skill
improve their performance over time.**

**Researchers have found** — Present Perfect: past research feeding into
current knowledge.

**people who regularly practice a skill** — a noun phrase with a
relative clause: *who regularly practice a skill* describes which
people.

Inside the noun clause that follows *found*, the main idea is: **people
improve their performance over time.** Here *improve* is Present
Simple — because this is a general relationship (regular practice →
improved performance), not a single event.
:::

## Real Example 2

::: example title="A past discovery about a past, ongoing state"
**Scientists discovered that people were behaving differently under
stressful conditions.**

**discovered** — Past Simple: the discovery itself happened at a
specific point in the past.

**were behaving** — Past Continuous: at that same past time, the
behavior was already in progress, ongoing.

```
Past
├── People were behaving differently   (ongoing, at that time)
└── Scientists discovered this         (the discovery itself)
```
:::

## Tense Changes With Context, Not Just With the Verb's Root

::: example title="One verb root, six different timelines"
**People work hard.** — general statement.
**People are working hard.** — currently working.
**People worked hard.** — past.
**People have worked hard.** — past experience, still relevant.
**People had worked hard before the company closed.** — an earlier past.
**People will work hard.** — future expectation.

The whole point of learning tense is being able to look at a verb's
*form* and immediately picture its place on this timeline.
:::

## Guided Reading Practice

Read the paragraph once, slowly, without a dictionary.

> Researchers have studied human decision-making for many years. Early
> studies focused mainly on the idea that people were rational
> decision-makers. Later research showed that emotions, habits, and
> unconscious biases can influence judgment. Today, researchers continue
> to examine how these factors affect the decisions people make. Future
> studies may provide a deeper understanding of human behavior.

**Sentence 1 — Researchers have studied human decision-making for many
years.**
*have studied* → Present Perfect: decades of research, still relevant to
current knowledge.

**Sentence 2 — Early studies focused mainly on the idea that people were
rational decision-makers.**
*focused* → Past Simple: an early, historical body of research. *were* →
Past Simple: the belief people held at that time.

**Sentence 3 — Later research showed that emotions, habits, and
unconscious biases can influence judgment.**
*showed* → Past Simple: a later historical finding. *can influence* →
modal + base form: a general possibility or capability, not tied to one
moment.

**Sentence 4 — Today, researchers continue to examine how these factors
affect the decisions people make.**
*continue* → Present Simple: describing the current, ongoing state of
the field.

**Sentence 5 — Future studies may provide a deeper understanding of
human behavior.**
*may provide* → modal: a future possibility.

## Today's Core Skill, as One Timeline

```
PAST -------------------- PRESENT -------------------- FUTURE

Past Simple                Present Simple               will
  completed event             general / current            prediction

Past Perfect                Present Perfect
  earlier past                 past → present connection

Past Continuous             Present Continuous
  ongoing in the past          ongoing now
```

## A Reading Strategy: Who + Verb + Time + State

::: framework title="Four questions for reading any verb phrase"
- Who? (the subject)
- Verb phrase? (read it as one unit, whatever its length)
- Time? (past, present, or future)
- State? (general, ongoing, or completed)
:::

::: example title="Applying the four questions"
**Researchers have been studying the problem.**

Who? → *Researchers*
Verb phrase? → *have been studying*
Time? → past continuing into present
State? → ongoing

Read this way, grammar stops feeling like a separate subject bolted onto
the sentence — it becomes part of the meaning itself.
:::

::: golden
Don't just name a tense — ask when it happens, whether it's ongoing or finished, and whether it connects the past to right now. Those four answers are the meaning.
:::

## Lesson Summary

Today you learned that Present Simple often signals a general truth
rather than "right now," and that Present Perfect exists specifically to
connect a past event to present relevance — the reason non-fiction
writers switch between Past Simple and Present Perfect so often. You met
all four aspects — simple, continuous, perfect, and perfect continuous —
and practised reading a full verb phrase, active or passive, modal or
plain, as one timeline signal. From here on, every verb you meet while
reading is a small amount of free information about when, how long, and
whether something is finished — as long as you stop to read its full
form.
