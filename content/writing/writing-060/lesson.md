## Introduction

[Lesson 60](/lessons/60/) taught you that "System A is better than System
B" is an incomplete claim until you name the dimension — faster, cheaper,
more reliable — the comparison is actually being made along. Writing a
comparison well means naming that dimension yourself, up front, instead of
leaving a reader to guess what "better" is quietly standing in for.

## The Rule, for Writing

::: concept
A weak comparison states a bare judgment: "The new process is better than
the old one." A strong one names the exact dimension being compared, uses a
precise contrast connector (whereas, unlike, compared with) to hold the two
sides together clearly, and names at least one other dimension the
comparison deliberately says nothing about.
:::

::: example
Compared with the old manual check-in process, the new self-service kiosks
are faster: average check-in time fell from six minutes to ninety seconds.
This says nothing about cost, since the kiosks required a significant
upfront investment the manual process never did — speed and cost are two
separate dimensions, and this comparison only speaks to one of them.
:::

## Your Turn

::: prompt words="110-160 words" time="20 min"
Compare two things you know well (two tools, two approaches, two systems,
two versions of the same product). Name the one specific dimension you're
comparing them along, use a contrast connector to state the difference
clearly, and name one other dimension your comparison doesn't address —
resist the pull toward a vague, all-purpose "better."
:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="dimension" pos="noun" register="formal" meaning="the specific respect or criterion a comparison is being made along, such as speed, cost, or reliability (মাত্রা)" collocation="along one dimension, key dimension, comparison dimension" example="The two ports are compared along one dimension: waiting time."
:::

::: vocabulary word="whereas" pos="conjunction" register="formal" meaning="introduces a clean, two-sided contrast between two things (যেখানে/অথচ)" collocation="whereas, contrast with whereas, whereas the other" example="Port A is fast, whereas Port B is cheap."
:::

::: vocabulary word="trade-off" pos="noun" register="neutral" meaning="a situation where gaining an advantage on one dimension costs something on another (ছাড়/বিনিময়)" collocation="trade-off between, difficult trade-off, accept a trade-off" example="Speed comes at a trade-off with inspection quality."
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
Compared with the previous invoicing software, the new platform is faster
to close a monthly cycle: what used to take three days of manual
reconciliation now takes under four hours. This comparison is about speed
alone. It says nothing about ease of use, and in fact several staff members
reported the new platform's interface as harder to learn at first, requiring
a week of training the old software never needed. Whereas the old system
traded speed for familiarity, the new one trades a steeper learning curve
for a much faster monthly close — two different dimensions, each worth
weighing on its own terms rather than collapsing into one vague "better."
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Did you name the specific dimension your comparison is made along?
- Did you use a precise contrast connector (whereas, unlike, compared with) rather than a vague "better than"?
- Did you name at least one dimension your comparison deliberately doesn't address?
- Did you avoid letting "better" on one dimension imply "better overall"?
:::
