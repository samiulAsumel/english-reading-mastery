## Introduction

[Lesson 44](/lessons/44/) taught you to read a paragraph as an argument —
claim, evidence, qualification — not a list of separately meaningful
sentences. Building one yourself means making the same three moves
deliberately, in order, and then proving you can compress the whole
argument into one sentence without quietly dropping the qualification
that made it honest.

## Claim, Evidence, Qualification — for Writing

::: concept
A paragraph's **claim** states what you're arguing. Its **evidence**
supports that claim with something broader than a single story — a
study, a pattern across several cases, a measured result — not just one
illustrative anecdote. Its **qualification** admits a limit on the
claim, usually with *however* or *although*, so the paragraph doesn't
claim more than its evidence actually earns.
:::

::: example
**Claim:** *Automated invoicing can shorten payment cycles.* **Evidence:**
*A review of twelve mid-sized firms found average payment times fell by
nearly a week after adoption.* **Qualification:** *However, firms that
also renegotiated payment terms during the same period saw similar
gains, so automation alone cannot be credited with the full effect.*
:::

## Your Turn

::: prompt words="100-150 words" time="20 min"
Write one paragraph (100-150 words) about a workplace or operational
topic. State a claim, support it with evidence broader than a single
anecdote (a study, a pattern, a measured result — not just "one company
noticed..."), and close with a qualification that limits how much credit
the claim can take. Then, underneath the paragraph, compress it into one
sentence that keeps the claim's modality (may/appears to), its scope,
and its qualification intact.
:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="illustration" pos="noun" register="formal" meaning="a single concrete example used to make an idea vivid, weaker on its own than systematic evidence (দৃষ্টান্ত)" collocation="a vivid illustration, illustration of, serve as an illustration" example="The delayed shipment serves as an illustration of poor coordination."
:::

::: vocabulary word="attribute to" pos="phrasal verb" register="formal" meaning="to credit something as the cause of an outcome, often limited by a hedge like 'partly' (আরোপ করা)" collocation="attribute to, be attributed to, partly attribute to" example="The improvement was partly attributed to the new roster."
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
*Predictive maintenance software can reduce unplanned equipment
downtime. An analysis of eighteen manufacturing plants found that
facilities using such software experienced roughly 20% fewer unplanned
stoppages over two years than those relying on scheduled maintenance
alone. However, the plants that adopted the software had also recently
retrained their maintenance staff, so the reduction in downtime cannot
be attributed to the software alone.* **One-sentence compression:**
*Predictive maintenance software may reduce unplanned downtime, though
an eighteen-plant study cannot separate its effect from concurrent staff
retraining.* The compression keeps the hedge ("may"), the evidence's
scope (eighteen plants, two years), and the qualification (staff
retraining) all intact.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Does your evidence sentence describe something broader than a single anecdote?
- Does your qualification genuinely limit the claim, rather than just restating it?
- Does your one-sentence compression keep the claim's hedge word, rather than stating it as flat fact?
- Does the compression keep the qualification, rather than dropping it to save words?
:::
