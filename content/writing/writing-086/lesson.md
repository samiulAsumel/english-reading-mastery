## Introduction

[Lesson 86](/lessons/86/) taught you to combine an author's distributed
information into one integrated understanding, instead of listing what was
said, topic by topic. This task makes you build that same synthesis
yourself — from two sources that don't fully agree with each other.

## The Rule, for Writing

::: concept
Synthesis is not two summaries placed side by side. State the actual
relationship between two claims — agreement, partial agreement,
contradiction, or a condition that explains why they differ — in one
combined account, without dropping either source's hedges or conditions in
the process.
:::

::: example
Source A: a pilot found employees rated remote work highly for focus time.
Source B: a larger survey found no overall productivity gain from remote
work. Weak (two summaries stapled together): "Source A found high
satisfaction. Source B found no productivity gain." Synthesis: "Employees
report higher satisfaction with remote work, particularly around focus
time, but a larger survey found this preference does not reliably translate
into measurable productivity gains — suggesting remote work may improve
experience without necessarily improving output."
:::

## Your Turn

::: prompt words="120-150 words" time="20 min"
Source A: a six-month pilot at one warehouse found that new picking-station
signage reduced picking errors by 10%. Source B: a larger study across
twelve warehouses found no significant change in picking errors after
similar signage changes, and noted that error rates depended heavily on
staff turnover. Write one academic-register paragraph (120-150 words) that
synthesizes both sources into a single, honest account. State the
relationship between them explicitly, preserve every hedge and condition,
and do not let one source's finding silently cancel or override the
other's.
:::

::: draft placeholder="Taken together, these two studies suggest.../ However, the larger study.../ This indicates that..."
:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="taken together" pos="phrase" register="formal" meaning="a signal phrase showing that separate pieces of evidence are being combined into one conclusion (একত্রে বিবেচনা করলে)" collocation="taken together, the findings, taken together these results" example="Taken together, the two studies suggest a link."
:::

::: vocabulary word="reconcile" pos="verb" register="formal" meaning="to show how two apparently conflicting findings can both be true, without dismissing either one (মীমাংসা করা)" collocation="reconcile the findings, reconcile two claims, difficult to reconcile" example="The author reconciles the two findings by noting they cover different periods."
:::

::: vocabulary word="erase" pos="verb" register="neutral" meaning="to accidentally remove a hedge, condition, or disagreement while combining information (মুছে ফেলা)" collocation="erase a distinction, erase the hedge, accidentally erase" example="A careless summary can erase the hedge in the original claim."
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
A six-month pilot at one warehouse found that new picking-station signage
reduced errors by 10%, while a larger study across twelve warehouses found
no significant change in error rates after similar signage changes. Taken
together, these findings do not straightforwardly agree or disagree: the
larger study noted that error rates depended heavily on staff turnover,
which may explain why the pilot's single, presumably more stable site saw
an improvement that a more turnover-affected sample did not replicate. This
suggests that signage alone is unlikely to reliably reduce picking errors;
its apparent benefit in the pilot may instead have depended on relatively
stable staffing, a condition the larger study's more varied warehouses did
not consistently share. Any recommendation to adopt the signage should
therefore treat staff turnover as a probable moderating factor, not treat
the pilot's result as a universal effect.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Does your paragraph state an explicit relationship between the two sources, not just report each one in turn?
- Did you preserve both sources' hedges and conditions — the six-month, one-site limit, the twelve-warehouse null result, and the staff-turnover factor?
- Does your synthesis avoid simply declaring one source "right" and the other "wrong"?
- Is the paragraph within 120-150 words and written in an academic register?
:::
