## Introduction

[Lesson 85](/lessons/85/) gave you a three-tier scale for evidentiary
language — proves/establishes at the top, demonstrates/supports in the
middle, suggests/may at the bottom — and warned about silent
strengthening: quietly restating a cautious "suggests" as a confident
"proves." This task asks you to write a claim at the correct tier, and
then catch yourself if you drift.

## The Rule, for Writing

::: concept
Match your verb to your evidence. A single small study "suggests." A
consistent pattern across many studies "supports" or "demonstrates."
Only overwhelming, repeated, controlled evidence earns "proves" or
"establishes." Writing "proves" when your evidence only suggests is the
single most common calibration error.
:::

::: example
Evidence: one six-month pilot at one warehouse showed a 10% drop in
picking errors after new signage was installed. Correct: "The pilot
suggests better signage may reduce picking errors." Silent strengthening
(wrong): "Better signage reduces picking errors" — stated as an
established fact from a single small pilot.
:::

## Your Turn

::: prompt words="60-90 words" time="20 min"
A single six-month pilot at one warehouse found that adding barcode
scanners at each station reduced picking errors by 15%. No other site
has tried it yet. Write two sentences: (1) a correctly calibrated claim
about what this evidence supports, and (2) an example of silent
strengthening — the same claim restated too strongly — followed by one
sentence explaining exactly what makes sentence 2 an overstatement.
:::

::: draft placeholder="(1) The pilot suggests.../ (2) Silently strengthened: Barcode scanners reduce picking errors. / This overstates because..."
:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="hedge" pos="verb" register="formal" meaning="to state a claim cautiously, signaling it isn't fully certain (সতর্কভাবে বলা)" collocation="hedge a claim, hedge carefully, hedge appropriately" example="Hedge the claim so it matches the strength of the evidence."
:::

::: vocabulary word="silent strengthening" pos="phrase" register="course term" meaning="restating a cautious, hedged claim as if it were a confident, established fact (নীরবে দাবি বাড়ানো)" collocation="avoid silent strengthening, guard against silent strengthening, silent strengthening of" example="Silent strengthening occurs when 'may reduce costs' becomes 'reduces costs'."
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
(1) The pilot suggests that barcode scanners may reduce picking errors,
though this is based on a single site over six months. (2) Silently
strengthened: "Barcode scanners reduce picking errors by 15%." This
overstates because it drops "suggests" and "may," and states a
one-site, six-month result as a general, proven fact — a reader would
now expect the same 15% drop everywhere, which the evidence never
claimed.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Did your calibrated claim use a hedge word matching the strength of one small pilot?
- Did your "silently strengthened" version genuinely overstate, not just reword?
- Did you name specifically which words were dropped or changed?
:::
