## Introduction

[Lesson 30](/lessons/30/) taught you to pull apart the layers a careless
reading blurs together — data, interpretation, inference, and
conclusion. This task asks you to build a paragraph that keeps those
layers honestly separate on the page: what you observed, in one
sentence, and what you think it means, in the next.

## The Rule, for Writing

::: concept
State your observation first, in plain factual language, with no
interpretation attached. Then, in a separate sentence, say what you
think it means, using a support-language verb sized to your evidence:
"suggests" or "is consistent with" for limited evidence, "supports" for
stronger evidence, and never "proves" unless your evidence is genuinely
that conclusive. Finally, state your claim's scope honestly — don't let
a claim about "our team" quietly become a claim about "every team."
:::

::: example
**Data/observation:** Sales at the pilot branch rose twelve percent in
the month after the new layout was installed. **Interpretation:** This
is consistent with the new layout encouraging customers to browse more
of the store, though the branch also ran a seasonal promotion that same
month. **Scope:** The finding applies to this one branch during this one
month; it does not yet show that the layout would work chain-wide.
:::

## Your Turn

::: prompt words="80-110 words" time="20 min"
Write a paragraph about a real or plausible result you know something
about (a change at work, a personal habit, a small experiment). Include,
as clearly separate sentences: the observation/data itself, an
interpretation using "suggests," "is consistent with," or "supports"
(matched to how strong your evidence really is), and a final sentence
stating the claim's actual scope — who or what it does and doesn't cover.
:::

::: draft placeholder="[Data/observation happened]. / This is consistent with/suggests .../ However, this only covers [narrow scope], not [wider group]."
:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="interpretation" pos="noun" register="formal" meaning="what a writer thinks an observation means, kept separate from the observation itself (ব্যাখ্যা)" collocation="offer an interpretation, alternative interpretation, open to interpretation" example="The data are clear, but their interpretation is open to debate."
:::

::: vocabulary word="scope" pos="noun" register="formal" meaning="how far a claim is meant to apply: the group, place, or time it covers (পরিধি)" collocation="scope of, limited scope, beyond the scope" example="The scope of the claim is limited to export cargo."
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
Container dwell time at the pilot terminal fell by eighteen percent in
the six weeks after staff began using the new yard-mapping app. This is
consistent with the app helping drivers locate containers faster,
although the terminal also hired two extra yard staff during the same
period. The finding covers only this one terminal over six weeks; it
does not yet show that the app alone would produce the same drop at a
larger or busier terminal.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Are your observation and your interpretation written as two clearly separate sentences?
- Does your support-language verb (suggests, is consistent with, supports) match how strong your evidence actually is?
- Did you state your claim's real scope, rather than letting it quietly widen?
- Did you write this directly in English, without translating from Bangla first?
:::
