## Introduction

Understanding what a sentence *says* is not the same as understanding
*why the author put it there*. [Lesson 78](/lessons/78/) taught you to
read for that purpose. This task makes you write with it deliberately —
placing a sentence in a paragraph because of the job it needs to do.

## Sentences Have Jobs

::: concept
A sentence can support a claim, provide evidence, concede a limitation,
respond to a possible objection, or transition to a new point — its
grammatical content and its rhetorical job are separate questions. Two
sentences can look structurally similar and do completely different
work in the argument around them.
:::

::: example
*The new scheduling software cut missed shifts by 18% (supports the
claim). It did, however, require two weeks of staff retraining before
the improvement appeared (concedes a limitation).* Same paragraph, two
different jobs — the second sentence isn't weakening the argument, it's
making it more credible by acknowledging a real cost.
:::

## Your Turn

::: prompt words="2 sentences + labels" time="15 min"
Write two sentences about the same claim from your own work or studies:
the first supporting the claim with evidence, the second conceding a
genuine limitation or cost. Then label each sentence with its job:
"Supports the claim" / "Concedes a limitation."
:::

:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="concede" pos="verb" meaning="to admit a point, often a limitation, that weakens your own case slightly — স্বীকার করা" example="The author concedes that the method has a real cost before defending its overall value."
:::

::: vocabulary word="function" pos="noun" meaning="the job a sentence performs within an argument, separate from its literal content — কাজ/ভূমিকা" example="Two sentences can look similar but serve a completely different function in the paragraph."
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
Switching to the new inventory system reduced stock discrepancies by
22% within the first quarter (supports the claim, with evidence). The
transition did, however, require nearly a month of parallel manual
tracking while staff learned the new interface (concedes a limitation).
Labelling: sentence 1 — supports the claim; sentence 2 — concedes a
limitation, which actually strengthens the argument's credibility by
showing the author isn't hiding the real cost.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Does your first sentence genuinely support the claim with a specific detail, not just restate it?
- Does your second sentence concede a real limitation, not a fake or trivial one?
- Could a reader tell each sentence's job from its function alone, even without your labels?
:::
