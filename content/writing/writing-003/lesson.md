## Introduction

One correct sentence is a start. Several correct sentences that clearly
follow one another — that's where writing starts to feel like writing,
not like a list of separate facts. This lesson adds one small tool:
words that show order. They cost almost nothing to learn and they do a
lot of work.

## Time Expressions That Hold Sentences Together

::: concept
**First**, **then**, **after that**, **next**, and **finally** tell the
reader where each sentence sits in a sequence, without needing a new
grammar structure. "**First**, I wake up. **Then**, I check my phone.
**After that**, I make tea." Three separate simple sentences — now
reading as one connected sequence, because the time words do the
connecting.
:::

Keep the verb form the same throughout: if you're describing what you do
*every day*, stay in present simple the whole way through — don't drift
into past tense halfway because you started thinking about what you did
*today* specifically. Pick one framing and hold it.

::: example
First, I wake up at six. Then, I check the vessel arrival schedule for
the day. After that, I have breakfast with my family. Next, I leave for
the port office. Finally, I start the first shift briefing at eight.
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
First, I wake up at six o'clock. Then, I check the overnight messages on
my phone before I get out of bed. After that, I have a quick breakfast
with my family. Next, I get dressed and check the vessel schedule for
the day. Finally, I leave the house at seven-thirty to catch the bus to
the port office.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Did you use at least four different time expressions to connect your sentences?
- Did every sentence stay in the same tense (present simple, for a routine)?
- Does the sequence make sense if you read it out loud, start to finish?
:::
