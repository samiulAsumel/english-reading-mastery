## Introduction

[Lesson 59](/lessons/59/) taught you that an example is never
automatically evidence for every claim standing near it — one case can
illustrate a mechanism while remaining weak proof of how common or large an
effect really is. Writing with an example means doing that honesty
yourself: saying clearly what the case shows, and naming what it still
leaves open.

## The Rule, for Writing

::: concept
A weak use of an example jumps straight from one case to a sweeping claim:
"Company X automated its warehouse and cut costs 20%, so automation cuts
costs." A strong one introduces the example with a clear signal phrase,
states the specific claim it supports, and names a limitation — sample size,
representativeness, or a confound — that stops the example from proving
more than it actually does.
:::

::: example
For example, a mid-sized freight forwarder that introduced automated rate
comparison reduced quoting time from two days to four hours. This shows the
tool can meaningfully speed up quoting at that scale of operation. It does
not show that every freight forwarder would see the same result, since this
company was already using digital rate sheets before the change — a
starting condition smaller, paper-based operators wouldn't share.
:::

## Your Turn

::: prompt words="110-160 words" time="20 min"
Describe one real or plausible example you know (a company, a team, a tool,
a project) that supports a broader claim. Introduce it with a signal phrase
("for example," "consider," "one case is"), state the specific claim it
genuinely supports, and name one limitation — sample size, representativeness,
or a confound — that stops the example from proving the fuller, broader
claim on its own.
:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="illustrate" pos="verb" register="formal" meaning="to make an abstract claim concrete with one case, without proving it holds universally (দৃষ্টান্ত দিয়ে বোঝানো)" collocation="illustrate the point, clearly illustrate, serve to illustrate" example="The delayed shipment illustrates what poor coordination costs."
:::

::: vocabulary word="representative" pos="adjective" register="formal" meaning="genuinely reflecting the wider group a conclusion is being generalized to (প্রতিনিধিত্বমূলক)" collocation="representative sample, fully representative, not representative" example="One busy week is not representative of the whole year."
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
Consider a regional airline that introduced predictive maintenance
scheduling and reduced unscheduled repairs by 30% over a year. This shows
that predictive maintenance can meaningfully cut unscheduled repairs, at
least for an airline with the flight-frequency data needed to train the
model reliably. It does not show that every airline would see a similar
drop, since this carrier already had a decade of detailed maintenance
records to draw on — an advantage smaller or newer operators, with far
thinner historical data, would not share. The example supports the
mechanism working under the right conditions; it doesn't yet support a
claim about how common that success would be more broadly.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Did you introduce the example with a clear signal phrase?
- Did you state the specific claim the example genuinely supports?
- Did you name a real limitation — sample size, representativeness, or a confound — not a vague one?
- Did you avoid generalizing the example into a universal claim it can't support?
:::
