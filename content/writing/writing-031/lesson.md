## Introduction

[Lesson 31](/lessons/31/) taught you to find the unwritten idea an
argument secretly depends on, by asking "what must be true for this
conclusion to follow?" This task asks you to build an argument yourself,
and then do the harder thing: name your own hidden assumption in plain
writing, and test whether your argument survives if that assumption
turns out to be false.

## The Rule, for Writing

::: concept
Write your evidence and your conclusion first. Then, in a separate
sentence, name the assumption bridging them — the unstated idea that
must be true for your conclusion to actually follow from your evidence.
Finally, apply the negation test in writing: state, in one sentence,
what happens to your conclusion if that assumption turns out to be
false. A necessary assumption breaks the argument when negated; a merely
helpful one weakens it without destroying it.
:::

::: example
**Sales rose after the store added self-checkout, so self-checkout
increases sales.** **Hidden assumption:** nothing else that could explain
higher sales changed at the same time. **Negation test:** if the store
also extended its opening hours that same month, the conclusion no
longer clearly follows — the assumption was necessary, not just helpful.
:::

## Your Turn

::: prompt words="80-110 words" time="20 min"
Write a short two- or three-sentence argument of your own (about work, a
habit, or a decision) that moves from evidence to a conclusion. Then, in
a separate sentence, name the hidden assumption your argument depends
on. Finally, write one sentence applying the negation test — what
happens to your conclusion if that assumption turns out to be false.
:::

::: draft placeholder="[Evidence], so [conclusion]. / Hidden assumption: this depends on .../ If that assumption is false, then [the conclusion weakens/no longer follows] because ..."
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
The new berth-scheduling software was installed in March, and turnaround
times improved the same month, so the software must be why turnaround
times improved. This argument depends on a hidden assumption: that
nothing else affecting turnaround time changed in March. If that
assumption is false — for instance, if the port also reduced vessel
traffic that month because of a seasonal lull — the conclusion no longer
clearly follows, since the slower traffic alone could explain the
faster turnaround.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Did you state your hidden assumption as a separate, explicit sentence, not left implied?
- Does your negation test genuinely show what happens to the conclusion if the assumption is false?
- Is the assumption you named actually necessary for the conclusion, not just loosely related to it?
- Did you write this directly in English, without translating from Bangla first?
:::
