## Introduction

[Lesson 37](/lessons/37/) taught you to unpack a dense sentence back into
plain English by finding its main clause first. This task runs the
process in reverse: you start with plain sentences and compress them,
deliberately, using the same devices — an appositive, and negative
inversion — that made the reading lesson's sentences feel dense in the
first place.

## The Two Tools

::: concept
An **appositive** renames a noun in a comma-bound phrase right beside it:
*Dhaka, the capital of Bangladesh, is a major economic center.*
**Negative inversion** moves a negative adverb (*rarely, never, not
until*) to the front of a sentence and swaps the subject and auxiliary
verb: *the system rarely fails* becomes *rarely does the system fail.*
:::

::: example
**Two plain sentences:** *The manager approved the proposal. She joined
the company in 2015.* **Compressed with an appositive:** *The manager,
who joined the company in 2015, approved the proposal.*
:::

## Your Turn

::: prompt words="2 sentences" time="20 min"
Write two compressed sentences. Sentence 1: combine two plain sentences
about a person, place, or system into one sentence using an appositive.
Sentence 2: write a sentence using negative inversion with "rarely" or
"never," and also write its plain, uninverted version underneath it so
the meaning is verifiably unchanged.
:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="appositive" pos="noun" meaning="a noun phrase placed beside another to explain or identify it — ব্যাখ্যামূলক বিশেষ্যপদ" example="'Dhaka, the capital of Bangladesh,' identifies Dhaka through an appositive."
:::

::: vocabulary word="inversion" pos="noun" meaning="reversing normal subject-verb word order, usually for emphasis — শব্দক্রম বিপরীতকরণ" example="'Rarely does the system fail' uses inversion for emphasis."
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
**Appositive:** *The terminal manager, a former ship's engineer,
approved the new berthing schedule.* (from: *The terminal manager
approved the new berthing schedule. She was a former ship's engineer.*)
**Inversion:** *Rarely does a single technological upgrade resolve every
operational bottleneck a terminal faces.* Plain version: *A single
technological upgrade rarely resolves every operational bottleneck a
terminal faces.* Both sentences carry the exact same core meaning as
their two-sentence originals.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Does your appositive sentence use commas correctly around the inserted phrase?
- Does your inverted sentence use correct subject-auxiliary order after the fronted negative adverb?
- Does the plain version underneath prove the inverted sentence's meaning is unchanged?
- Is the compressed sentence still fully grammatical on its own, without needing the original two sentences to make sense?
:::
