## Introduction

[Lesson 43](/lessons/43/) taught you to read a paragraph by tracking what
each sentence takes as given and what it adds as new. Writing one is the
same skill run in reverse: you have to plan, sentence by sentence, what
information to hand forward so a reader never has to backtrack to find
out what "this," "it," or "the improvement" is pointing at.

## Given-to-New, for Writing

::: concept
To build a paragraph a reader can follow without backtracking, open each
new sentence with something the reader already knows (**given**), and
close it with the one new fact you're adding (**new**). Chaining several
sentences this way — new information in sentence one becoming the given
topic of sentence two, and so on — produces **linear progression**, the
easiest structure for a reader to follow at speed.
:::

::: example
**Linear chain:** *The port introduced an automated berth-scheduling
system. The system reduced the time vessels spent waiting outside the
harbor. That reduction in waiting time allowed the port to handle more
vessels per week.* Each sentence's new information — *automated
scheduling*, then *reduced waiting time* — becomes the next sentence's
opening subject.
:::

## Your Turn

::: prompt words="100-150 words" time="20 min"
Write one paragraph (100-150 words) about an operational or workplace
change, using linear progression: at least three sentences where each
one's new information becomes the next sentence's given subject. Include
one nominalization-based link (turning a previous sentence's verb into a
noun, as Lesson 41 and Lesson 43 both taught). End the paragraph with a
qualification — a word like "however" or "although" that limits how much
credit the change alone can take for the result.
:::

:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
*The terminal introduced a digital cargo-manifest system to replace
paper records. The system allowed clerks to verify container contents
in seconds rather than minutes. This faster verification shortened the
average time each truck spent waiting at the gate. The shortened wait,
in turn, let the terminal process a noticeably higher number of trucks
per shift. However, this improvement was not entirely attributable to
the manifest system alone, since the terminal had also added two extra
gate lanes during the same period.* Each sentence's new information —
*digital manifest system*, *faster verification*, *shortened wait* —
becomes the next sentence's opening subject, and the closing "however"
keeps the paragraph from overclaiming a single cause.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Does each sentence's new information genuinely become the next sentence's given subject, rather than just sitting nearby?
- Is at least one link built from a nominalization (a verb turned into a noun), not just a pronoun?
- Does the closing qualification limit the claim, rather than merely repeating it in different words?
- Could a reader follow the whole paragraph without ever backtracking to find out what a pronoun refers to?
:::
