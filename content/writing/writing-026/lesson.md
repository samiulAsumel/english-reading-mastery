## Introduction

[Lesson 26](/lessons/26/) taught you to find a claim, a reason, and
evidence inside someone else's paragraph — and to notice a counterargument
being answered by a rebuttal. This task flips the direction: you build
that same skeleton yourself, from nothing, and you get to decide how
much evidence a claim this size actually deserves.

## The Rule, for Writing

::: concept
Start with a claim you can actually defend in four or five sentences —
not "remote work is good" (too broad to support briefly) but something
narrow enough that one piece of evidence and one counterargument can
carry it. Then build outward: claim → reason (why should I accept this?)
→ evidence (what actually backs it up?) → counterargument (what would a
skeptic say?) → rebuttal (how do you answer them?).
:::

::: example
**Small companies should adopt cloud-based inventory systems.** (claim)
**Manual stock tracking causes frequent counting errors as a business
grows.** (reason) **A 2023 survey of small retailers found that
businesses using cloud inventory systems cut stock discrepancies by
nearly a third.** (evidence) **Some owners argue that the monthly
subscription cost isn't worth it for a small operation.** (counterargument)
**However, the cost of a single major stock error usually exceeds a
year of subscription fees.** (rebuttal)
:::

## Your Turn

::: prompt words="5-6 sentences" time="20 min"
Write a five- or six-sentence argument on a topic of your own choosing
(work, technology, a habit, a local issue — anything you can genuinely
defend). It must contain, in order: a claim, a reason, one piece of
evidence, a counterargument, and a rebuttal. Keep the claim narrow enough
that your one piece of evidence is actually enough to support it — don't
let "some employees" quietly become "all employees" by your last sentence.
:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="rebuttal" pos="noun" register="formal" meaning="a response that answers a counterargument without necessarily disproving it entirely (প্রতিউত্তর)" collocation="offer a rebuttal, strong rebuttal, rebuttal to" example="The letter offers a rebuttal of the claim that the delay was avoidable."
:::

::: vocabulary word="overgeneralize" pos="verb" register="formal" meaning="to stretch a narrow piece of evidence into a much broader claim than it can support (অতি-সাধারণীকরণ করা)" collocation="tend to overgeneralize, risk overgeneralizing, overgeneralize from" example="To say every supplier is late from one case is to overgeneralize."
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
Port operators should invest in real-time container tracking software.
Manual tracking often leaves staff unsure exactly where a container sits
in the yard, which slows down loading schedules. A 2024 review of three
mid-sized terminals found that real-time tracking cut average retrieval
time by nearly twenty percent. Some terminal managers argue that the
software is too expensive for smaller operations to justify. However,
the recurring labor cost of manual searches, repeated every single day,
usually outweighs the software's one-time setup cost within the first
year.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Is your evidence actually matched to the size of your claim — not a single anecdote propping up a universal statement?
- Did you include a real counterargument, not just a weak objection you immediately dismiss?
- Does your rebuttal answer the counterargument's actual concern, rather than changing the subject?
- Did you write this directly in English, without composing it in Bangla first?
:::
