## Introduction

[Lesson 57](/lessons/57/) taught you to read a classification for its
criterion — the one rule an author used to split a concept into categories —
and to catch it when two different rules get mixed together by mistake.
Writing a classification well means doing that discipline yourself: picking
one criterion, naming it, and building categories that genuinely follow from
it.

## The Rule, for Writing

::: concept
A weak classification lists types without a rule: "Systems are manual,
automated, or expensive." A strong one states a definition first, names its
single classification criterion, and divides into categories that actually
follow that one criterion — never mixing, say, a cost-based category into an
automation-based split.
:::

::: example
Port billing systems can be classified according to their level of
automation. Manual systems require staff to calculate and enter every
charge by hand. Semi-automated systems calculate standard charges
automatically but still require staff review for unusual cargo. Fully
automated systems calculate and issue charges without human intervention,
except when a rule-based exception is flagged for review.
:::

## Your Turn

::: prompt words="120-170 words" time="20 min"
Choose a concept you know well (a type of software, a role, a process, a
product category) and write a short classification of it. Open with a
definition using a signal phrase (is defined as, refers to, consists of).
State one explicit classification criterion, then divide the concept into
two or three categories that follow strictly from that single criterion,
with one concrete example for each category.
:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="criterion" pos="noun" meaning="the single rule or standard a classification is built on — মানদণ্ড" example="Before judging the categories, name the criterion the author used to build them."
:::

::: vocabulary word="mutually exclusive" pos="phrase" meaning="categories that don't overlap, so nothing belongs to more than one at once — পরস্পর বর্জনশীল" example="Keep the categories mutually exclusive — a system shouldn't fit two of them at once."
:::

::: vocabulary word="falls into" pos="phrasal verb" meaning="belongs to a particular category within a classification — এই শ্রেণিভুক্ত হয়" example="A container ship falls into the vessel category, not the cargo category."
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
Customer support tickets can be classified according to how they are
resolved. Self-resolved tickets are closed by the customer using a help
article or automated chatbot, without any staff involvement. Agent-resolved
tickets require a support agent to respond directly, usually because the
issue is account-specific or the automated options failed to answer it.
Escalated tickets are passed from a frontline agent to a specialist team,
typically because the issue involves a technical bug or a billing dispute
beyond the frontline agent's authority to resolve. This resolution-based
criterion is separate from ticket priority or channel, and mixing either of
those in would break the classification's single, consistent rule.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Did you open with a clear definition, signaled by a phrase like "is defined as" or "refers to"?
- Did you name one explicit classification criterion?
- Do all your categories follow that single criterion, without a second rule sneaking in?
- Did you give at least one concrete example per category?
:::
