## Introduction

[Lesson 28](/lessons/28/) taught you to read a paragraph's hedges and
reconstruct exactly how certain the author really is. This task reverses
the direction: you are given a piece of evidence, and you must choose
your own modal verbs and evidence verbs so that your written claim is
neither more nor less confident than what the evidence actually earns.

## The Rule, for Writing

::: concept
Match your verb to your evidence. If a single small study "suggests"
something, don't write that it "proves" or "demonstrates" it — save
those stronger verbs for well-replicated findings. Use modal verbs
(may, could, is likely to) and adverbs (possibly, largely, to some
extent) to mark exactly how far your evidence actually travels, and
attribute a reported finding to its source ("the study found," "the
report claims") instead of quietly adopting it as your own certainty.
:::

::: example
**A single survey of one shipping company found that switching to
digital manifests reduced paperwork errors.** Written carelessly: "Digital
manifests eliminate paperwork errors." Written with calibrated stance:
**"A survey of one shipping company suggests that digital manifests may
reduce paperwork errors, though this finding has not yet been tested
across the wider industry."** The second version keeps the claim honestly
tied to its one-company source.
:::

## Your Turn

::: prompt words="80-100 words" time="20 min"
Pick a finding you know something about (from work, a habit, or general
knowledge) that is based on limited evidence — one study, one example,
or one personal observation. Write a paragraph reporting it using at
least one modal verb (may, could, might), one hedging adverb (possibly,
largely, to some extent), and one attribution phrase (the report found,
according to). Your final sentence must stay exactly as qualified as
your first sentence — don't let the claim harden as the paragraph goes on.
:::

::: draft placeholder="A [report/study/observation] found that .../ This suggests that [X] may .../ However, this has only been shown in ..."
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
A recent internal review at one container terminal found that switching
berth-scheduling staff to a shared digital board reduced missed handoffs
during shift changes. This suggests that shared scheduling tools may
help other terminals avoid similar errors, though the review covered
only one site over a single quarter. According to the terminal's own
report, the effect could partly reflect staff simply paying closer
attention during a new system's first months, rather than the tool
itself. A larger, longer study would be needed before the finding could
be called reliable across the industry.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Does your modal verb or adverb match the actual size of your evidence, rather than overstating it?
- Did you attribute the finding to its source instead of presenting it as your own certainty?
- Is your last sentence exactly as qualified as your first, with no quiet upgrade in confidence?
- Did you write this directly in English, without translating from Bangla first?
:::
