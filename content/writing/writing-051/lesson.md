## Introduction

[Lesson 51](/lessons/51/) taught you to read a passage as a chain of ideas, not
just a list of statements — each claim building on, qualifying, or leading to
the next until the author reaches a conclusion. Writing one is harder than
reading one: you have to hold the whole chain in your head while you build
it, sentence by sentence, and make sure the last link still connects
honestly back to the first.

## The Rule, for Writing

::: concept
An argument chain in writing works the same way it works in reading, just in
reverse: instead of reconstructing links an author already built, you build
them yourself. Each sentence should follow visibly from the one before it —
not with vague transitions like "also" or "and," but with connectors that
show exactly *how* one idea leads to the next: because, which means, as a
result, this in turn.
:::

::: example
A container arrives at the port two days late because of a mechanical fault
on the vessel. That delay means the trucking company scheduled to collect it
has already reassigned its drivers elsewhere, which means the container now
waits an extra day in the yard. As a result, the importer misses the
delivery window promised to their own customer — a two-day mechanical fault
has become a broken promise three links down the chain.
:::

## Your Turn

::: prompt words="120-180 words" time="20 min"
Write one paragraph that builds a four-link argument chain in a realistic
technology, business, or logistics scenario of your own choosing — something
like "a software bug causes X, which causes Y, which causes Z." Use at least
three explicit connectors (because, which means, as a result, this in turn,
consequently) to make every link visible, and end with a conclusion that
genuinely follows from the chain rather than a new, unconnected point.
:::

::: draft placeholder="Start with the first cause / then its first consequence, joined by 'because' or 'which means' / keep building link by link"
:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="consequently" pos="adverb" register="formal" meaning="as a result of something just mentioned (ফলস্বরূপ)" collocation="consequently, consequently the, and consequently" example="The pilot was delayed; consequently, the launch moved to March."
:::

::: vocabulary word="in turn" pos="phrase" register="formal" meaning="as a direct result, continuing a chain of cause and effect (ফলে)" collocation="in turn, which in turn, and in turn" example="Delays raise costs, which in turn raise prices."
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
A warehouse management system crashes during a routine software update
because a compatibility check was skipped before deployment. That crash
means the staff can no longer scan incoming shipments, which means every
arriving truck has to wait in a manual queue instead of being processed
automatically. As a result, the yard fills faster than trucks can be
released, and within three hours the queue backs up onto the public road
outside the gate. A skipped compatibility check, four links later, has
become a traffic problem the warehouse manager never saw coming — exactly
the kind of chain a careful reader, and a careful writer, learns to trace
all the way through.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Does your paragraph have at least four genuine links, each following from the one before it?
- Did you use at least three explicit connectors, not vague ones like "and" or "also"?
- Does your final conclusion actually follow from the chain, rather than introducing a new unconnected idea?
- Could a reader trace your chain backward from the conclusion to the first cause without a gap?
:::
