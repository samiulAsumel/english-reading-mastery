## Introduction

[Lesson 61](/lessons/61/) taught you to read the precise boundary an
author draws between two familiar-sounding words — efficiency versus
effectiveness, automation versus autonomy — and to tell a necessary
condition apart from a sufficient one. This task asks you to build one
of those distinctions yourself, from scratch, rather than decode
someone else's.

## The Rule, for Writing

::: concept
To draw a real distinction, name both terms, then state precisely what
divides them — never just "they are different." Add one necessary
condition ("X requires Y, though Y alone doesn't guarantee X") and,
if you introduce a classification, state its single criterion openly.
:::

::: example
*Uptime is not the same as reliability. Uptime measures how much of the
time a system is running; reliability measures how consistently it
produces correct results while running. A stable connection is
necessary for accurate synchronisation, though a stable connection
alone does not guarantee it — the software must also be configured
correctly.*
:::

## Your Turn

::: prompt words="100-150 words" time="20 min"
Choose two familiar-sounding terms from your own field (e.g. "capacity"
vs. "throughput," "delay" vs. "downtime," "accuracy" vs. "precision").
Write one paragraph that: (1) states they are not the same, using "is
not the same as"; (2) explains the precise boundary between them; (3)
states one necessary condition using "necessary" or "requires"; and (4)
if natural, classifies something related into 2-3 categories with one
named criterion.
:::

::: draft placeholder="Term A is not the same as Term B. / Term A means... / Term B means... / X is necessary for..."
:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="necessary condition" pos="noun phrase" meaning="something required for an outcome, though it may not guarantee it on its own — আবশ্যক শর্ত" example="A verified account is a necessary condition for withdrawal, though it does not by itself guarantee approval."
:::

::: vocabulary word="criterion" pos="noun" meaning="the single standard used to judge or classify something — মাপকাঠি" example="The classification criterion here is response time, not cost."
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
Berth occupancy is not the same as port capacity. Occupancy measures
how many berths are physically in use at a given moment; capacity
measures the maximum volume a terminal could handle under ideal
conditions over time. A terminal can show high occupancy while still
operating far below its real capacity, if vessels are simply waiting
rather than being worked efficiently. A confirmed berth allocation is
necessary for a vessel to begin unloading, though allocation alone does
not guarantee unloading starts on schedule — crane availability and
customs clearance must also be in place. Vessels in this port are
classified as fast-turnaround, standard, or delayed, based strictly on
the ratio of actual berth time to scheduled berth time.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Did you use "is not the same as" to state the distinction directly, rather than a vague "these are different"?
- Did your necessary-condition sentence avoid claiming the condition alone guarantees the outcome?
- If you classified something, did you name one explicit criterion rather than mixing unrelated ones?
- Did you write directly in English, without composing the paragraph in Bangla first?
:::
