## Introduction

[Lesson 11](/lessons/11/) taught you to decode stacked clauses by finding
a sentence's one backbone first, then attaching each clause to the noun
it actually describes. Writing is the mirror image: instead of
untangling someone else's clauses, you're going to build your own —
taking short, separate facts and fusing them into one flowing paragraph,
with every relative clause hooked onto exactly the right noun.

## The Rule, for Writing

::: concept
Find the noun that two facts share. Turn the second fact into a clause
starting with **who** (people), **which** (things), **that** (people or
things, especially in defining clauses), or **whose** (possession) — and
drop it in immediately after that shared noun, not at the end of the
sentence. Do this across several sentences in a row, and a paragraph
stops sounding like a list of facts and starts sounding like one
connected description.
:::

::: example
Two facts: "The manager approved the shipment. The manager works in
logistics." Combined: "The manager **who works in logistics** approved
the shipment." Now link it to the next fact: "She flagged one container
**that arrived without the correct paperwork**." The clause sits right
after the noun it describes each time — never detached at the end.
:::

## Your Turn

::: prompt words="one paragraph (5-8 sentences)" time="15 min"
Describe a small team, office, or group of people you know — real or
invented. Write 5-8 connected sentences, and use a relative clause (who,
which, that, or whose) in at least four of them to add detail about a
person, place, or thing you've just mentioned. Keep the sentences
flowing as one paragraph, not a list of separate combined pairs.
:::

::: draft placeholder="Our team has five people. The manager, who..., The driver whose... / The email that arrived..."
:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="colleague" pos="noun" meaning="a person you work with — সহকর্মী" example="The colleague who joined last month already handles the busiest route."
:::

::: vocabulary word="flag" pos="verb" meaning="to mark something as needing attention (ত্রুটি চিহ্নিত করা)" example="She flagged the shipment that arrived without paperwork."
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
Our shipping office has five people, and each one handles a different
part of the process. The manager, who has worked in logistics for
twelve years, approves every shipment before it leaves the yard. Beside
her sits the driver whose truck broke down twice last month, which is
why we now schedule a spare vehicle every Monday. The clerk who checks
the paperwork flagged one container that arrived without the correct
customs form. Luckily, the colleague who joined the team last month
already knows the system that we use to track every delay. Together,
they form a small team that keeps a genuinely complicated process
running smoothly.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Did you place each relative clause right after the noun it describes, not at the end of the sentence?
- Did you choose "who" for people, "which" for things, and "whose" for possession correctly?
- Did the sentences read as one connected paragraph, not a list of separately combined pairs?
- Did you avoid repeating the noun inside its own clause (e.g. "the manager who the manager approved")?
:::
