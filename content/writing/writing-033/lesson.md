## Introduction

[Lesson 33](/lessons/33/) gave you eighteen named logical fallacies and a
framework for judging whether an argument's evidence is relevant,
sufficient, and reliable. This task asks you to write with that
discipline: build a short argument that deliberately steers around one
named fallacy, and then prove, in your own explanation, that you
actually avoided it.

## The Rule, for Writing

::: concept
Pick one fallacy from Lesson 33 (hasty generalization, post hoc
reasoning, false dilemma, straw man, and so on) that your topic makes it
tempting to commit. Write your argument so it avoids that exact trap —
for example, qualify a generalization with a sample size instead of
generalizing from one case, or acknowledge the sequence isn't proof of
cause instead of committing post hoc reasoning. Then add one sentence
naming the fallacy you avoided and explaining, specifically, how your
wording avoided it. Use "not established" for a claim that's simply
unproven, reserving "wrong" for a claim actually contradicted by evidence.
:::

::: example
**Tempting, fallacious version:** "My last shipment was delayed by the
new customs software, so the software clearly causes delays." **Version
that avoids post hoc reasoning:** "My last shipment was delayed after the
new customs software was introduced, but one case isn't established
proof — the delay could also be explained by the holiday backlog that
same week." **Explanation:** this avoids post hoc reasoning by explicitly
naming an alternative explanation instead of treating sequence as proof.
:::

## Your Turn

::: prompt words="80-110 words" time="20 min"
Choose one fallacy from Lesson 33 that your topic makes tempting (hasty
generalization, post hoc reasoning, false dilemma, or another you
remember). Write a short argument on a real or plausible topic that
deliberately avoids committing it — use "not established" rather than
"wrong" for anything merely unproven. Then add a final sentence naming
the fallacy and explaining specifically how your wording avoided it.
:::

:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="hasty generalization" pos="noun phrase" register="technical" meaning="drawing a broad conclusion from too small or narrow a sample (তড়িঘড়ি সাধারণীকরণ)" collocation="commit a hasty generalization, avoid a hasty generalization, example of hasty generalization" example="Judging a supplier from a single late delivery is a hasty generalization."
:::

::: vocabulary word="post hoc reasoning" pos="noun phrase" register="technical" meaning="assuming that because one event followed another, the first must have caused the second (পরবর্তী-অতএব-কারণ যুক্তি)" collocation="avoid post hoc reasoning, fall into post hoc reasoning, example of post hoc reasoning" example="Crediting the new policy because accidents fell afterwards is post hoc reasoning."
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
Two of the five warehouse staff who switched to the new inventory
scanner reported fewer counting errors this month. This is not
established proof that the scanner reduces errors across the whole
warehouse, since a sample of two employees over one month is far too
small, and both happened to be the most experienced staff on the floor.
A larger trial across more staff and more months would be needed before
drawing that conclusion. This avoids hasty generalization by keeping the
claim's scope tied honestly to its small sample, instead of extending it
to the whole team.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Did you pick one specific named fallacy and write around it deliberately, not just generally hedge?
- Does your explanation sentence show exactly how your wording avoided that fallacy, not just claim it did?
- Did you use "not established" for an unproven claim, rather than the stronger "wrong"?
- Did you write this directly in English, without translating from Bangla first?
:::
