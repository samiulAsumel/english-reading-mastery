## Introduction

[Lesson 87](/lessons/87/) — the last lesson in this course's reading track —
taught you to sort a paragraph's sentences into a hierarchy: a main idea,
genuine support, illustration, and mere detail. This task asks you to build
that hierarchy yourself, deliberately, sentence by sentence, rather than
just recognizing it when someone else has already written it.

## The Rule, for Writing

::: concept
A main-idea sentence states the paragraph's actual point, not merely its
topic. Each supporting sentence must then do real work for that point — a
reason, evidence, or explanation that would weaken the paragraph if removed
(apply Lesson 87's removal test to your own writing) — not simply mention
something related to the same subject.
:::

::: example
Topic: warehouse robots. Weak paragraph (support drifts into unrelated
detail): "Warehouse robots are increasingly common. They were first
developed decades ago. Many companies now use them." Strong paragraph (main
idea + real support): "Warehouse robots can significantly cut order-picking
time, but only where inventory stays organized. When shelving is
predictable, robots retrieve items directly, cutting walking time almost
entirely. When inventory is rearranged frequently, however, robots struggle
to relocate items, and the time savings shrink sharply." Removing either
supporting sentence here weakens the point; removing the weak paragraph's
second sentence changes nothing at all.
:::

## Your Turn

::: prompt words="120-150 words" time="20 min"
Pick a process, technology, or policy you know well. Write one
academic-register paragraph (120-150 words): a single sentence stating your
main idea, not just the topic, followed by two or three supporting
sentences that each genuinely develop that idea — a reason, evidence, or an
explanation — rather than sentences that merely relate to the same subject.
Before finishing, apply the removal test to each supporting sentence in
your head.
:::

::: draft placeholder="Main idea: .../ Support 1 (reason/evidence): .../ Support 2: .../ Support 3 (optional): ..."
:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="removal test" pos="noun phrase" meaning="checking a sentence's importance by mentally deleting it and seeing what breaks — the main idea, the argument, or nothing at all (অপসারণ পরীক্ষা)" example="She ran the removal test on her own third sentence and cut it, since nothing in the paragraph actually depended on it."
:::

::: vocabulary word="tangent" pos="noun" meaning="a sentence that relates loosely to the topic but does not actually support the main idea — মূল বিষয় থেকে বিচ্যুতি" example="The sentence about the technology's founding year was a tangent, not real support."
:::

::: vocabulary word="rhetorical weight" pos="noun phrase" meaning="how much a sentence actually matters to a paragraph's point, regardless of its length or position — বাচনিক গুরুত্ব" example="A short qualifying sentence carried more rhetorical weight than the paragraph's longest, most detailed one."
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
Automating berth scheduling can meaningfully reduce vessel turnaround time,
but only in ports with consistently high cargo volume. In three high-volume
ports, automated scheduling cut turnaround time by roughly 30 percent,
because the system had enough daily bookings to optimize berth assignments
efficiently. In two smaller ports, however, turnaround time barely
improved, since there were too few vessels scheduled each week for the
algorithm to find any meaningful efficiency gain. Staff at the smaller
ports also reported continuing to adjust assignments manually, which
further limited the system's effect. This pattern suggests the benefit
depends less on the technology itself than on whether a port generates
enough scheduling volume to make automated optimization worthwhile in the
first place.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Does your first sentence state an actual main idea, not just a topic?
- Does each supporting sentence pass the removal test — would the paragraph genuinely weaken without it?
- Did you avoid at least one tangent — a sentence that relates to the topic but does no real supporting work?
- Is the paragraph within 120-150 words and written in an academic register?
:::
