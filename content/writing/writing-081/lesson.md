## Introduction

[Lesson 81](/lessons/81/) taught you to read a book as a network of
chapters rather than a sequence of pages, and to compress its whole
argument into one qualified thesis sentence. Reading that compression and
writing it are different skills — reading lets you recognize a good
thesis when a critic states it for you; writing forces you to choose the
exact words yourself, under the same discipline: no vaguer than the book
actually is, no stronger than the evidence actually earns.

## The Rule, for Writing

::: concept
A qualified thesis sentence has three parts: the book's position, the
scope it applies to, and an honest signal of how strong the claim is
("suggests" is not "proves"). Leave any one part out and the sentence
either says too little to be useful or too much to be true.
:::

::: example
Weak: "This book is about port automation." (no position, just a topic.)
Qualified thesis: "Automating container terminals reduces turnaround
time in high-volume ports, but the book concedes the gains shrink
sharply below a certain cargo threshold." — a position, a scope, and an
honest limit, all in one sentence.
:::

## Your Turn

::: prompt words="60-90 words" time="20 min"
A short book has four chapters: (1) container terminals were the
slowest link in regional shipping, (2) three ports that automated berth
scheduling cut turnaround time by 30%, (3) two smaller ports that
automated the same process saw almost no gain, (4) the difference was
cargo volume, not the technology itself. Write one paragraph: state the
book's central question in one sentence, then compress its whole
argument into a single qualified thesis sentence that would satisfy a
reader who wants the honest, scoped version — not the marketing one.
:::

:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="qualify" pos="verb" meaning="to limit or add a condition to a claim so it isn't stated too broadly — সীমিত করা" example="The author qualifies the thesis by noting it only holds above a certain cargo volume."
:::

::: vocabulary word="scope" pos="noun" meaning="the range of situations a claim is actually meant to cover — পরিসর" example="The thesis's scope is high-volume ports, not every port."
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
Central question: does automating berth scheduling actually speed up
container terminals? Thesis: Automating berth scheduling meaningfully
reduces turnaround time in high-volume container ports, but the book's
own evidence shows the benefit depends on cargo volume and largely
disappears in smaller ports — so the technology is a genuine but
conditional fix, not a universal one.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Does your thesis state a clear position, not just a topic?
- Did you name the scope (which ports, under what condition) explicitly?
- Did you avoid overclaiming — no word stronger than the evidence given supports?
- Is the chapter relationship (chapters 2-3 as contrast, chapter 4 as explanation) implicit in how you wrote the thesis?
:::
