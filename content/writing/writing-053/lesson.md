## Introduction

[Lesson 53](/lessons/53/) taught you a reading strategy for technical
English: understand the sentence's structure first, infer meaning from how
the unfamiliar word relates to the rest of the sentence, and only look up
the vocabulary that genuinely still matters afterward. Writing technical
English well means doing the same work for your reader — building the
sentence so the meaning is inferable, not just correct.

## The Rule, for Writing

::: concept
When you introduce a technical term to a reader who may not know it, don't
stop and define it like a glossary entry ("Latency. Definition: ..."). Fold
the explanation into the sentence itself, using structure — an appositive, a
"which," a contrast — so the reader picks up the meaning without the flow
breaking.
:::

::: example
The vessel was held at anchorage — the designated waiting area outside the
port where ships wait for a berth to become free — for nearly six hours.
Notice the dash-set phrase does the defining work without ever stopping the
sentence to say "anchorage means...".
:::

## Your Turn

::: prompt words="80-130 words" time="20 min"
Write a short passage (2-4 sentences) from your own field that introduces
one piece of technical vocabulary and explains it *in context*, using an
appositive, a relative clause, or a contrast — not a stand-alone
definition sentence. A reader with no background in your field should be
able to infer the term's meaning without looking it up.
:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="appositive" pos="noun" meaning="a phrase set off by commas or dashes that renames or explains the noun before it — সমানাধিকরণ" example="Demurrage, a fee for keeping a container too long, adds up fast."
:::

::: vocabulary word="infer" pos="verb" meaning="to work out a meaning from context rather than being told directly — অনুমান করা" example="A well-written sentence lets the reader infer a new word's meaning."
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
The deployment failed because of a race condition — a bug that only appears
when two processes try to update the same piece of data at almost exactly
the same moment, and the result depends on which one finishes first. Because
the failure was intermittent, it took the team three days to reproduce it
reliably before they could even start fixing it.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Did you introduce exactly one technical term and explain it in context, not with a separate definition sentence?
- Could a reader outside your field infer the term's meaning from the sentence alone?
- Did the explanation avoid breaking the sentence's natural flow?
- Is the technical term itself used correctly, not just defined correctly?
:::
