## Introduction

A text can be entirely factual and still slanted. [Lesson
76](/lessons/76/) taught you to notice framing when reading. This task
makes you build it yourself — proof that you understand exactly which
words do the slanting work, because you chose them on purpose.

## Framing Without Lying

::: concept
Framing works through selection and word choice, not falsehood: which
numbers get mentioned, which get left out, whether a change is called
"a rise" or "a surge," whether a percentage is stated on its own or
next to a comparison. The same true number can sound alarming or
reassuring depending only on how it's dressed.
:::

::: example
Neutral: *The port's container throughput fell by 4% this quarter.*
Slanted: *The port's container throughput suffered a worrying decline
this quarter, continuing a troubling pattern.* Same fact, same number —
"suffered," "worrying," and "troubling pattern" (an added, unsupported
claim of a pattern) do all the slanting.
:::

## Your Turn

::: prompt words="2 sentences + explanation" time="15 min"
Pick one true, neutral fact from your own work, studies, or daily life
(a number, a change, an event). Write it once as a plain, neutral
sentence. Then write it again as a positively or negatively slanted
sentence, using only word choice and emphasis — add nothing false. Write
a third sentence naming exactly which words created the slant.
:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="slant" pos="verb" register="neutral" meaning="to present true information in a way that favors one impression over another (পক্ষপাতমূলকভাবে উপস্থাপন করা)" collocation="slant the facts, slant the story, slant towards" example="The article slants the story by quoting only management."
:::

::: vocabulary word="emphasis" pos="noun" register="formal" meaning="the relative weight or importance given to a particular part of a statement (গুরুত্ব)" collocation="place emphasis on, strong emphasis, shift the emphasis" example="The report puts more emphasis on cost than on safety."
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
Neutral: *Customer wait times at the help desk averaged six minutes
last month.* Slanted: *Customers were left waiting an average of six
frustrating minutes at the help desk last month, a clear sign of a
service failing to keep up.* "Frustrating," "left waiting," and "a
clear sign of a service failing to keep up" (an unsupported causal
claim) turn a plain number into an implied crisis, without adding a
single false fact.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Are both sentences built entirely from true information, with nothing false added?
- Did your explanation name the specific words doing the slanting, not just say "it sounds more negative"?
- Would a careful reader be able to strip the slanted words back out and recover the same plain fact?
:::
