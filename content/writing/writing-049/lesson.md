## Introduction

[Lesson 49](/lessons/49/) taught you to reconstruct an author's overall
argument across a whole book — the thesis, and the points built to
support it — before evaluating any of it. This task has you build that
same structure yourself, in miniature: one thesis, a small set of
distinct reasons behind it.

## The Rule, for Writing

::: concept
A thesis is one sentence stating your overall position — not a topic,
not a question, a position someone could disagree with. The supporting
points that follow should each give a genuinely different reason for
believing it, the way a book's chapters each add a different kind of
support to its overall argument rather than repeating the same one.
:::

::: example
Thesis: "The company should move its customer support team back
in-house." Support 1: response times have gotten worse since it was
outsourced. Support 2: customers increasingly complain about not being
understood. Support 3: the cost savings from outsourcing were smaller
than projected. Three different kinds of reasons — speed, quality, cost —
not the same point said three ways.
:::

## Your Turn

::: prompt words="100-150 words" time="15 min"
Write a short paragraph (100-150 words). Start with one sentence stating
a clear position on something you actually believe — about your work,
your studies, or something in your daily life. Follow it with 2-3
sentences, each giving a distinct supporting reason.
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
Our team should switch to a four-day work week. Productivity data from
similar companies shows output per employee did not drop after the
change, which removes the main financial worry. Employee retention has
also improved wherever it was tried, saving on hiring and training costs
over time. Finally, our own recent survey found that scheduling
flexibility was the single most requested change among staff, which
means the shift addresses a real, stated need rather than a guess about
what people want.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Is your first sentence a genuine position someone could reasonably disagree with, not just a topic?
- Does each supporting point give a different kind of reason, not the same reason restated?
- Would a reader who only read your first sentence know exactly what you're arguing for?
:::
