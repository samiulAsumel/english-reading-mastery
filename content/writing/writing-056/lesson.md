## Introduction

[Lesson 56](/lessons/56/) taught you to push a single number through three
levels — observation, interpretation, causal claim — and to stop before the
top level unless the evidence genuinely earns it. Writing a quantitative
argument means building that same ladder yourself: stating what changed,
what else might explain it, and exactly how far the number is entitled to
take your conclusion.

## The Rule, for Writing

::: concept
A weak quantitative argument jumps straight from a number to a cause: "Costs
fell 18% after we automated invoicing, so automation cut costs." A strong
one separates the observation from the interpretation, names at least one
confounding factor the data doesn't rule out, and ends with a conclusion
that is honest about what the number does and doesn't prove.
:::

::: example
Average berth turnaround time fell from 9.5 hours to 7.2 hours in the
quarter after the terminal introduced automated crane scheduling — a 24%
reduction. That's the observation. The terminal also increased night-shift
staffing over the same period, so the drop can't be credited to the
scheduling system alone; the honest conclusion is that turnaround time
improved after the change, not that the change caused all of it.
:::

## Your Turn

::: prompt words="110-160 words" time="20 min"
Take a number from work, study, or a field you know (a cost, a delay, a
conversion rate, an error count) that changed after something happened.
State the observation as a plain fact, name one thing besides the change you
are crediting that could also explain the result, and write a conclusion
that only claims what the data actually supports — resist the urge to
declare a clean cause.
:::

## Vocabulary in Context

::: vocabulary word="confounding factor" pos="noun phrase" register="technical" meaning="something else that changed at the same time as the thing you're crediting, making the true cause hard to isolate (বিভ্রান্তিকর উপাদান)" collocation="control for a confounding factor, possible confounding factor, identify a confounding factor" example="Weather is a confounding factor that affects both delays and productivity."
:::

::: vocabulary word="attributable to" pos="phrase" register="formal" meaning="able to be credited as the cause of a result (যা কারণ হিসেবে ধরা যায়)" collocation="be attributable to, largely attributable, directly attributable to" example="Most of the increase is attributable to higher fuel prices."
:::

::: vocabulary word="associated with" pos="phrase" register="formal" meaning="showing a relationship with something, without claiming it is the cause (সম্পর্কযুক্ত)" collocation="be associated with, strongly associated with, associated with delays" example="Longer dwell times are associated with incomplete paperwork."
:::

## Model Answer

::: model-answer
Customer complaint volume fell from 340 to 210 per month in the three months
after the support team introduced a new ticket-routing system — a 38%
reduction. Over the same period, the company also hired two additional
support agents, so the drop cannot be credited to the routing system alone.
What the data supports is a genuine, sizeable improvement in complaint
volume following both changes together; it does not support the stronger
claim that the routing system by itself caused a 38% reduction, since
staffing increased at the same time and no comparison team was tracked
without the new system.
:::

## Self-Check

::: rubric
- Did you state the observation as a plain, separate fact before interpreting it?
- Did you name a genuine confounding factor, not just gesture at "other things"?
- Does your conclusion stop at what the data actually supports, without overclaiming causation?
- Did you avoid writing "after X, therefore because of X" as if the two were the same thing?
:::
